## The course in one paragraph

The day moves from occlusion to pain. The morning sets out the vocabulary of occlusion (CR, MIP, guidance) and Okeson's idea of orthopedic stability. It then tests that idea against the evidence: occlusion is a weak and mostly non-causal factor in TMD. The midday lecture recasts TMD as a pain condition, with the IASP, ICOP and ICD-11 definitions, epidemiology (Saudi data included) and the biopsychosocial model. The afternoon is practical. Screen with 3Q/TMD, examine with DC/TMD, manage by the 2023 BMJ guideline, then decide when orthodontics can start, using the Jbara and Baghdadi score. One thread runs through the day: diagnosis before treatment, reversible care first, and orthodontics never offered as a TMD cure. A quiz opens and closes the day.

## The four readings at a glance

| Reading | Type | One-line bottom line | Answer to its "Think about" |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Okeson 2015**, AJODO 147(5 Suppl):S216–23 | Narrative review of the past, present and future of occlusion and TMD in orthodontics (abstract only read) | Alignment, a stable intercuspal position and aesthetics are not enough; orthopedic stability should be a routine goal, to reduce TMD risk factors (abstract) | **Orthopedic stability**: the teeth reach maximum intercuspation while the condyles sit in the musculoskeletally stable position (Okeson 2018). **When occlusion may matter**: a large joint-to-tooth discrepancy (over 3–4 mm; the usual 1–2 mm is not correlated with TMD) (Okeson 2018); skeletal anterior open bite, overjet over 6–7 mm, slide over 4 mm, unilateral lingual crossbite, 5 or more missing posterior teeth (McNamara 1995, cited by Okeson). Occlusion is only one of five factors, with trauma, emotional stress, deep pain input and parafunction, so stability lowers one risk and guarantees nothing (Okeson 2018) |
| **Michelotti 2010**, J Oral Rehabil 37:411–29 | Narrative critical review (abstract only read) | TMD is multifactorial and common at orthodontic ages (more prevalent between 15 and 45 years); claims that orthodontics causes or cures it need good evidence (abstract) | **Cause, prevent or cure?** None shown. Macfarlane 2009: TMD 3.2 % at 11–12 years, 17.6 % at 19–20 years, no link with orthodontics. Egermark 2003: treated and untreated alike at 20 years. Kim 2002: 31 studies, no rise with treatment. The author states in 2025 that no orthodontic approach is proven to cause, cure or prevent TMD. **Signs during treatment**: make a differential diagnosis, pause active treatment, give conservative care (counselling, behavioural therapy, home exercises, physiotherapy, medication, a splint if needed), resume or modify once pain-free (Michelotti 2018) |
| **Busse 2023**, BMJ 383:e076227 | GRADE clinical practice guideline (BMJ Rapid Recommendation); 153 RCTs, 8713 patients with pain ≥3 months | Start with education, exercise, manual therapy and CBT; avoid irreversible treatment | **Strong for**: CBT (with or without biofeedback or relaxation), therapist-assisted mobilisation, manual trigger point therapy, supervised postural exercise, supervised jaw exercise and stretching, usual care. **Reversible occlusal splints**: conditional against; low certainty; −1.81 cm on a 10 cm VAS against placebo, close to CBT's −1.88 cm (Yao 2023, Fig 3). **Irreversible oral splints**: strong against, with discectomy and NSAID + opioid. No orthodontic intervention was among the 59 assessed |
| **Jbara 2026**, Adolescents 6(1):3 | Expert-informed framework; no patients, no Delphi, narrative literature search | A DC/TMD-based questionnaire and score, under 10 min, to sort patients before orthodontics | **Tiers**: 0–3 low, proceed; 4–6 moderate, proceed with review every 6 months and referral to orofacial pain; ≥7 high, manage the TMD first. Separate modules for MAD trismus, DJD (0–5) and arthralgia (0–2). Bruxism and psychosocial stress are modifiers, not points. **Limits**: no sensitivity, specificity, reliability or pilot data; most item weights not printed; how modules feed the total is not stated; DJD bands overlap (0–2, 2–3, 3–5); its <38 mm opening flag differs from the DC/TMD <40 mm assisted opening |

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Centric relation (CR) | Jaw-to-jaw relation, independent of tooth contact, with the condyles anterior-superior against the eminences; a repeatable reference position | GPT (Glossary of Prosthodontic Terms); exact wording UNVERIFIED |
| Maximum intercuspal position (MIP, ICP) | Full intercuspation of the teeth, whatever the condyle position | GPT; wording UNVERIFIED |
| Centric occlusion (CO) | The tooth contact made with the mandible in CR; may or may not be MIP. Often used loosely for MIP | GPT 6th edition, via a secondary source |
| RCP–ICP slide | Shift from first contact in CR into MIP. 1–2 mm is usual and not tied to TMD; over 3–4 mm correlates with symptoms. Study cut-offs: >2 mm (Pullinger 1993), >4 mm (McNamara 1995) | Okeson 2018; PubMed abstracts |
| Musculoskeletally stable (MS) position | Condyles most superior and anterior in the fossae, against the posterior slopes of the eminences, discs interposed; set by the elevator muscles | Okeson 2018 |
| Orthopedic stability | MIP reached while the condyles are in the MS position | Okeson 2018 |
| Mutually protected occlusion | Posterior teeth take the load in MIP; anterior teeth disocclude the posterior teeth in eccentric movements | Textbook; UNVERIFIED |
| Canine guidance / group function | Laterotrusion guided by the working-side canine alone / shared by several working-side teeth | Textbook; UNVERIFIED |
| Working / non-working interference | Posterior contact on the side the mandible moves toward / on the opposite side | Textbook; UNVERIFIED |
| Pain (IASP 2020) | An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or potential tissue damage, or resembling one. "Resembling" replaced the 1979 clause "described in terms of such damage", which implied a verbal report. Six notes were added, e.g. pain and nociception differ; inability to communicate does not rule out pain | Raja 2020 (paraphrased) |
| Chronic pain (ICD-11) | Pain that persists or recurs for over 3 months. **Primary**: pain as a disease in itself (e.g. fibromyalgia). **Secondary**: pain from an underlying disease; 6 groups, one being chronic secondary headache or orofacial pain | Treede 2019 |
| ICOP 2020 | First edition; the preface says no comprehensive, internationally accepted orofacial pain classification existed before it. 7 sections: dentoalveolar, myofascial, TMJ, cranial nerve, pains resembling primary headaches, idiopathic, psychosocial assessment. Myofascial and TMJ pain each split into primary and secondary; painful TMD criteria adopted from DC/TMD | ICOP, Cephalalgia 2020 |
| DC/TMD | Dual-axis criteria. Axis I: physical diagnoses (12 common TMDs). Axis II: pain intensity, disability, distress, jaw function, parafunction | Schiffman 2014 |
| Familiar pain | Provoked pain that is like the pain the patient has been having; needed for every pain-related DC/TMD diagnosis ("familiar headache" for headache attributed to TMD) | Schiffman 2014 |
| Biopsychosocial model | Pain is expressed and amplified by psychological and behavioural factors beyond the tissue; basis of Axis II | Schiffman & Ohrbach 2016 |
| Chronic TMD pain (BMJ) | TMD pain lasting ≥3 months | Busse 2023 |
| Predisposing / initiating / perpetuating | Raises risk / starts the disorder / keeps it going (e.g. loss of posterior teeth / trauma / parafunction) | Trauma as initiating and oral behaviours such as clenching as perpetuating: Srivastava 2021, Background. Loss of posterior teeth as predisposing: Exam bank answer, UNVERIFIED |

## Where the readings agree and where they pull apart

**Common ground.**
- TMD is multifactorial and occlusion is at most one factor. Okeson names five factors and says orthodontics changes only occlusion, so its effect on TMD is minimal (Okeson 2018).
- Screen before orthodontics and record a baseline (Okeson 2018; Michelotti 2018; Jbara 2026).
- Painful TMD is managed before active orthodontics (Okeson 2018; Michelotti 2025; Jbara high tier).
- Reversible care comes first. Irreversible change to joint or occlusion is avoided as TMD treatment (Busse 2023: irreversible splints and discectomy strong against).

| Issue | Okeson 2015 | Michelotti 2010 | Busse 2023 | Jbara 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Role of occlusion | Orthopedic stability as a routine goal, to lower one risk factor | Weak associations; healthy people adapted to an experimental interference, people with a TMD history less so (Michelotti 2005; Le Bell 2002) | Not assessed | Malocclusion cited as risk context (43 % TMD in malocclusion, Huang 2025) |
| Splints | Stabilisation appliance to settle symptoms and to test whether occlusion matters (Okeson 2018) | A splint if needed, inside conservative care (Michelotti 2018) | Reversible splint: conditional against, low certainty | Not addressed in the score |
| After a good splint response | Build the final occlusion to the stable joint position (Okeson 2018) | No orthodontic approach proven to cure TMD (Michelotti 2025) | Irreversible occlusal change (irreversible splints): strong against | Not covered |
| Evidence base | Expert view plus older cohorts | Cohorts, a meta-analysis, experimental trials | 153 RCTs, GRADE | Expert opinion only |

**The main split.** Okeson's orthopedic-stability view sits against the evidence-first view of Michelotti and the BMJ panel. Okeson would build a stable condyle–tooth relation into every case. No study shows that doing so prevents TMD. Orthodontics in adolescence neither raises nor lowers TMD risk (McNamara 1995). Condyles are centred in the fossa in only about 50 % of people on MRI (Michelotti 2025). Okeson himself says the effect is minimal and cannot be promised (Okeson 2018).

**Splints.** One way to reconcile the readings: a reversible splint is an acceptable adjunct, not the main treatment. Busse's "conditional against" covers chronic pain only (≥3 months) and rests on low-certainty evidence, while the splint's point estimate sits close to CBT's (Yao 2023). Okeson's further step, reshaping the occlusion to match the splint, is irreversible. The guideline's direction (strong against irreversible splints) and a Cochrane review finding insufficient evidence for occlusal interventions (57 RCTs, cited in Michelotti 2025) both argue against it as TMD treatment.

**Orthodontics as TMD treatment.** None of Busse's 59 interventions was orthodontic, and the linked review lists orthodontics among irreversible TMD treatments (Yao 2023). So orthodontics rests on its own indications.

**Screening.** Jbara's score has no accuracy data. DC/TMD tools do: TMD Pain Screener sensitivity and specificity ≥0.95; myalgia 0.90 and 0.99 (Schiffman 2014). The score frames decisions; it does not diagnose.

**Numbers that differ because definitions differ.** Chronic TMD pain 6–9 % of adults (Busse 2023). Any TMD 5–12 % (Schiffman 2014). 10–15 % (Jbara, citing Michelotti 2010). Saudi samples run higher: 27.2 % of Jeddah adolescents with any RDC/TMD diagnosis (Al-Khotani 2016); 36.99 % of dental students with any DC/TMD diagnosis, a self-selected sample (Srivastava 2021); 35 % of Jeddah adults with the TMD Pain Screener only (Nadershah 2019, as cited in Srivastava 2021; original not checked). Slide cut-offs also vary: >2 mm, >3–4 mm, >4 mm.

## One-line verdict per reading

- **Okeson 2015**: A clear case for orthopedic stability as a treatment goal; its benefit for TMD is plausible but unproven, and Okeson himself rates orthodontics' effect on TMD as minimal.
- **Michelotti 2010**: The evidence anchor of the course: orthodontics neither causes, prevents nor cures TMD; worth knowing for the pause-and-manage protocol during treatment.
- **Busse 2023**: The strongest evidence of the four (153 RCTs, GRADE), though adult-only, with pooled subtypes and a median follow-up of 12 weeks, so transfer to adolescents is uncertain.
- **Jbara 2026**: A useful checklist for structuring pre-orthodontic decisions; unvalidated and with arithmetic gaps, so it supports a DC/TMD exam rather than replacing it.
