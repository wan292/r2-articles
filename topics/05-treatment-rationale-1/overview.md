# Topic 5 — Treatment Rationale (1)

Five papers, studied as one topic for the promotion exam even though papers 1 and 5 are the ones presented. Page numbers are journal pages. Facts about other papers or Proffit are marked **VERIFIED** (seen in a source this run: PubMed or Crossref abstract, the primary PDF, the printed 7th-edition Proffit PDF, or the resident's Proffit study pages) or **UNVERIFIED**. "Derived" means computed here from printed numbers.

## The question this topic answers

Treatment rationale is the reasoning behind the large early decisions, most of them made between 8 and 15 years of age, that a patient then lives with for decades. The five papers take one decision each:

- how to uncover an impacted maxillary canine, and when to intercept it (Kokich 2004);
- whether to keep, close or replace the space of a congenitally missing lower second premolar, and when (Kokich & Kokich 2006);
- whether to extract serially in the mixed dentition or wait (Yezdani 2020);
- whether four-premolar extraction changes the vertical dimension of the face (Kouvelis 2018);
- whether extraction changes how the profile ages after treatment (Stephens 2005).

Read together, they test how much of each rule is evidence and how much is expert habit. Three of the five are expert opinion with no data (Oxford level 5). The two with data agree that the extraction label does less to the face than tradition claims. Stephens shows that incisor position and growth matter more; Kouvelis names factors other than tooth number, such as neuromuscular balance and function, as probably more important (p 184).

**What the topic does not contain:** a randomised trial. The only data inside the topic are Stephens' Tables I-VII (cephalometric and perception) and Kouvelis' Tables I-III; the extraction-versus-nonextraction comparisons are Stephens Table III and Kouvelis Table III. The trials and reviews that now test the expert rules came later and from outside the topic: canine interception (Naoumova 2015, Bazargani 2014), open vs closed exposure (Cochrane 2017, Björksved 2021) and hemisection (Abdul Jabbar 2025).

## What they agree on

- **Growth is the clock for every decision.**
  - Kokich 2004: primary canine extraction from 8-9 y for a labial or intra-alveolar canine (citing Williams, p 278); palatal exposure sometimes in the late mixed dentition (p 283).
  - Kokich 2006: keep the E until vertical growth ends; extract an ankylosed E while growth remains; place the implant only when serial cephs show growth has stopped (p 441, p 443). Patient 5's implant waited until 17 y (p 440).
  - Yezdani: the first extraction comes at 8 to 8.5 y (pp 6438-6439).
  - Stephens: between about 15 and 30 y, pronasale moved forward 6.7 vs 3.1 mm and soft-tissue pogonion 7.9 vs 4.0 mm, males vs females (Table V).
  - Boys finish later in both Kokich 2006 (about 21 vs 17 y, as cited) and Proffit Ch 4 (early 20s in boys; VERIFIED, study notes). The direction agrees; the cut-off ages are UNVERIFIED.
- **Bone goes where teeth go.**
  - Kokich 2006: an ankylosed E submerges because its neighbours erupt and carry bone (p 442); in Patient 2, eruption after the extraction brought bone and tissue back up and removed the defect (pp 437-438). Teeth that drift together and are later pushed apart build the ridge, which the paper calls orthodontic implant-site development (p 443). Patient 5's first premolar, moved distally, regenerated a narrowed ridge (Fig 5).
  - Kokich 2004: enamel has no cells to resorb bone, so bone is removed down to the CEJ and the tooth is erupted into the centre of the ridge (pp 279, 282-283).
  - Yezdani: physiologic tooth movement is one of the two principles of serial extraction (p 6433). Proffit Ch 3 gives the same mechanism for ankylosis (VERIFIED, study notes).
- **Incisor position, not the extraction, sets the profile.**
  - Stephens matched the groups on U1-SN, L1-NB and the H-line at debond, and they did not diverge over 15 y (Table III).
  - Kokich 2006 Patient 4: extracting the remaining upper second premolar and the Es and closing all the spaces conventionally would have over-retracted the lips, so the molars were protracted instead. The lower incisors did not move lingually (p 439, Fig 4F). Hence the paper's rule: closure suits opposing crowding or a protrusive profile; with no crowding and a normal profile, add anchorage (p 443).
  - Yezdani: no extraction in Class I alveodental retrusion, because extraction gives a dished-in face (p 6436).
  - Proffit Ch 7: the lips follow about two-thirds of incisor retraction, and comparable extraction and nonextraction cases differ by about 2 mm of lip fullness (VERIFIED, study notes).
- **The face is less sensitive to the extraction label than folklore says.**
  - Kouvelis: 10 of 14 studies found no vertical difference. The four significant between-group differences were 1.7° and 2.3° (angular) and 1.4 and 4.0 mm (linear) (derived from Table III). The authors call the first three clinically questionable (Table III) and discount the 4.0 mm (Kumari) for serious risk of bias and no matching FMA change (p 185).
  - Stephens: LS-E −1.5 vs −1.6 mm over 15 y (P .82).
  - Both papers cite Paquette 1992 and Luppanapornlarp 1993: Kouvelis includes them as data, and Stephens uses Paquette for context and Luppanapornlarp's discriminant analysis to choose its matching variables (p 451).
- **The cheapest tool in the topic is extracting a primary tooth at the right time.** The primary canine for a palatal canine (Kokich 2004). The E for an ankylosed tooth or to let the 6 drift (Kokich 2006). The C and D as the first steps of serial extraction (Yezdani). Proffit adds that early primary canine extraction also lowers the risk of canine impaction (VERIFIED, printed 7th ed p 446), so Dewel's first step doubles as canine interception.

## Where they disagree or pull against each other

- **Hold the space or let it close?**
  - Kokich 2006 places no space maintainer after extracting the E when an implant is planned (p 443).
  - Proffit Ch 12 holds leeway space with a lower lingual arch in borderline crowding (VERIFIED, study notes). Bazargani 2014 found arch perimeter lost on the side where the primary canine was extracted (VERIFIED).
  - For an ankylosed E with no successor, Proffit Ch 12 gives Kokich's own plan: extract early and place no space maintainer, so the 6 drifts forward and brings bone (VERIFIED, study notes).
  - They reconcile by goal. If arch length is needed for alignment, hold it. If the goal is a ridge for an implant, let the space close and reopen it. A maintainer does not stop ridge resorption (Proffit Ch 11, 7th-edition box; VERIFIED, study notes).
- **Moving a tooth through a narrow ridge.**
  - Kokich 2004 warns that labial traction causes dehiscence and recession (p 279).
  - Kokich 2006 pushes a wider premolar root through a narrow ridge without losing support (p 443, refs 12-13).
  - The movements differ: buccal, out of the alveolus, against mesiodistal translation within it. Proffit Ch 19 [7th Ch 20]: possible, but slow, about 3 y (VERIFIED, study notes). Hom & Turley 1984: crestal loss about 1.3 mm in most, ridge width +1.2 mm (VERIFIED).
- **Does extraction flatten the face?**
  - Yezdani's Fig 4 legend lists a flat face, prominent chin and aged look (p 6440).
  - Stephens found no extra flattening after debond in matched patients (Table III). Wilson 1999 found no soft-tissue profile difference between serial extraction alone, serial extraction with treatment, and late extraction (VERIFIED).
  - The reconciliation: any flattening is made during treatment, through incisor retraction (about 2 mm of lip, Proffit Ch 7). It is not a later consequence of the label. Stephens' design, which matched at debond, cannot measure that treatment effect.
- **Is serial extraction "more stable" and cheaper?**
  - Yezdani asserts shorter fixed treatment, lower cost, more stable results and less retention (p 6435) without data.
  - Little 1990: 73 % unsatisfactory at ≥ 10 y (VERIFIED). O'Shaughnessy 2011: shorter active phase, but longer total time and more visits (VERIFIED). Yezdani's own disadvantage list gives 2-3 y of visits (p 6435).
  - Proffit: an adjunct to comprehensive treatment, not a substitute (VERIFIED, printed 7th ed pp 443, 445).
- **Can extraction help the vertical?**
  - Proffit Ch 7 keeps a severe vertical discrepancy as a rare reason to extract even with under 4 mm crowding (VERIFIED, printed 7th ed p 221).
  - Kouvelis says extracting to reduce or control the vertical is not evidence-based (abstract, p 175) and not justified (p 185). But it measured only skeletal angles and heights. Overbite and molar position were not extracted (Table III).
  - Yezdani lists deep and open bites as contraindications and bite deepening as a disadvantage (p 6435). These are dental vertical effects, which Kouvelis did not measure.
  - Narrow-indication studies outside the review conflict: Aras 2002 (posterior extractions closed posteriorly extended open bites) and Garlington & Logan 1990 (VERIFIED).
- **How certain is interception?**
  - Kokich 2004: "high predictability" if the crown has not passed the mesial surface of the lateral incisor root (pp 281-282).
  - Proffit's account of Ericson & Kurol uses the lateral-root midline: 91 % normalised with less than half the root overlapped, 64 % with more (Proffit Ch 11; VERIFIED, study notes; the 1988 original was not read, so VERIFIED-secondary only). The RCTs give 69 % vs 39 % and 67 % vs 42 % (VERIFIED).
  - Yezdani times its sequences by age (8 y, 8.5 y; p 6438). Proffit times the D by root formation: extract it when the first premolar root is ½-⅔ formed (VERIFIED, printed 7th ed p 444).
- **Ankylosed E with no successor: extract or decoronate?**
  - Kokich 2006: extract while the face is growing (p 443); decoronation is never mentioned.
  - Proffit Ch 12 gives both options (VERIFIED, study notes). The two past-exam keys for the same stem disagree (see below).
- **Hemisection.**
  - Kokich 2006 calls it especially attractive if the child is seen early (p 444).
  - Abdul Jabbar 2025, a split-mouth RCT with n = 40: residual space 2.04 ± 1.67 vs 2.39 ± 1.86 mm (P = .053), more complications (P = .0176); the authors advise against it (VERIFIED).

## How this topic connects to the rest of the hub

- **Superimposition topic.**
  - Kokich 2006 times the implant on superimposed serial cephs (p 441) without naming the method. Nielsen 1989 found that a palatal best fit under-reads vertical dental change by about 30 % at the molars and 50 % at the incisors over 4 y, so a best-fit series errs toward calling growth finished too early (inference).
  - Fig 4F's statement that the lower incisors did not move lingually needs a mandibular regional superimposition. Nguyen 2018 validates the chin (0.37 ± 0.16 mm) and inner symphysis (0.40 ± 0.14 mm) as stable.
  - Kouvelis' outcomes are mandibular-plane angles to SN, FH, the palatal plane or a horizontal plane, plus linear facial heights, height ratios and the vertical position of B point and pogonion (Table III); all are total change. Proffit Ch 4 says internal rotation is masked by surface remodelling (VERIFIED, printed pp 92-93), so these measures cannot isolate a wedge rotation.
- **Khaleel 2026.** Within each mechanics arm the Mn-Mx angle rose +0.69° to +1.84° (no untreated control), the same small order as Kouvelis' largest between-group differences (1.7°, 2.3°). Neither isolates the cause: Kouvelis names neuromuscular balance and function as probably more important than tooth number (p 184), and its one study with deliberately extrusive mechanics in the nonextraction arm (Gkantidis 2011) found no difference (Table III).
- **Inside the topic.**
  - Stephens and Kouvelis share Paquette 1992 and Luppanapornlarp 1993.
  - Kokich 2004 and Yezdani share the primary-canine extraction.
  - Kokich 2006 and Stephens share the idea that the profile is protected by not retracting incisors, and that males keep growing longer.

## Exam radar for the topic

1. **Kokich 2004, labial canine.** Four criteria: labiolingual position, height against the MGJ, amount of gingiva, mesiodistal position. Excision only if ≥ 2-3 mm of attached gingiva will remain. APF for short gingiva or a crown mesial over the lateral root. Closed eruption for a high or mid-alveolar crown.
2. **Kokich 2004, palatal canine.** Two-thirds of impacted canines are palatal. Extract the primary canine early (Kokich: crown not past the mesial surface of the lateral root; Proffit's account of Ericson & Kurol uses the lateral-root midline: 91 % vs 64 %). Early open exposure removes bone to the CEJ, and the canine erupts on its own in about 6-8 months. SLOB localises the canine.
3. **Kokich 2006, keeping the E.** Patient 1's E was 13 mm against 7.5 mm for an average premolar. Trim about 2 mm a side toward 7-8 mm, guided by the CEJ width, and cover with composite. The E's roots resorb and are replaced by bone.
4. **Kokich 2006, ankylosis.** Flat interdental bone means the teeth are erupting together; oblique bone confirms ankylosis. Extract while growth remains, with no space maintainer, which allows orthodontic implant-site development.
5. **Kokich 2006, ridge and timing.** The ridge narrows about 25 % early and 30 % by 7 y as cited (Ostler abstract: 25 % in 3 y, then 4 %), more buccally, so the implant sits lingually. Time the implant on serial cephs: about 17 y in girls and 21 y in boys as cited.
6. **Kokich 2006, closing the space.** Close if the opposing arch is crowded or the profile protrusive. Otherwise use extra anchorage (Patient 4: chincup and elastics, 4 y, 3 missing 5s). Hemisect early. An implant is the first choice for an open space.
7. **Serial extraction indications and history.** Class I, no skeletal disproportion, normal overbite, good profile; crowding 8-10 mm or more (Yezdani) or > 10 mm per arch (Proffit). Kjellgren coined the term (1929), Hotz renamed it "guidance of eruption" (both per Yezdani p 6433), and Proffit defines it as timed extraction of primary and then permanent teeth for severe crowding.
8. **Serial extraction sequences.** Dewel C→D→4: C at 8.5 y, D a year later. Tweed and Nance D→4→C, starting at 8 y. Proffit extracts the D at ½-⅔ root formation of the 4. Sequelae: deeper bite, lingually tipped incisors, residual canine-to-premolar space. It is an adjunct, and Little found 73 % unsatisfactory at ≥ 10 y.
9. **Kouvelis 2018.** A systematic review of 14 retrospective studies (ROBINS-I moderate to critical), with no meta-analysis. Ten found no difference; four were significant, two each way (derived differences: Porto 1.7°, Kumari 4.0 mm with nonextraction higher; Sivakumar 1.4 mm, Paquette 2.3° the other way). Four-premolar extraction has no specific effect on the skeletal vertical. The wedge effect is unproven; one proposed reason (Gkantidis, p 184) is that molars mesialise along the occlusal plane, not along the jaw planes.
10. **Stephens 2005.** 20 vs 20, matched at debond on age, sex, U1-SN, L1-NB and the H-line, followed from about 15.1 to 29.7 y (derived means, Table I). LS-E −1.5 vs −1.6 mm (P .82). The profile straightened (N'-Sn-Pg' +4.0° / +2.9°) while the H-line held. Males changed more (−3.2 vs −0.9 mm; nose 6.7 vs 3.1 mm, chin 7.9 vs 4.0 mm).

## One-paragraph verdict

The topic's rationale is consistent even though its evidence is thin. Decide early, by the growth clock. Remember that bone follows teeth, so a tooth can be used to build or keep a ridge. Judge every extraction by where it leaves the incisors and lips, not by the word "extraction". The two papers with data are a 14-study review of retrospective cohorts and a 20-per-group matched cohort. Both say the label itself does little: no skeletal vertical effect (Kouvelis) and no difference in how the profile ages once incisors finish in the same place (Stephens). The three expert papers give the working algorithms: canine uncovering, the missing-premolar decision tree and the serial-extraction sequences. They also carry the topic's overstatements. Interception is said to work with "high predictability" but fails in about a third. Open exposure is not faster. Hemisection is not better. Serial extraction is not more stable. For the exam, quote the algorithms with the later numbers beside them, use Proffit's thresholds (> 10 mm, ½-⅔ root, the lateral-root midline) where the papers differ, and say plainly that four-premolar extraction to shorten a long face has no skeletal support (posterior extractions in true open bite, as in Aras 2002, are the narrow exception).
