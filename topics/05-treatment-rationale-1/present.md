### 🔗 Topic 5 (Treatment Rationale) · papers 1 and 5 at a glance
- **Kokich 2004, labial canines.** *Which uncovering technique should the orthodontist ask the surgeon for?* Decide on **4 criteria**: labiolingual position, vertical position against the **MGJ**, amount of gingiva, mesiodistal position. **Excision** only if the crown is coronal to the MGJ with enough gingiva; **apically positioned flap** when gingiva is short or the crown lies mesially over the lateral root; **closed eruption** for a high or mid-alveolar crown.
- **Kokich 2004, palatal canines.** *When and how?* Extract the primary canine early if the crown is not past the **mesial surface of the lateral root**. Otherwise **uncover before brackets**, remove bone **down to the CEJ** and let the canine erupt alone (**about 6–8 months**), then align. He claims shorter treatment and better periodontal and esthetic results; later RCTs found open exposure **equivalent to closed exposure, not superior** (VERIFIED, abstracts).
- **Stephens 2005, the profile.** *Do extraction and nonextraction faces age differently when both finish at the same incisor position and lip line?* **No.** Over about **15 years (≈15 → 30 y)** both flattened the same way: lips **≈1.5 mm** further behind the E-line, profile straighter. The authors attribute it to **nose and chin growth**, much larger in males (upper lip **−3.2 vs −0.9 mm** to the E-line).
- **Stephens 2005, perception.** *Do people see it, and do they mind?* 105 orthodontists and 225 laypeople saw a **small-to-moderate change (33.9–39.2 %)**, but it did not match the ceph changes (**1 of 18** correlations) and there was **no preference pattern** (about 30 / 30 / 30 %).

<div class="banner first"><h1>Surgical and Orthodontic Management of Impacted Maxillary Canines</h1><p>by <b>Vincent G. Kokich</b> · Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2004;126(3):278–283 · University of Washington</p></div>

## 📚 Study purpose
To review the management of impacted maxillary canines: which **uncovering technique** and **timing** the orthodontist should choose and **tell the surgeon**. If not instructed, the surgeon may choose an inappropriate technique: a difficult, sometimes lengthy eruption and an unpredictable esthetic result (p 278).

## 🔬 What the article is (design)
- **Narrative review (symposium paper)**, section "Advances in Orthodontics & Dentofacial Surgery". Presented at the **AAO/AAOMS Symposium, 6–8 February 2004**, Palm Springs; submitted and accepted June 2004.
- **No methods, sample or statistics.** 13 references; **5 single-patient cases** (Figs 1–5, 30 panels, derived). <span class="bad">Level 5 (expert opinion; our grading)</span>.

## 📌 General facts (p 278)
- After the **third molars**, maxillary canines are the **most commonly impacted** permanent teeth (Bass 1967).
- About **⅓ labial or within the alveolus, ⅔ palatal** (Johnston 1969): 2 : 1 palatal to labial (derived).
- **Labial impaction causes:** ectopic migration of the crown **over the lateral incisor root**, or a **midline shift** leaving too little space.
- **Before surgery:** Williams (1981) suggested extracting the **primary canine as early as 8 or 9 years** (labial or intra-alveolar canine); Olive (2002): **open space** and it may erupt on its own. If both fail, uncover surgically.

## 🛠️ The three uncovering techniques (labial / intra-alveolar canine)

| Technique | What is done | Use when | <span class="bad">Avoid when</span> |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Excisional uncovering** (Fig 1) | Tissue over the crown cut away, a simple procedure | Crown labial, **coronal to the MGJ**, enough gingiva | Crown **apical** to the MGJ: no gingiva left over the labial surface; too little gingiva (Fig 3); crown mesial over the lateral root |
| **Apically positioned flap, APF** (Vanarsdall & Corn; Figs 2, 3) | Flap moved apically: exposes the crown and **increases the gingiva width** (Fig 3B) | **Too little gingiva** (the only technique that predictably makes more); crown **mesial over the lateral root** | Crown **significantly apical** (high): unstable crown, **reintrusion** after treatment |
| **Closed eruption** (Kokich & Mathews; Fig 4) | Labial flap raised, enough bone around the crown removed for eruption without impinging on bone (Fig 4D); then erupted with a ballista loop (Fig 4E–G). | **High** or **intra-alveolar** crown | Crown mesial over the lateral root (generally not recommended) |

## 🧭 Key "result": the four selection criteria (pp 278–279)

| Criterion | Finding → technique |
|---|---|
| **1 · Labiolingual** position | **Labial:** any of the 3 (little if any bone over the crown). **Centre of the alveolus:** excision and APF harder (extensive labial bone removal) → closed eruption (Fig 4) |
| **2 · Vertical** position vs the MGJ | **Mostly coronal:** any of the 3 (Fig 1). **Apical:** no excision (Figs 2, 3). **Significantly apical:** no APF either → **closed eruption**, which gives adequate gingiva and no long-term reintrusion |
| **3 · Amount of gingiva** | **Insufficient:** <mark>APF is the only technique that predictably makes more gingiva</mark> (Fig 3). **Enough to leave ≥2–3 mm of attached gingiva over the crown after eruption:** any of the 3 |
| **4 · Mesiodistal** position | Crown **mesial, over the lateral incisor root:** <mark>APF</mark>, complete exposure needed to move it through the alveolus (Fig 2); closed eruption or excision generally not recommended |

### 📐 "Above / below the MGJ": reading the figure legends
- The text says **apical / coronal** to the MGJ; the legends of Figs 2–4 say **above / below**. In the maxilla, **above = apical (high)** and **below = coronal**.
- **Fig 3** crown is "partially below" the MGJ: APF was chosen for **insufficient gingiva** (criterion 3), not for height.

## ⚙️ Orthodontic mechanics and stability (pp 279–281)
- **Mimic normal eruption.** After closed eruption, erupt the tooth into the **centre of the alveolar ridge** (normal labial gingiva), e.g. with a **ballista loop** (Fig 4): a simple, unobtrusive **vertical** force.
- <span class="bad">Avoid pulling it labially</span>: risk of **bony dehiscence**, accelerated migration of the labial gingival margin and **labial recession**.
- **Crown mesial over the lateral root** (APF, Fig 2): **elastomeric chain toward the centre of the edentulous ridge**; the canine was then moved distally (Fig 2E).

## 📊 Labial evidence: Vermette et al 1995 (APF vs closed eruption)

| Outcome | APF | Closed eruption |
|---|---|---|
| Gingival index, plaque index, pocket depth, bone level | <span class="good">No significant difference</span> between the 2 techniques | (same) |
| Crown length | <span class="bad">Longer than normal</span>: gingival margin migrates apically | Similar to the contralateral tooth |
| High labial canines | <span class="bad">Tend to reintrude</span> after treatment | Not observed |
| Why reintrusion | The flap heals to the adjacent **mucosa**; eruption draws the mucosa coronally; afterwards it **pulls the crown apically** | Becker 2002: similar favourable esthetics (**central incisors**) |

**Radiographic localisation (p 281): buccal object rule (Richards)** on **2 periapicals** at slightly different horizontal angles. The buccal object moves **opposite** to the beam; a palatal crown moves the **same** way: <mark>SLOB = Same Lingual, Opposite Buccal</mark>.

## 🦷 Palatal impaction (pp 281–283)
- **The most common impaction orthodontists see.** **Ericson & Kurol 1988:** extracting the **primary canine** early gives self-correction with **high predictability** if, on periapicals, the canine crown overlies the lateral incisor root but is **not past its mesial surface**. **Well beyond** the mesial surface: no self-correction; it must be uncovered and aligned.

| | Conventional | Early (Kokich & Mathews) |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | After the first **6–9 months** of alignment, space opened first | **Before** orthodontic treatment; in some cases the **late mixed dentition** |
| **Surgery** | Crown uncovered; if too little bone is removed, the canine seems not to move (p 282) | Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap; <mark>all bone over the crown removed down to the CEJ</mark>; flap replaced with a **hole** over the crown; dressing occasionally if high in the palate |
| **Eruption** | Dragged soon after surgery toward the space, **across the lingual of the lateral incisor roots** | **Erupts on its own** to the occlusal plane in **about 6–8 months**; then bracket, root moved labially, crown translated into the arch |
| **Cited outcome** | **Woloshyn (n = 32):** bone more apical on the **distal of the lateral** and **mesial of the canine** vs contralateral; lateral and canine root resorption typical; judges could identify the treated canine | **Schmidt (thesis):** better bone and attachment levels on canine and lateral; **little to no lateral root resorption**; hard to tell which canine was impacted |

**Why a palatal canine "will not move" (p 282).** The crown often touches the lingual of the central and lateral incisor roots. Once the follicle is removed, the tooth **cannot resorb bone** over the crown efficiently: **enamel has no resorbing cells**, so bone goes only slowly by **pressure necrosis**. It looks ankylosed, but <span class="good">ankylosis is uncommon</span> ("low", no figure given); the usual cause is **insufficient bone removal** (Proffit Ch 15 agrees, VERIFIED, study notes).

## 🧠 Main conclusions
1. The orthodontist must **choose the technique and instruct the surgeon**.
2. **Labial canines:** use the 4 criteria; excision only coronal to the MGJ with enough gingiva; APF for short gingiva or a mesial crown; closed eruption for a high or intra-alveolar crown.
3. Erupt into the **centre of the ridge** (ballista loop); **avoid labial pull**. APF on high canines → long crowns and reintrusion.
4. **Palatal canines:** extract the primary canine early when the crown is not past the mesial surface of the lateral root; otherwise **uncover early, remove bone to the CEJ, let it erupt autonomously**, then align: claimed <span class="good">shorter treatment, superior periodontal and esthetic results</span>.

## ⚠️ Limitations (our reading)
- **Expert opinion** with 5 successful cases: no measurements, no n of its own, no statistics.
- The main palatal claim rests on an **unpublished thesis**. Published as **Schmidt & Kokich 2007** (VERIFIED, abstract): treated canine and adjacent lateral roots **shorter** than controls, lower crestal bone at the lateral, treated canine identified **78.9 %** of the time; better for the canine than closed exposure, **similar for adjacent teeth**.
- **Timing and surgery change together** (younger patients, different era), so age is confounded with technique; the **treatment-time** claim cites no data.
- **Vermette:** 30 maxillary **anterior** teeth, not canines only (APF 18, closed 12; VERIFIED). **Becker** studied central incisors.
- **Reporting note (p 283):** judges could identify the treated canine because its tissue "compared **favorably**": the two halves point opposite ways; "unfavorably" fits Woloshyn's ~75 % identification (VERIFIED, abstract).

## 🔄 What later evidence and Proffit say

| Kokich's claim | Later evidence (VERIFIED, abstracts) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Primary canine extraction "will" normalise it | Naoumova 2015 RCT **69 % vs 39 %**; Bazargani 2014 **67 % vs 42 %** (better if younger; lost arch perimeter → palatal arch); Ericson & Kurol: 91 % distal vs **64 %** mesial to the lateral-root **midline** (VERIFIED, secondary) | <span class="good">Helps</span>, not guaranteed |
| Open early exposure superior | Parkin 2013 RCT attachment **MD 0.1 mm** (−0.2 to 0.5); Cochrane 2017 success **RR 0.99** (0.93–1.06) | <span class="bad">Equivalent</span> |
| Treatment time reduced | Björksved 2021 RCT total time **MD −0.1 months** (−3.2 to 2.9) | <span class="bad">No difference</span> |

<div class="banner"><h1>Long-Term Profile Changes in Extraction and Nonextraction Patients</h1><p>by <b>Stephens, Boley, Behrents, Alexander and Buschang</b> · Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2005;128(4):450–457 · Baylor College of Dentistry and Saint Louis University</p></div>

## 🎯 Study purpose
To find whether **extraction and nonextraction patients treated to the same incisor position and lip line** show different **long-term soft-tissue profile changes**, and whether **orthodontists and laypeople perceive** and **prefer** those changes.

**Background (pp 450–451):** a debate of almost 100 years: extraction "dishes in" the face vs nonextraction "blows out" the lips. At debond extraction faces are on average **2–4 mm flatter** (refs 3–7); a change that is measured but not perceived is questionable.

## 🔬 Study design
- **Retrospective matched cohort + perception survey.** **40 white, Class I and Class II** patients, **one orthodontist**, one private practice: **20 four-premolar extraction, 20 nonextraction**, each **6 M + 14 F**.
- **Matched at posttreatment** on age, sex and **3 ceph values**: **U1-SN** and **L1-NB** (dental protrusion, chosen from a discriminant analysis) and **Holdaway's H-line** (lip protrusion independent of nose length). The extraction group was picked first with values "in the normal range"; nonextraction patients were matched to it.
- **Ceph:** Dentofacial Planner, **15 landmarks**, x–y coordinates from **posttreatment sella** on a reference line **S-N −7°**; h = parallel, v = perpendicular; **9 measurements** (Table II). 15 patients replicated: no systematic error; method error 0.3–1.4.
- **Survey:** 2 versions of **20 patients** each, posttreatment vs long-term profile photos side by side (left/right random, 4 pairs per page). Raters: **105 orthodontists, 115 laywomen, 110 laymen** (330, derived); mailed surveys 60 % reply. **106-mm VAS** "same" → "very different" (distance ÷ 106 = % change perceived), plus a preference: posttreatment, long-term or neither.
- **Stats:** t tests (groups), paired t (changes), single-sample t (perception), Pearson r, chi-square.

## 🧠 Main conclusions (paper's 5)
1. Treated to the **same incisor position and lip line**, extraction and nonextraction patients **stayed alike** 15 years later: <mark>the long-term change was not due to the type of treatment</mark>.
2. Between **15 and 30 years** the lips became **relatively retrusive** to the E- and S-lines; H-line did not change, so the authors attribute it mainly to **nose and chin growth**.
3. **Males changed more** than females: normal sexual dimorphism in soft-tissue aging, especially nose and chin.
4. Small-to-moderate change was perceived, equally by orthodontists and laypeople, with **no correlation** to the ceph change.
5. **No pattern of preference**; clear preferences for 87 % of profiles, but nothing showed what drove them.

## ⚠️ Limitations (our reading)
- **Matched at posttreatment**, with no pretreatment data: it tests whether extraction changes **aging**, not whether extraction **flattens the face**. Paquette 1992 matched **pretreatment** and found nonextraction faces about **2 mm fuller** (p 454), still present about 14.5 years out of retention (VERIFIED, abstract).
- **Small:** 20 per group; only differences of about **d 0.9** (≈1.5–1.9 mm in lip change) were detectable (derived). "No difference" ≠ equivalence: 95 % CI of the LS-E change difference **−1.22 to 1.42 mm** (derived).
- **No untreated controls**; white patients, one practice, finished "in the normal range"; Class I/II split, which premolars and retention not reported.
- Raters could see the age difference; photo standardisation and the ceph superimposition are not described; about 200 tests (derived count) without correction.
- **Reporting notes:** convexity wording (box above); the text gives ~30 / 30 / 30 where Table VII ranges 26.5–42.3 %; the abstract says raters judged among 40 profiles, each saw 20.

## 🔄 Other studies and Proffit
- **Agree:** Zierhut 2000 (no posttreatment difference; both flattened with nasal and mandibular growth), Luppanapornlarp 1993, Bravo Vallejo 2024 (similar attractiveness about 35 years after treatment), Formby 1994 (untreated, 18–42 y: male lips retrude). All VERIFIED, abstracts. Cited in the paper (p 456): Boley 1998, dental professionals could tell an extraction case only **50 %** of the time.
- **Treatment effect itself:** Konstantonis 2018 SR: extraction retracts the **lower lip 1.96 mm**, **upper 1.26 mm** more, nasolabial angle **+4.21°**; very low certainty (VERIFIED).
- **Proffit (VERIFIED, study notes):** Ch 2, the nose and chin grow more prominent after adolescence, the lips do not, so <mark>plan lip support at the end of treatment</mark>. Ch 7: comparable extraction and nonextraction cases differ by about 2 mm of lip fullness; the borderline decision rests on crowding, incisor position and the nose–lip–chin profile.

## 🦷 Clinical implications
- Decide extraction on the **target incisor and lip position**, not on fear of later "dishing in": finished alike, both groups aged alike.
- **On average both groups flattened** after treatment: about **1.5 mm** more relative upper-lip retrusion to the E-line over 15 years (Table III), **3.2 mm in males vs 0.9 mm in females** (Table IV); individual faces varied (SD up to 2.5 mm). Avoid finishing a teenage boy's lips already flat (our inference).
- Ceph numbers are not what people notice; a profile can age better or worse whatever the treatment.
