## Slide 2 — Purpose of the Article
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Options when at least one lower second premolar is congenitally missing
  - Right decision at the right time: its effects last a lifetime
  - Two themes: implant-site preservation and interdisciplinary planning
  - Format: five patients plus expert discussion, no methods section
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: The authors present the treatment options for a patient missing at least one lower second premolar [p437]. Their key message is timing: some early decisions, often made in the mixed dentition, affect dental health for life. Two themes run through the paper: preserving the future implant site, and planning with the surgeon and the restorative dentist. The paper gives no prevalence figure. If asked: in Polder's 2004 meta-analysis, agenesis of any tooth, third molars aside, affected 4.6% of European males and 6.3% of European females, and the lower second premolar was the tooth most often missing; the abstract gives no figure for that tooth alone.
- Time: 40 s

## Slide 3 — Key Clinical Decisions: Two Pathways
- Layout: table
- Content:
  | | Keep the space open | Close the space |
  |---|---|---|
  | Aim | Right width + ideal ridge → implant | No harm to occlusion or profile |
  | Favoured when | Closure would harm the profile (no crowding, normal profile); lingual incisors (Patient 2) | Opposing-arch crowding; protrusive profile |
  | Main risk | Ridge loss while growth finishes | Lips over-retracted |
  | How, when | Implant after vertical growth stops | Plain closure, anchorage, or early hemisection |
  - Why not a bridge? Pulp risk in young teeth; bonded posterior bridges questionable
  - Decide on growth stage, ridge, profile (lips vs chin), crowding, occlusion
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: Every case starts with one decision: keep the space open or close it [p437]. Either choice affects the profile, the occlusion and the patient's dental health for life [p437]. Open means two keys: exactly the right amount of space, and an ideal ridge. Closed means no damage to the occlusion or the profile. Why an implant, not a bridge? Full-coverage bridges in young patients can devitalise the pulp, and posterior resin-bonded bridges have questionable survival, so a single-tooth implant is the first choice [p441]. The choice depends on five things: growth stage, the ridge, the profile, meaning the lips against the chin, crowding in the opposing arch, and the occlusion [p443]. If asked: Creugers 1998 found agenesis and a mandibular site were both risk factors for posterior resin-bonded bridge failure.
- Time: 55 s

- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Girl, 8 y 3 mo; both lower 5s missing
  - Deciduous molars ankylosed: oblique bone in panel B
  - Molars out, no space maintainer: 4 and 6 drift, defect self-corrects
  - Lingual incisors proclined → space reopened (implant-site development) → implant, Class I
- Figure: kfig2.png
- Speaker notes: Patient 2 is a girl of 8 years 3 months missing both lower second premolars [p437-439]. Panel A shows her submerged deciduous molars. In B the bone between the E and the first molar is oblique: the molar kept erupting. All were extracted, with no space maintainer. In C and D the premolar and molar drifted together, and their eruption erased the vertical defect. The ceph in E shows lingual lower incisors, so they were proclined and the space reopened, F. Then implant, G, a second-premolar crown, H, and Class I molars and canines, I. The paper calls this orthodontic implant-site development: moving the teeth built the bone [p438]. If asked: the text says "second molars" but the caption says deciduous first and second molars; only one side is shown.

## Slide 6 — Patient 3: One Implant Anchors a Two-Tooth Space
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Adult woman; lower right 5 and 6 missing; lower 7 Class II
  - Space too big for 1 tooth, too small for 2
  - Wax-up → second-premolar implant → bracket on provisional crown
  - Lower 7 protracted to Class I; incisors and profile unchanged
- Figure: kfig3.png
- Speaker notes: Patient 3 is an adult woman missing her lower right second premolar and first molar [p438-439]. Her lower second molar was Class II to the upper first molar, panel A, and the space was too big for one tooth, too small for two, B. After alignment, C, a diagnostic wax-up set the implant at the second-premolar site, D. After integration a provisional crown went on, E, with a bracket, F. The implant anchored the second molar forward, G, without moving the front teeth: a normal-width crown, H, and Class I, I. This kept the orthodontics and restorations simpler [p439]. The upper second molar has no antagonist; if it overerupts, extract it or add an implant. If asked: the D-E caption says first premolar; the text and photos show the second.

## Slide 7 — Patient 4: Close All Spaces With a Chincup
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Girl, 13 y 8 mo; upper right 5, both lower 5s missing
  - Class II, 5-mm overjet, minor crowding; jaws well related
  - Extract + close all → lips over-retracted, so protract the molars
  - Chincup + elastics: Class I, profile kept, no restorations, 4 y
- Figure: panels fig4-A=Before: Class II, 5-mm OJ | fig4-C=Start: profile ideal | fig4-D=After: Class I molars | fig4-F=Lower incisors held, 6s mesial
- Speaker notes: Patient 4 is a girl of 13 years 8 months missing the upper right and both lower second premolars [p439-440]. Panel A shows Class II with a 5-mm overjet; crowding was minor. The tracing in C shows well-related jaws, a good profile and normally placed incisors [p439]. Extracting the upper left premolar and closing every space would have over-retracted the lips against the chin, so the molars had to come forward. Today that means mini-implants; she was treated before them, so a chincup and elastics slid the first molars forward on a continuous archwire. D shows Class I molars, and F shows the lower incisors did not move back. No restorations, but 4 years. If asked: the caption's "end-to-end" most likely means the molars; the overjet mechanics are not reported.

## Slide 9 — Preserve the Space: Reduce the E
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Guide: E width at the CEJ on a bitewing
  - Bur: ~2 mm off each side → crown ~7-8 mm, pulp safe
  - Composite: covers dentin, builds occlusion, prevents supereruption
  - Divergent E roots resorb and bone replaces them
- Figure: panels fig1-C=Before: 13 mm wide | fig1-D=Mesial and distal wafers cut | fig1-F=Composite build-up | fig1-H=Spaces closed around E
- Speaker notes: Now the techniques. First, keeping the E until the implant [p441-442]. An E is too wide for the posterior fit, so it is narrowed to premolar width [p441]. Measure its width at the cementoenamel junction on a bitewing; C is the 13-mm tooth. With a carbide fissure or diamond bur, cut a wafer mesially and distally, D, about 2 mm a side, for a crown of about 7 to 8 mm, sparing the pulp. Composite, F, covers the exposed dentin and builds up the short crown so the opposing tooth does not supererupt. Then the spaces close, H, and the E works as a premolar [p442]. Its divergent roots usually resorb, and bone replaces them. If asked: 13 minus 2 a side is 9 mm, so follow the CEJ guide.
- Time: 55 s

## Slide 10 — Ridge Resorption and Implant Timing
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - E out: ridge −25% by 4 y, −30% by 7 y (Ostler)
  - Still implantable, but facial loss > lingual → lingual implant
  - Keep the E until vertical growth stops: girls ~17, boys ~21 y
  - Confirm on serial cephs (ramus growth stopped), not by age
- Figure: panels fig5-B=Narrowed ridge | fig5-G=Implant at 17 y
- Speaker notes: Why keep the E? Because the ridge shrinks once it goes [p441]. The paper, citing Ostler and Kokich, says it narrows 25% in the first 4 years after extraction and another 5% by 7 years: 30% in total. It stays wide enough for an implant without a graft, but the facial side resorbs more, so the implant sits lingual and the restorative dentist must adjust cusp loading to avoid abutment or crown fracture. Panel B shows a narrowed ridge. The implant waits until vertical growth stops on serial cephs: about 17 in girls and 21 in boys, citing Fudalej. Panel G is Patient 5's implant at 17. If asked: the Ostler abstract says 25% within 3 years, then 4% over the next 3.
- Time: 50 s

## Slide 11 — Diagnosing Ankylosis
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Flat bone between E and the 6 and 4 → not ankylosed
  - Oblique bone, lower around the E → ankylosis
  - Ankylosed + significant growth left → extract early
  - Little growth left + slight infraocclusion → can keep it
- Figure: panels fig1-B=Flat bone: not ankylosed | fig2-B=Oblique bone: ankylosed
- Speaker notes: How do we diagnose ankylosis? The paper's most reliable sign is the bone level between the E and the first molar and first premolar [p443]. Panel 1B, Patient 1: flat bone, so the teeth erupt together and the E can stay. Panel 2B, Patient 2: oblique bone, lower around the E, which confirms ankylosis. Then growth decides. With significant growth left, extract, because the ridge could become vertically deficient and need a graft, and vertical grafting is unpredictable and costly [p442]. With little growth left and only slight infraocclusion, keep the E to save ridge width. If asked: Proffit also offers decoronation for a growing patient, which this paper does not mention.
- Time: 45 s

## Slide 12 — Orthodontic Implant-Site Development
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - No space maintainer: let the 4 and 6 drift together
  - Push apart later: roots leave bone → robust ridge, longer treatment
  - Late extraction, narrow ridge: move the 4 into the 5 site
  - Implant goes in the old 4 site, no graft
- Figure: panels fig2-C=4 and 6 drifted together | fig2-F=Space reopened | fig5-D=4 pushed distally | fig5-F=Bone found at surgery
- Speaker notes: After the E comes out, the authors usually place no space maintainer [p443]. The first molar and premolar drift and erupt together, 2C. Pushing them apart later, 2F, takes longer, but the moving roots leave bone as wide as a premolar and a molar: a robust ridge. This is orthodontic implant-site development. The second route, for a late extraction and a narrow ridge: move the first premolar into the second-premolar site, 5D; at surgery, 5F, its old site had bone for the implant. The authors cite earlier studies showing a wide root can cross a narrow ridge, plus their own unquantified cases. If asked: Hom and Turley (14 adults, molar sites): ridge 1.2 mm wider, mean crestal bone loss 1.3 mm. Eliasova 2014 (55 patients): ridge width up 28.5%.
- Time: 50 s

## Slide 13 — Implant as Anchor, and When to Load It
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - 5 and 6 missing: one implant, two jobs, fewer restorations
  - Place it from a diagnostic wax-up, before space closure
  - Bracket on crown: immobile anchor, no unwanted movement
  - Authors: load once the provisional is on; force continuous, one-way
- Figure: panels fig3-D=Implant from wax-up | fig3-F=Bracket on provisional crown | fig3-G=Lower 7 pulled forward | fig3-H=Final crown, normal width
- Speaker notes: When the second premolar and first molar are both missing, one implant can do two jobs [p443]. Place it precisely from a diagnostic wax-up, panel D, restore it provisionally and bond a bracket, F. It is an immobile anchor for protraction or retraction, G, with no unwanted movement elsewhere, and the patient needs fewer restorations, H. Loading traditionally waited for full integration. The authors argue for early loading, soon after the provisional crown: orthodontic force is continuous and one-directional, unlike intermittent, multidirectional chewing, and they state it stimulates bone and enhances osseointegration [p443]. If asked: the support is a rabbit study in which a static load did no harm; no bone stimulation, no orthodontic patients. Patient 3's implant went in after initial alignment and was loaded after integration.
- Time: 50 s

## Slide 14 — When to Close the Space Instead
- Layout: two-column
- Content:
  Left: Closure is the simple choice when
  - The opposing arch is crowded
  - The profile is protrusive
  Right: Normal profile, no crowding → close without retracting incisors
  - Miniscrews or mini-implants (intraoral)
  - Facemask or chincup (extraoral; Patient 4)
  - Or early hemisection of the E: the 6 drifts mesially
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: Now the other pathway: closing the space, which needs no restoration, as in Patient 4 [p443-444]. Closure is easy when the opposing arch is crowded or the profile is protrusive. With no crowding and a normal profile, plain closure could harm the profile, so we need anchorage to bring the molars forward instead of the incisors back: miniscrews or mini-implants inside the mouth, or a facemask or chincup outside. A third route is early hemisection of the E, so the first molar erupts mesially without moving the lower incisors; it needs an early first visit and regular monitoring. If asked: a 2025 split-mouth trial found hemisection gave no clinically relevant gain over simple extraction, with more complications. Proffit adds pulp therapy on the retained half, which this paper omits.
- Time: 50 s

## Slide 15 — Summary of Treatment Options
- Layout: table
- Content:
  | Clinical scenario | Approach |
  |---|---|
  | Healthy E, implant planned | Reduce E + composite; keep until growth stops |
  | Ankylosed E, growth remaining | Extract early (avoid vertical defect), no space maintainer; later push 4 and 6 apart |
  | No crowding, normal profile, closure wanted | Close only with anchorage (TADs, chincup) |
  | 5 and 6 missing: wide space | One implant as anchor; close the rest |
  | Narrow ridge after late extraction | Move 4 into 5 site; implant in 4 site |
  - Opposing-arch crowding or protrusive profile → simple closure
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: This table pulls it together. A healthy E with an implant planned: reduce it, add composite, and keep it until growth stops. An ankylosed E with growth left: extract early to avoid a vertical defect, with no space maintainer, and later push the premolar and molar apart to build the site. Normal profile, no crowding, but closure wanted: only with anchorage. Second premolar and first molar missing: one implant from a wax-up, used as the anchor. Narrow ridge after a late extraction: move the first premolar back and put the implant in its site. And with crowding or a protrusive profile, simple closure is the easy answer.
- Time: 45 s

## Slide 17 — Final Conclusions
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Decide early: choices made in childhood last a lifetime
  - Growth is the clock: implant after vertical growth stops on cephs
  - Protect the ridge: keep a reduced E, or build bone orthodontically
  - Close the space only when profile and occlusion allow, or add anchorage
  - Teamwork: orthodontist, surgeon and restorative dentist decide together
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: To conclude. First, decide early, because choices made in childhood last a lifetime. Second, growth is the clock: place the implant only after vertical growth has stopped on serial cephs. Third, protect the ridge, by keeping a reduced E or by moving teeth to build bone. Fourth, close the space only when profile and occlusion allow, or add anchorage. In one line, the options are an implant, space closure, or moving teeth to build bone. Finally, these decisions used to be made mainly by the orthodontist; now the surgeon and the restorative dentist share them, and teamwork gives the best result [p444].
- Time: 45 s

## Slide 18 — How Strong Is the Evidence?
- Layout: table
- Content:
  | Claim | What it rests on |
  |---|---|
  | Implant is the first choice | General ADA statement (2004) |
  | Ridge −25% / −30% | Retrospective cast study, same group (1994) |

  | A wide root crosses a narrow ridge | Two older space-closure studies (1979, 1984) |

  - No outcome measurements, follow-up periods or failures reported
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: How much weight can we give this? It is a Clinician's Corner: expert opinion illustrated by five selected, successful patients, with no methods, outcome measures, follow-up or failures. So it sits low on the evidence hierarchy: Oxford level 5, or level 4 at best for the cases. The table shows the support. Implant-first rests on a general ADA statement. The ridge and growth numbers come from the first author's group; the growth paper was in press. The narrow-ridge claim rests on two older studies, one in adult molar sites. Early loading in orthodontic patients rests on one rabbit study. Its strength is a clear, practical framework from an experienced interdisciplinary team. If asked: 5 of 22 references (23%) include the first author; several technique steps have no reference.

## Slide 19 — Points to Discuss
- Layout: bullets
- Content:
  - Patient 2 at 8 y: was agenesis confirmed? Late premolars occur
  - Decoronation instead of extracting an ankylosed E?
  - Autotransplantation: a biological option the paper does not discuss
  - Chincup protraction in a Class II girl: any vertical change?
  - Does an implant stay level after growth "ends"?
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: Five points for discussion. First, Patient 2 was treated at 8, and the paper does not say how agenesis was confirmed. Proffit says good premolars seldom form after 8. But Aasheim and Ogaard found that 11.3% of boys with apparently missing second premolars at 9 formed them by 12; for girls the abstract gives 2.9%. Second, Proffit offers decoronation for an ankylosed E in a growing child. Third, autotransplantation is not discussed. Fourth, Proffit links the chincup to downward and backward mandibular rotation, and Patient 4 has no vertical measures. Fifth, implants can sink relative to their neighbours even in adults; my backup slide covers this.

## Slide 21 — Backup: Does a Retained E Last?
- Layout: stats
- Content:
  - 7 / 59 — Es lost by a mean age of 20.5 y (Bjerklin 2000)
  - 55% — some infraocclusion (0.5-4.5 mm) at 19-20 y (Bjerklin 2000)
  - 10.53 — mean width (mm) of a retained lower E (Bjerklin 2000)
  - 86% — adults' retained Es still working at mean 12.4-y follow-up (Sletten 2003)
  - No E lost after age 20: a sound E at 20 does well
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: This paper keeps the E only until growth ends. But how long does a retained E last on its own? Bjerklin and Bennett followed 59 Es in 41 patients from 11 or 12 years to a mean of 20.5 years. Seven were lost, none after age 20, and 55% showed some infraocclusion by 19 to 20. Their mean E width was 10.53 mm, so Patient 1's 13 mm was a large tooth. Sletten and colleagues followed adults for a mean of 12.4 years: 24 of 28 Es, 86%, were still working. So an E that reaches 20 in good shape has a good prognosis. If asked: Proffit says many Es last into the early twenties or beyond.
- Time: 45 s

## Slide 22 — Backup: Does Growth Really Stop at 17 / 21?
- Layout: stats
- Content:
  - 8 mm — upper 6 eruption in girls aged 9-25 y (Iseri 1996)
  - 28 / 28 — adults with a step beside the implant (Bernard 2004)
  - ~40% — implant infraposition after 17-19 y of follow-up (Andersson 2013)
  - 0.08 — mm a year of infraocclusion beside implants in adults (Polymeri 2020)
  - Fudalej 2007 abstract: growth insignificant only after the second decade
- Figure: none
- Speaker notes: The 17 and 21 come from a paper that was in press. Its published 2007 abstract gives no cut-off ages; it says growth becomes clinically insignificant only after the second decade. Teeth also keep erupting. Iseri and Solow measured 8 mm of upper first-molar eruption in girls from 9 to 25. Bernard found a step beside every implant, in 14 young and 14 mature adults. Andersson found infraposition in about 40% of patients after 17 to 19 years. Polymeri measured about 0.08 mm a year in adults. So, as Iseri and Solow advise, we warn patients the crown may need revision later. If asked: these data are mostly anterior maxilla, with few premolar sites; Heij 2006, with Kokich as co-author, says long- and short-face patients stay at risk after 20.
- Time: 50 s
