## Root resorption associated with orthodontic tooth movement

### Q1 · R2 exam 2019 · Promotion 2021

> Most resorption occurs with:

- **Recalled answer:** intrusion and lingual torque; Intrusion and lingual torque (highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Intrusion and lingual root torque (Weltman 2010, Discussion p471-472)
- Weltman finds intrusive movements the most damaging because they load the apex. Lingual apical torque of maxillary centrals is strongly correlated with RR, and the two together are the strongest causes of OIIRR.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q2 · R2 exam 2019

> Most resorbed tooth

- **Recalled answer:** maxillary central · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Maxillary incisors (Weltman 2010, Introduction p462)
- Weltman says maxillary incisors average more apical RR than any other teeth, followed by mandibular incisors and first molars. The paper treats maxillary incisors as one group and does not separate centrals from laterals.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q3 · R2 exam 2019

> Active & passive SLB and conventional brackets with broad wires

- **Recalled answer:** all give same results · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** No difference: conventional, active and passive self-ligating brackets give the same root resorption (Weltman 2010, Self-ligating vs conventional p470 and Discussion p472 (Blake, Pandis, Scott et al))
- Weltman reports no significant RR difference between conventional and active or passive self-ligating systems. In the Scott RCT, Damon3 vs Synthesis lower incisor RR was 2.26 vs 1.21 mm, not significant. The 'broad wires' part of the stem is probably garbled, so the match holds only if the question was about root resorption.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q4 · R2 exam 2019

> Root length & root resorption
>
> - a. ↑ length → ↑ root resorption
> - b. No correlation

- **Recalled answer:** a · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** a. ↑ root length → ↑ root resorption (Weltman 2010 p463 (root length listed among patient-related risk factors); reference 74 (Bollen 2002: longer teeth associated with more RR))
- Weltman lists root length as a patient-related risk factor, so 'no correlation' does not fit. The direction (longer roots resorb more) comes from a cited reference title, not from the review's own text. The RCT evidence on unusual root morphology was not significant.

### Q6 · R2 exam 2019

> Severe root resorption in ortho patients: 1-5%

- **Per the paper:** True: severe RR (>4 mm or >1/3 of root length) occurs in 1–5% of teeth (Weltman 2010, Introduction p462)
- Weltman puts severe resorption, defined as more than 4 mm or a third of the root, at 1–5% of teeth (the paper counts teeth, not patients). Burnheimer uses a different cut-off (severe >5 mm) and says about one third show moderate 3–5 mm.
- *Source tag in the recall:* Study notes: prevalence list

### Q7 · R2 exam 2019

> External root resorption 15% before treatment and 73% after; histologic studies >90% OIIRR in ortho patients; OIIRR usually less than 2.5 mm

- **Per the paper:** True: histologic OIIRR >90%; radiographic EARR 15% before and 73% after treatment; usually <2.5 mm (Weltman 2010, Introduction p462)
- All three figures appear in Weltman's introduction. Histology finds OIIRR in over 90% of treated teeth, radiographs show 15% before vs 73% after treatment, and the loss is usually under 2.5 mm and clinically minor.
- *Source tag in the recall:* Study notes: prevalence list

### Q8 · Promotion 2021

> Radiograph of tooth with root resorption of the apex. What is the movement that will cause this?
>
> - Intrusion
> - Torque
> - Extrusion

- **Recalled answer:** Intrusion (highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Intrusion (Weltman 2010 p466 (Han et al: intrusion about 4-fold RR area, extrusion not different from control) and Discussion p471)
- Weltman reports that intrusive force significantly increased apical RR, while extrusion did not differ from controls. Intrusion focuses pressure at the apex. Lingual torque also contributes, but intrusion is the classic cause.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–115, no header (p4–12)

### Q10 · Promotion 2021

> What is most ortho tx resorption?

- **Recalled answer:** External inflammatory resorption? (p13 pastes slide 'Factors affecting root resorption during orthodontic tooth movement') · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** External apical root resorption: orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption (OIIRR), a sterile surface-type (cemental) resorption (Weltman 2010 p462 (OIIRR/EARR terminology); Kindelan 2008 p70-71 (orthodontic RR is surface/cemental resorption that heals with cementum))
- Weltman calls the typical form OIIRR, seen at the apex. Under Kindelan's trauma classification (surface, inflammatory, replacement), orthodontic RR counts as surface resorption, whereas 'inflammatory' there means infection-driven resorption. So pick 'surface' if that option is offered.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

### Q11 · Promotion 2021

> Pic of OPG with dilacerated roots of 1st premolars – what will happen?

- **Recalled answer:** Root resorption · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Increased risk of root resorption (abnormal root form) (Weltman 2010 p463 (roots with developmental abnormalities among risk factors) and Discussion p472)
- Weltman lists abnormal root shape and developmental root anomalies as OIIRR risk factors in observational studies. The RCT data (Brin) found the excess non-significant. In Topic 5, the serial-extraction paper (t05_3) also lists dilaceration as a contraindication to serial extraction.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

### Q12 · Promotion 2021

> Retraction movement of lateral incisor with 200 g – what will happen?

- **Recalled answer:** Root resorption · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Root resorption (heavy force) (Weltman 2010 p465-466 (225 g vs 25 g split-mouth trials) and Conclusions p473)
- In the trials Weltman reviewed, heavy forces (225 g) produced significantly more RR than light forces (25 g): about 9x vs 5x control. About 200 g on a lateral incisor is in the heavy range, so RR is the expected harm.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

## Medication effects on the rate of orthodontic tooth movement

### Q20 · R2 exam 2019 · Promotion 2021

> What increases OTM (orthodontic tooth movement)

- **Recalled answer:** Thyroxine; Thyroxine (highlighted; pasted drug-effects table) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Thyroxine (Bartzela 2009, abstract p16; Thyroid hormones p22; Discussion p24)
- Bartzela reports that exogenous thyroxine raises the rate of tooth movement in a dose-dependent way (rat studies). Corticosteroids, PTH and vitamin D3 also increase it, while NSAIDs, bisphosphonates and dietary calcium slow it.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q21 · Promotion 2021

> Patient has decreased function of thyroid and takes thyroxine. What is the expected outcome?
>
> - Increased root resorption
> - Increased alveolar bone resorption

- **Recalled answer:** Increased alveolar bone resorption (highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Increased alveolar bone resorption/turnover (faster tooth movement), not increased root resorption (Bartzela 2009, Thyroid hormones p22 and Discussion p24; Weltman 2010 p463 (hypothyroidism a risk factor) and ref 82 (thyroid administration to reduce RR))
- Bartzela says thyroxine raises bone turnover, can cause osteoporosis and speeds OTM. Weltman lists untreated hypothyroidism as an RR risk factor and cites thyroxine given to reduce RR. A treated hypothyroid patient therefore shows more bone resorption rather than more root resorption.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–98, no header, article-based (p16–25)

### Q22 · Promotion 2021

> Patient came to ER after extraction with severe pain and there was a sign of bone exposure and necrosis. Patient has diabetes and osteoporosis. What could be the possible cause?

- **Recalled answer:** Bisphosphonate-induced osteonecrosis (highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (Bartzela 2009, Bisphosphonates p23 and Discussion p24)
- Bartzela warns that long-term bisphosphonates (used for osteoporosis) can cause osteonecrosis, especially of maxillary and mandibular alveolar bone. The risk persists for years because of the drugs' very long half-life.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–98, no header, article-based (p16–25)

### Q23 · Promotion 2022

> Patient taking L-thyroxine:

- **Recalled answer:** b. decrease root resorption or increase bone resorption (annotation: 'both are true') · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** b. Decreased root resorption / increased bone resorption (not increased root resorption) (Bartzela 2009 p22 and p24 (thyroxine raises bone turnover and OTM rate); Weltman 2010 p463 and ref 82 (thyroid administration to reduce RR))

### Q24 · Promotion 2022

> Bisphosphonate:
>
> - a. increase root resorption

- **Recalled answer:** a. increase root resorption (marked 'not sure!!!') · ⚠️ **differs from the paper**
- **Per the paper:** Decreased rate of tooth movement (strong inhibition) and osteonecrosis risk; 'increases root resorption' is not supported (not stated in the paper) (Bartzela 2009, Bisphosphonates p23 and Discussion p24; Weltman 2010 p463 refs 75-78)

## Dental trauma an overview of its influence on the management of orthodontic treatment. Part 1

### Q29 · Promotion 2021

> Young patient with traumatic intruded tooth 3 mm with open apex. What to do?

- **Recalled answer:** Wait for spontaneous eruption · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Allow spontaneous re-eruption; reposition actively if there is no movement after about 2 weeks (Kindelan 2008, Intrusion injuries p75)
- Kindelan advises letting mild to moderate intrusions re-erupt spontaneously in patients up to 17 years. Severe intrusions and moderate intrusions with closed apices are repositioned promptly. A young open-apex tooth intruded 3 mm fits the 'wait' group.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–115, no header (p4–12)

### Q30 · Promotion 2021

> Figure of patient with increased overjet. What is the most appropriate reason to treat this case?

- **Recalled answer:** Prevent trauma (highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** To reduce the risk of incisor trauma (Kindelan 2008, Risk factors p69 and Prevention p69-70)
- Kindelan cites increased overjet and poor lip coverage as major trauma risk factors: 45% of upper incisors were damaged by age 12 with overjet >9 mm vs 23% below that. Note that Koroluk found early treatment did not significantly lower trauma incidence.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–98, no header, article-based (p16–25)

### Q32 · R2 exam 2019

> Prevalence of root resorption with clear aligner treatment (CAT) 56%; with fixed appliances 82%

### Q33 · Promotion 2021

> Patient came after gene therapy with fast tooth movement. What is most likely the gene?
>
> - RANKL
> - Runx

- **Per the paper:** RANKL (Burnheimer 2024, Introduction p2 (RANKL induced on the compression side drives osteoclastogenesis; Runx2 acts upstream by regulating RANKL/OPG); Bartzela 2009 p23 (vitamin D3 speeds OTM, possibly via RANKL))
- Burnheimer describes RANKL as the signal that turns on osteoclast formation in the PDL, with more RANKL under heavier force. Runx2 only regulates it upstream, so RANKL is the gene expected to speed tooth movement. The gene-therapy experiment itself is not in these papers (it is Proffit Ch 8 material, UNVERIFIED).
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–115, no header (p4–12)

### Q34 · Promotion 2021

> Which root formation will have the least amount of root resorption with movement?
>
> - Blunted
> - Dilacerated
> - Pointed

- **Per the paper:** Blunted (not stated in the paper) (Kindelan 2008 p71 (blunt or pipette-shaped roots resorb significantly more than normal roots); Weltman 2010 p472 (abnormal root shape a risk factor in observational studies only))
- The paper does not rank these three shapes. Kindelan (and Hamilton & Gutmann, t12_1) actually flag both blunt and pipette (pointed) roots as higher risk than normal roots. 'Blunted = least' comes from wider literature that links long, pipette and dilacerated roots to the most RR (UNVERIFIED), so the item is contested.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–115, no header (p4–12)
