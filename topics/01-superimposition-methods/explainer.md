## 🧩 The one question behind all three papers
A child comes in at 11 and again at 15, and the teeth have moved. **Did the braces move them, or did the face grow and carry them?** You can't tell from one X-ray, so you lay the two X-rays on top of each other and **line them up on a bone that did not change**. Whatever still looks different is real change. That lining-up is **superimposition**.

**The catch:** a growing face is not frozen. Some surfaces add bone, some lose bone, and whole jaws drift. Line up on a bone that moved and you hide real change or invent fake change. So the whole topic is one question: **which bones can you trust to stay still?**

**How they found out:** Björk put tiny **metal pins (implants)** into children's jaws. Pins don't grow, so lining up the pins shows the truth. Then you check which natural bones stayed lined up with the pins.

## 🦴 Paper 1 · Doppel et al. 1994: which upper-jaw spots stay still?
**What they did:** 50 children with three pins in the upper jaw, X-rays about **4 years apart** (11.9 → 16.0 years). They lined up the pins, then measured **how far each of 17 natural spots drifted away**. Small drift means trustworthy.

**How to read the colleague's table:** "mean displacement" = **how far the spot drifted**, in mm. Think of it as a trust score: **about 1 mm or less = good**; everything above that was rejected, even the palate borders at 1.05–1.14 mm.
- **Zygomatic process (cheekbone ridge), front and back: about 1 mm.** The winners. The back surface can **always** be traced and always moves the same way (backward, as bone is added). The front can be traced in 80 % of films and moves forward in some children (27.5 %), backward in others (35 %) and not at all in the rest (37.5 %).
- **Internal palate: 0.85 mm, the smallest number, but rejected.** It *looked* wrong when lined up by eye, so a good number alone wasn't enough.
- **ANS, PNS, A point, PTM: 1.3–2.4 mm.** Don't register on them: the front of the palate sinks, the back grows backward, and A point moves with the teeth.

**The rotation table:** the **palatal plane** (ANS–PNS line) tilted anywhere from **−5° to +8°** inside the maxilla. A line that tilts differently in every child **can't be your reference line**.

**The 1.5 : 1 rule:** as the child grows, the **eye-socket floor rises 1.5 mm for every 1 mm the palate sinks**. So when you line up on the cheekbone, let the orbit floor rise a bit more than the nasal floor falls (Björk and Skieller found 3 : 2, the same ratio).

## 🔀 Paper 2 · Nielsen 1989: three ways to line up the upper jaw
**What they did:** 18 children with pins, X-rays 4 years apart, lined up three ways:
1. **Best fit:** line up the palate outlines at ANS. The old, easy way.
2. **Implants:** the truth.
3. **Structural (Björk–Skieller):** line up on the front of the cheekbone ridge and let the orbit floor rise as much as the nose floor sinks.

**The result in one sentence:** best fit **hides about 30 % of molar eruption and 50 % of incisor eruption**, because the palate itself sinks and drags the reference down with it. The structural method matched the pins vertically.

**How to read the colleague's numbers table** (how far the teeth moved down in 4 years):

| | Molar cusp | Incisor edge |
|---|---|---|
| Implants (truth) | 4.75 mm | 3.39 mm |
| Best fit | 3.40 mm | 1.40 mm |
| Structural | 4.99 mm | 3.39 mm |

Best fit saw the incisor come down **1.40 mm**; really it came down **3.39 mm**. That missing 2 mm is the sinking palate. The paper does not say exactly how it got 30 % and 50 %. From its Table II, best fit misses about 28 % of the molar cusp's movement and about 59 % of the incisor edge's (derived).

## 🧊 Paper 3 · Nguyen et al. 2018: the lower jaw in 3D
**What they did:** Björk's pin studies can't really be repeated today (radiation-dose and ethics-board rules), so they used **bone plates** already placed for Class III treatment as the "pins". 20 growing children had CBCT scans 1 year apart. A computer lined up the two scans on the plates and screws (voxel-based registration), then measured how far each of three candidate spots had moved.
- **Chin front: 0.37 mm. Inside of the chin bone (symphysis): 0.40 mm.** Stable, under half a millimetre.
- **Wisdom-tooth pocket (third-molar crypt): 1.94 mm.** It moves, so don't use it.
- **In a separate group of **25 growing children** (20 Class II, 5 Class III), **two different people** each lined up the scans on chin + symphysis. Their results differed by only **0.12 mm on average**, and the **ICC was 0.998**, near perfect.

## 📖 What is what

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Superimposition / registration | Stacking two X-rays; the bone you line them up on is where you "register" |
| Implants (here) | Tiny metal marker pins in bone, not dental implants |
| Zygomatic process, key ridge | The curved cheekbone root seen above the upper first molar |
| Orbital floor / nasal floor | Bottom of the eye socket (gains bone, rises) / top of the palate (loses bone, sinks) |
| ANS / PNS / PTM / A point | Front tip of the upper jaw / back end of the palate / teardrop gap behind the maxilla / deepest point above the upper incisors |
| Symphysis | The chin bone where the two halves of the lower jaw join |
| CBCT, voxel | 3D X-ray; a voxel is a 3D pixel (0.3 mm here) |
| ICC | Agreement between two people; 1.0 = perfect |

## 🔎 Small points to know about the colleague's summary
- **"Posterior zygomatic process most stable":** the two cheekbone numbers are practically equal (0.97 vs 0.95 mm). Doppel does not rank one surface above the other: the abstract and conclusions recommend both. If you favour the back surface, the reason is that it can **always** be traced and always moves the same way, not its number (which is actually slightly larger).
- **"Infraorbital foramen visible in only 32 %":** that is what Doppel's text says, but the paper's own table lists it in 34 of 50 children (68 %). The paper contradicts itself.
- **Nielsen's implant molar value:** the colleague's numbers table gives 4.15 mm, but Nielsen's Table II prints **4.75 mm**. Use 4.75; with it, best fit misses about 28 % of the molar's movement.
- **"Björk and Skieller disputed this conclusion":** in the paper this sentence is about Moss and Greenberg's infraorbital-foramen method; in the summary it has lost that context.

## 🎯 If a consultant asks…
- **"Why not line up on the palate? It's right there."** Because the palate sinks as the child grows: Nielsen showed it hides 30–50 % of tooth eruption over 4 years.
- **"Where do you register the maxilla?"** On the front and back of the cheekbone ridge. Allow for the orbit floor rising about 1.5 times what the palate sinks (Doppel); Nielsen's version of Björk and Skieller's structural method set the two equal and slightly overstated eruption.
- **"And the mandible?"** On the chin and the inside of the symphysis; avoid the third-molar crypt (Nguyen: 0.37–0.40 mm vs 1.94 mm).
- **"What's weak about these studies?"** Small, selected samples (Doppel used 50 of about 700 implant records; Nielsen 18 of 35), treated or mixed samples (Nielsen mixed treated and untreated children and did not separate them), and in 3D only one year of growth.
