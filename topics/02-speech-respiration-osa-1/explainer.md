## 🧩 The one question behind all three papers
**Do the things we do with our mouths (talking, sucking, swallowing) shape the teeth, and do the teeth shape how we talk?**
The short answer: **much less than people think**, with one big exception. A force that acts **for many hours a day, for years**, like a thumb or a pacifier, or a tongue that *rests* forward, can move teeth. A force that lasts a second, like a swallow or a spoken sound, cannot.

## 🗣️ Paper 1 · Johnson and Sandy 1999: Do crooked teeth cause speech problems?
**What it is:** a review. The authors went through the main studies (no systematic search) and asked what they really show.

**The simple version:** the tongue is a brilliant adapter. Many people with crooked teeth speak perfectly well, because the tongue quietly finds another way to make each sound. So malocclusion and speech problems are **only loosely linked**.

**Where a link does show up:**
- **Anterior open bite and lisping.** If the front teeth don't meet, the tongue slips into the gap on /s/. This is the most consistent finding.
- **Class III**, a **big overjet** and **gaps between upper front teeth** also show some link.
- But **worse teeth do not mean worse speech**. A mild open bite can lisp and a severe one can sound normal.

**Does fixing the teeth fix the speech?** Sometimes it helps, but there is **no proof**. In one small study, orthodontics alone took patients from about 6 faulty sounds to about 1.5, yet the hardest sounds, /s/ /z/ /sh/, stayed wrong. Jaw surgery results are mixed too. So the rule is: **fix teeth for the teeth, and send speech problems to a speech therapist.**

**Bloomer's formula, the line to remember:** what matters is not the structure alone but whether the person **adapts**. Abnormal teeth with good adaptation give normal speech; normal teeth with poor function still give abnormal speech.

## 🍼 Paper 2 · Warren and Bishara 2002: Do pacifiers and thumbs damage the bite?
**What it is:** a real study. They followed **372 Iowa children from birth to age 5**, asked parents about feeding and habits every few months, then took models of the teeth.

**What they found, in plain words:**
- **Breast-feeding** for longer or shorter made **no difference** to the teeth, at least in children who hardly used a pacifier or thumb.
- **Pacifier for more than about 2 years:** more **posterior crossbite**. The lower arch gets wider and the upper doesn't keep up. With a pacifier for 4 years or more, about **4 in 10** children had a crossbite.
- **Thumb or finger beyond about 3–4 years:** more **open bite** and a **big overjet**. The upper incisors are pushed forward and the upper arch becomes narrower and deeper from front to back. With a thumb habit for 4 years or more, about **4 in 10** had an open bite and about **4 in 10** an overjet of 4 mm or more. The open bite is often **lopsided**, on the side of the hand the child uses.
- **Some of the damage stayed even after the habit stopped**, at least up to age 5.

**Why it matters:** the official advice at the time said not to worry until the early mixed dentition, around age 6. This paper says that is **too late**: the trouble starts after **2–3 years**.

**What is what:** *nutritive sucking* means feeding (breast or bottle); *nonnutritive sucking* means comfort sucking (pacifier, thumb, finger).

## 👅 Paper 3 · Proffit and Mason 1975: Should we treat tongue thrust?
**What it is:** a review by Proffit himself, using his own measurements of tongue and lip pressure.

**The simple version:** babies swallow with the **tongue pushed forward** between the gums. Adults swallow with the **teeth together** and the **tongue tip on the palate** behind the front teeth. Children switch over **somewhere between 2 and 12**. A child still pushing the tongue forward is usually **just late, not broken**.

**Why young children push the tongue forward:** the tongue is nearly full size by age 8 but the jaw is still small, and big tonsils and adenoids crowd the throat. The tongue comes forward to keep the **airway** open. At puberty the jaw grows, the tonsils shrink and there is more room, so the tongue moves back by itself. **Up to 80 %** of children with tongue thrust and open bite at 8 improve by 12 with no treatment.

**Does tongue thrust cause open bite?** Mostly **no**:
- About half of 6-year-olds tongue-thrust, but only about **6 %** of children have an open bite.
- Proffit measured the pressures, and swallowing pressure does not match tooth position: Australian Aborigines, with **bigger** arches, had **lower** tongue pressure than American whites. What matters is where the tongue **rests** all day. Teeth erupt with a force of only about **5 g**, so a tongue resting forward may stop the front teeth coming down (never shown directly).

**What to do:**
- Tongue thrust with no speech or tooth problem: **do nothing**.
- With a speech problem: **speech therapy**, not swallowing exercises.
- With a malocclusion: **fix the malocclusion first**; the thrust usually goes away.
- Still there at **puberty**: train the **resting** tongue position, **together with braces**.
- Never do swallowing exercises if the **airway is small**.

## 📖 What is what

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Articulation | Shaping sounds with the tongue, lips and teeth |
| Lisp (sigmatism) | Faulty /s/: *interdental* = tongue between the teeth; *lateral* = air escapes at the sides |
| Sibilants, fricatives | Hissing sounds made through a narrow gap: /s/ /z/ /sh/ /zh/ |
| Mesial occlusion | Class III; *postnormal* = Class II |
| Risk ratio ×3.4 | 3.4 times as likely as in someone without that trait |
| Myofunctional therapy | Exercises to retrain the tongue and lips |
| Deglutition | Swallowing; *infantile* or *visceral* swallow = the baby pattern |
| Faucial isthmus | The opening at the back of the mouth into the throat, narrowed by big tonsils |
| Equilibrium theory | Teeth sit where the forces around them balance; **how long** a force lasts matters more than how hard it is |

## 🎯 If a consultant asks…
- **"Should I refer a lisping child with open bite for orthodontics to fix the speech?"** Not for the speech alone. Johnson and Sandy found no proof that moving teeth fixes articulation; refer to a speech therapist.
- **"When should a thumb habit stop?"** By about 2–3 years, not the early mixed dentition. Warren and Bishara showed open bite and overjet climbing after 36 months, and the changes persisting at age 5.
- **"Is tongue thrust the cause of this open bite?"** Usually it is an adaptation or a delayed stage. Look at the resting tongue posture and the airway; Proffit found swallowing pressures do not explain tooth position.
- **"What is weak about these papers?"** Paper 1 is a narrative review of old, mostly cross-sectional studies; paper 2 relies on parents' reports and stops at age 5; paper 3 is a 1975 narrative review of the authors' pressure studies and clinical experience, with no trial testing its recommendations.
