# How an article-review session works and how to present one

A note on sources. Everything in this guide is general advice about presenting, drawn from how article-review sessions commonly run in orthodontic residency programmes; none of it comes from the three papers, and your own department may run things differently, so check with a senior resident. Where a number from Nielsen 1989, Doppel 1994 or Nguyen 2018 appears as a worked example, it is tagged with the paper and the table.

## The format (general advice)

The article, here three of them, is handed out a few days before. The resident presents for 20–25 minutes; the group then discusses for roughly as long again. Consultants may ask questions during or after, and some will interrupt to test whether you know where a number came from. Nobody expects a lecture. The presenter is usually the person who has most recently read the papers in detail, and the session is the group borrowing that reading; the consultants will know the background better than you, so present the papers, not the field. For a three-paper topic the job is comparison: say what each paper did, then what they agree on, where they differ, and what the set changes clinically. Three summaries in a row is the commonest weakness.

## What consultants tend to listen for (general advice)

Three questions, in this order.

1. Did you read the tables? The abstract says 30–50 %; the table says 1.33 mm of 4.75 mm at the molar cusp (Nielsen, Tables II and IV). A consultant may ask for the number the abstract does not contain, or for the sample size, or for the P value behind the claim you just made.
2. Can you judge the design? Who was selected and from how many (Doppel: 50 of about 700 implant records), what the reference standard was and what its error is, which direction the bias runs, and whether the sample looks like your patients.
3. Can you say what it changes at the chair or at the viewbox? One sentence per paper, with a number. Without that, the discussion tends to drift to "interesting paper" and move on.

A fourth, usually unspoken: can you hold a position when pushed, and admit the limit of what you know without bluffing.

## A 20–25-minute structure (general advice; the deck outline runs about 23.5 minutes)

| Minutes | Segment |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | Title; the question the topic answers, in one sentence |
| 1–4 | Why it matters; the gold standard |
| 4–7.5 | Paper 1: aim and method (90 s), results and take-home (120 s) |
| 7.5–11 | Paper 2, same shape |
| 11–14.5 | Paper 3, same shape |
| 14.5–18.5 | Agree, differ, stable-versus-unstable map |
| 18.5–20 | What it changes in our own records |
| 20–22 | Limitations; what the textbook says |
| 22–23.5 | Future; summary; your three questions for the room |

If you run long, cut the future slide and shorten limitations. Keep the summary. Keep a backup slide with the full tables; it is used only when someone asks.

## How to open and how to close (general advice)

Open with the question the topic answers and the single number that shows it matters, in two sentences, then the names of the papers. Avoid opening with the history of cephalometrics or with an apology. Close with five one-line take-aways, then hand the room three questions you would like their view on. Ending on your own questions tends to turn questioning into discussion and shows you thought past the papers.

## How to present a results table (general advice, with a worked example)

Three beats per number: say the number, say the direction, say the caveat.

Worked example, Nielsen Table III: "Implant minus best fit at A point is 2.24 mm vertically, P < .001 by the Table III footnote. Direction: best fit shows A point descending 0.47 mm, implants show 2.33 mm (Table I), so best fit under-reads the descent. Caveat: 18 children, four years of growth from age 10, treated and untreated mixed, so the size belongs to that interval and that age; and Table IV prints its asterisks with different thresholds, so name the table when you quote a P value."

Before any table, state the unit and the sign convention once (Nielsen: X anterior positive, Y downward negative, Fig 6; his difference tables print posterior and downward as positive). Pre-select two or three rows and point at them; avoid reading a whole table aloud. Put the SD next to the mean when the SD is larger than the effect, because a consultant may. Say "derived" when you computed a percentage yourself.

## A question you cannot answer (general advice)

Say so in one sentence, say what you do know that is adjacent, and offer to check. For example: "I do not have that number. The paper reports the maximum deviation, not the mean displacement, so it may not be in there. I will check Table II and the discussion and bring it next week." Do not invent a number and do not guess a P value. If a consultant challenges a number you quoted, go to the backup slide and show the row. If you think the consultant has misread, say "my reading of Table II is" and show it; do not argue past the table, and be ready to be wrong.

## Three common weaknesses (general advice)

1. Quoting the abstract without the table. Saying "30–50 %" and not being able to say 1.33 of 4.75 mm.
2. Reading paragraphs off slides. The room reads faster than you speak; a slide holds numbers and a caption, the sentences are yours.
3. No verdict. Ending with "this was a good study" instead of, for example, "the structural method showed no significant vertical bias and about 0.5 mm of horizontal bias against implants; the palatal method hid 30–50 % of eruption; here is why."

Close behind these: not knowing the sample size, and overrunning.

## Checklist (general advice)

Night before:
- Say the whole talk aloud once, timed; mark where you passed 10 and 20 minutes.
- For every number on a slide, know its table or section; write the source in the notes.
- Reread the abstract and the conclusions of each paper; note where they differ from the tables.
- Fill a one-page sheet: eight numbers you may be asked for, three questions you will ask, one verdict per paper.
- Check the Proffit chapter for anything you will attribute to the textbook; mark the rest UNVERIFIED and say so.
- Export the deck to PDF as a fallback and keep a copy where you can reach it.
- Get a normal night's sleep.

Morning of:
- Arrive early; test the projector, the clicker and the PDF fallback.
- Put the backup-table slide and your one-page sheet where you can reach them.
- Reread the three take-home slides, nothing else.
- No new slides, no last-minute reordering.
- Decide now what you will cut if time runs short.
