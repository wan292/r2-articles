# How an article-review session works and how to present one

A note on sources. Everything in this guide is general advice about presenting, drawn from how article-review sessions commonly run in orthodontic residency programmes; none of it comes from the three papers. Where a number from Nielsen 1989, Doppel 1994 or Nguyen 2018 appears as a worked example, it is tagged with the paper and the table.

## The format (general advice)

The article, here three of them, is handed out a few days before. The resident presents for 20–25 minutes; the group then discusses for roughly as long again. Consultants ask questions during or after, and a good one will interrupt to test whether you know where a number came from. Nobody expects a lecture. The presenter is the person in the room who has read the papers most closely, and the session is the group borrowing that reading. For a three-paper topic the job is comparison: say what each paper did, then what they agree on, where they differ, and what the set changes clinically. Three summaries in a row is the commonest failure.

## What consultants are actually listening for (general advice)

Three questions, in this order.

1. Did you read the tables? The abstract says 30–50 %; the table says 1.33 mm of 4.75 mm at the molar cusp (Nielsen, Tables II and IV). A consultant will ask for the number the abstract does not contain, or for the sample size, or for the P value behind the claim you just made.
2. Can you judge the design? Who was selected and from how many (Doppel: 50 of about 700 implant records), what the reference standard was and what its error is, which direction the bias runs, and whether the sample looks like your patients.
3. Can you say what it changes at the chair or at the viewbox? One sentence per paper, with a number. If you cannot, the room will decide the paper was interesting and move on.

A fourth, unspoken: can you hold a position when pushed, and admit the limit of what you know without bluffing.

## A 20-minute structure (general advice)

| Minutes | Segment |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | Title; the question the topic answers, in one sentence |
| 1–3 | Why it matters; the gold standard |
| 3–6 | Paper 1: aim and method (90 s), results and take-home (90 s) |
| 6–9 | Paper 2, same shape |
| 9–12 | Paper 3, same shape |
| 12–15 | Agree, differ, stable-versus-unstable map |
| 15–17 | What it changes in our own records |
| 17–19 | Limitations; what the textbook says |
| 19–20 | Summary; your three questions for the room |

If you run long, cut the future slide and shorten limitations. Never cut the summary. Keep a backup slide with the full tables; it is used only when someone asks.

## How to open and how to close (general advice)

Open with the question the topic answers and the single number that shows it matters, in two sentences, then the names of the papers. Do not open with the history of cephalometrics or with an apology. Close with five one-line take-aways, then hand the room three questions you would like their view on. Ending on your own questions turns an interrogation into a discussion and shows you thought past the papers.

## How to present a results table (general advice, with a worked example)

Three beats per number: say the number, say the direction, say the caveat.

Worked example, Nielsen Table III: “Implant minus best fit at A point is 2.24 mm vertically, P < .001. Direction: best fit shows A point descending 0.47 mm, implants show 2.33 mm, so best fit under-reads the descent. Caveat: 18 children, four years of pubertal growth, treated and untreated mixed, so the size belongs to that interval and that age.”

Before any table, state the unit and the sign convention once (Nielsen: X anterior positive, Y downward negative, Fig 6). Pre-select two or three rows and point at them; never read a whole table aloud. Put the SD next to the mean when the SD is larger than the effect, because a consultant will. Say “derived” when you computed a percentage yourself.

## A question you cannot answer (general advice)

Say so in one sentence, say what you do know that is adjacent, and offer to check. For example: “I do not have that number. The paper reports the maximum deviation, not the mean displacement, so it may not be in there. I will check Table II and the discussion and bring it next week.” Never invent a number and never guess a P value. If a consultant challenges a number you quoted, go to the backup slide and show the row. If you think the consultant has misread, say “my reading of Table II is” and show it; do not argue past the table.

## Three things that make residents look unprepared (general advice)

1. Quoting the abstract without the table. Saying “30–50 %” and not being able to say 1.33 of 4.75 mm.
2. Reading paragraphs off slides. The room reads faster than you speak; a slide holds numbers and a caption, the sentences are yours.
3. No verdict. Ending with “this was a good study” instead of “the structural method is valid to within 0.5 mm, the palatal method is not, and here is why.”

Close behind these: not knowing the sample size, and overrunning.

## Checklist (general advice)

Night before:
- Say the whole talk aloud once, timed; mark where you passed 10 and 20 minutes.
- For every number on a slide, know its table or section; write the source in the notes.
- Reread the abstract and the conclusions of each paper; note where they differ from the tables.
- Fill a one-page sheet: eight numbers you will be asked for, three questions you will ask, one verdict per paper.
- Check the Proffit chapter for anything you will attribute to the textbook; mark the rest UNVERIFIED and say so.
- Export the deck to PDF as a fallback; put it and the papers on a USB stick and in your e-mail.
- Sleep.

Morning of:
- Arrive 15 minutes early; test the projector, the clicker and the PDF fallback.
- Put the backup-table slide and your one-page sheet where you can reach them.
- Reread the three take-home slides, nothing else.
- Water, no new slides, no last-minute reordering.
- Decide now what you will cut if time runs short.
