## Obstructive sleep apnea and orthodontics

### Q1 · R2 exam 2019

> 62 yrs old, snoring, sleepy, history of stroke

- **Recalled answer:** CPAP · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** CPAP (positive airway pressure), once a sleep physician confirms OSA with polysomnography (AAO White Paper (Behrents 2019) p14 Prevalence (higher in post-stroke patients); p15 ICSD criteria (stroke listed); p17 'PAP therapy is the gold standard')
- The white paper says OSA is more common in post-stroke patients and lists stroke among the diagnostic co-morbidities. It names PAP as the gold-standard adult treatment. If the item asks for the next step rather than the treatment, the answer is referral for PSG.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q2 · R2 exam 2019

> 55 [yrs old] with crowding, complaining of daytime sleepiness, headaches, fatigue, …

- **Recalled answer:** do pharengeoscopy and polysomnography · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Refer to a physician (sleep medicine preferred) for polysomnography, the gold-standard diagnosis, before any orthodontic or OSA treatment (AAO White Paper p15 Diagnosis (in-center PSG gold standard); p17 'Diagnosis and treatment planning in adult OSA'; p26 Executive summary recs 2-3)
- Sleepiness, headaches and fatigue are OSA screening red flags. The paper says only a physician can diagnose OSA, by PSG or home testing. The PSG part of the recalled answer matches. Pharyngoscopy is not in the white paper, but Neelapu 2016 notes that endoscopy, not PSG, locates the site of obstruction.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q3 · R2 exam 2019

> Oral appliances with OSA

- **Recalled answer:** Improve airway patency · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** They hold the mandible and tongue forward, widening the oropharyngeal airway (improving airway patency). Used for mild-to-moderate OSA, or severe OSA when PAP is refused or not tolerated (AAO White Paper p18 'Oral appliance therapy')
- Mandibular advancing appliances work by holding the jaw and soft tissues forward, which enlarges the upper airway at the oropharynx. The paper gives their indications as mild-to-moderate OSA, or severe OSA in patients who cannot use PAP.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q4 · R2 exam 2019

> Picture: Mallampati score

- **Recalled answer:** class 2
- **Per the paper:** Modified Mallampati class II = soft palate, fauces and uvula visible, tonsillar pillars hidden (I = pillars also visible; III = soft palate and base of uvula only; IV = soft palate not visible) (AAO White Paper p16 'Clinical examination' (modified Mallampati, Appendix III))
- The paper defines the four modified Mallampati classes, with the patient protruding the tongue without making a sound. The exam picture cannot be checked here: class II is right only if the image showed the uvula but not the tonsillar pillars. The paper adds that the score should not be used alone to predict OSA.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list); figure-based

### Q5 · R2 exam 2019

> [Exam-content note, items 24-25] CPAP question (exact question not remembered); there is another question on the expansion related to it

- **Per the paper:** No single item recalled. Key facts: PAP (CPAP/BPAP/APAP) is the adult gold standard. In children, RME for true transverse deficiency may lower nasal resistance and enlarge the nasopharynx and nasal cavity. In adults, SARME may improve PSG values. Miniscrew-supported expansion (MARPE) has very limited PSG evidence (AAO White Paper p17 (PAP); p19 (SARME, miniscrew-supported RME); p23 (RME secondary effects; RME lowered AHI in mixed dentition, low-level evidence))
- The note only says there was a CPAP question and a linked expansion question. The white paper covers both. Expansion should be used only when a skeletal transverse problem exists. Prophylactic expansion has not been shown to prevent OSA.

### Q6 · Promotion 2021

> What is the first line of tx for OSA?

- **Recalled answer:** p13 handwritten: CPAP · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** CPAP (positive airway pressure therapy) in adults; in children, adenotonsillectomy is first line (AAO White Paper p17 (PAP gold standard, adults); p23 (tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy first line, children))
- The white paper calls PAP the gold-standard adult treatment and describes it as a pneumatic splint that keeps the airway open. Oral appliances and MMA come in when PAP is refused or fails. For children the first-line answer changes to T&A.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

### Q7 · Promotion 2021

> Polysomnography

- **Per the paper:** In-center overnight polysomnography is the gold-standard diagnostic test for OSA (adults and children). It is ordered and read by a sleep physician (AAO White Paper p15 Diagnosis (≥7 channels incl. EEG; HSAT 4-7 channels, no EEG); p20 pediatric PSG with CO2 monitoring; p21 HSAT not for under-18s)
- Attended PSG records at least 7 channels, including EEG, ECG, airflow, oximetry, respiratory effort and leg movement. Home testing uses fewer channels, has no EEG and underestimates events. In children, PSG is the only accepted test and now includes CO2 monitoring.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

### Q8 · Promotion 2021

> Pt with mandibular advancement appliance a long time – what will happen?

- **Recalled answer:** Flat curve of Spee · ⚠️ **differs from the paper**
- **Per the paper:** Progressive occlusal change: reduced overjet and overbite, anterior crossbite, posterior open bite and altered facial height (curve of Spee flattening is not stated in the paper) (AAO White Paper p19 'Change in occlusion')
- The paper lists the long-term side effects of oral appliances: less overjet and overbite, anterior crossbites, posterior open bite and facial-height change. It does not mention the curve of Spee. If any of the listed changes was an option, that is the paper-backed answer.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 Promotion Exam 2021

### Q9 · Promotion 2021

> Patient came with hyperdivergent mandible, sleep apnea, and adenoid obstruction. What's the appropriate treatment?

- **Recalled answer:** Adenoidectomy (written 'Adenectomy'; highlighted) · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Adenoidectomy / adenotonsillectomy, after referral to an otolaryngologist (AAO White Paper p23 (T&A first line); p26 Executive summary rec 4 (refer to ENT). Flores-Mir 2013 p274 and Table 1 (Zettergren-Wijk: near-normalisation 5 years after T/A))
- The white paper makes tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy the usual first-line treatment for pediatric OSA and tells orthodontists to refer adenotonsillar hypertrophy to ENT. The JADA review adds that clearing the obstruction can steer vertical growth back toward normal.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–98, no header, article-based (p16–25)

### Q10 · Promotion 2021

### Q11 · Promotion 2021

> 24-year-old male patient with class II div 1 with severely retruded mandible, sleep apnea, and small upper and lower airway. He also snores at night and can't sleep well, and he is very enthusiastic about treatment. What is the best treatment?
>
> - Mandibular advancement
> - Maxillary and mandibular surgery
> - CPAP
> - Mandibular positioning appliance

- **Recalled answer:** Mandibular advancement (highlighted) · ⚠️ **differs from the paper**
- **Per the paper:** Maxillary and mandibular surgery (maxillomandibular advancement, MMA) (AAO White Paper p19 'Maxillomandibular advancement and SARME'; p17 (PAP gold standard))
- Surgery for an OSA patient with a sagittal skeletal discrepancy and an orthodontic indication points to MMA in the paper, and the small upper and lower airway also points to both jaws. Isolated mandibular advancement is not the paper's OSA operation. The paper still names CPAP as the gold standard, so CPAP is the answer if the item asks for first-line treatment.
- *Source tag in the recall:* numbered list 1–98, no header, article-based (p16–25)

### Q12 · Promotion 2022

- **Recalled answer:** b. Adenotonsillectomy · ✅ matches

### Q14 · Promotion 2023

> Patient diagnosed with OSA, gold standard treatment is?

- **Recalled answer:** CPAP · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** CPAP (positive airway pressure therapy) (AAO White Paper p17 'Treatment of OSA in adults by physicians and surgeons')
- The white paper states that PAP is the gold-standard adult treatment, given as CPAP, BPAP or auto-titrating PAP. PAP non-adherence (use of 4 hours a night or less) is reported in 29%–83% of patients.

### Q15 · Promotion 2023

> Scenario of a patient asking for a MAD (mandibular advancement device); what is the appropriate action?

- **Recalled answer:** Refer for sleep study · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Do not make the device yet: refer to a physician (sleep medicine) for diagnosis by sleep study (PSG or home testing), then provide the MAD on the physician's referral (AAO White Paper p17 'Diagnosis and treatment planning in adult OSA'; p25 Legal issues; p26 Executive summary)
- The paper places OSA diagnosis outside the orthodontist's scope and advises against starting OSA treatment without a physician referral. After the device is fitted, the physician usually orders a sleep study with it in place to confirm the jaw position works.
- *Source tag in the recall:* OSA

### Q17 · Promotion 2024

> Male patient presented complaining of excessive daytime sleepiness and restlessness. It was revealed that his BMI is more than 50 and the AHI is 35. What is the severity of his OSA?
>
> - a) Mild
> - b) Moderate
> - c) Severe

- **Recalled answer:** c) Severe · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** c) Severe (AAO White Paper p15 'Severity' (mild AHI 5 to <15, moderate 15 to <30, severe ≥30))
- Adult severity is set by the AHI (or RDI) alone: 35 events per hour is above the 30 cut-off, so the OSA is severe. The BMI is a risk factor, not a grading criterion. Children use lower cut-offs (mild 1–5, moderate 5–10, severe ≥10).
- *Source tag in the recall:* Obstructive sleep apnea and orthodontics: An American Association of Orthodontists White Paper (Severity section: mild AHI 5-15, moderate 15-30, severe >=30)

### Q18 · Promotion 2024

> Case scenario about a patient having symptoms of OSA; asks what is the gold standard for diagnosis?
>
> - a) Polysomnography

- **Recalled answer:** a) Polysomnography · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** a) Polysomnography (in-center overnight PSG) (AAO White Paper p15 Diagnosis; p20 pediatric Diagnosis)
- The paper names in-lab overnight PSG, run by a sleep specialist, as the gold standard for diagnosing OSA in adults and the only confirmatory test in children. Neelapu 2016 agrees but notes that PSG cannot show where the airway blocks.

## Craniofacial morphological characteristics in children with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome

### Q21 · R2 exam 2019

> Child with bilateral crossbite, hyperdivergent mandible, headache, sleepiness, steep mandibular plane angle

- **Recalled answer:** OSA · ✅ matches
- **Per the paper:** Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSAS) (Flores-Mir 2013 JADA: Box 'Common reported findings' p273; Table 3 p275; symptoms p270. Also AAO white paper p20 (pediatric risk factors))
- The meta-analysis links pediatric OSAS to a steeper MP-SN (+4.20°), smaller SNB and larger ANB. Its box of common findings lists a narrow maxilla with posterior crossbites, a long lower face and a retrusive chin. Daytime sleepiness is a typical symptom, and the white paper also lists morning headaches.
- *Source tag in the recall:* R2 exam 2019 recall (typed list)

### Q22 · R2 exam 2019

> Prevalence of OSA in children: 1-2.2%

- **Per the paper:** 1.0%–2.2% of children (Flores-Mir 2013 JADA p270, opening paragraph)
- The JADA review quotes a pediatric OSAS prevalence of 1.0%–2.2%. The AAO white paper (p21) gives a wider 1%–4% range for OSA under varying criteria, and 1.5%–6% for habitual snoring.
- *Source tag in the recall:* Study notes: prevalence list
