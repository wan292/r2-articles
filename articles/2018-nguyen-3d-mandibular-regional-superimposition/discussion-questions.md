# Questions to ask about this paper

This is a Techno Bytes technique report: eight pages, one table, eight figures, two aims. The abstract carries two misprints (interobserver error 0.12 ± 1.1 mm, symphysis SD 0.15 mm), the only P values in aim 1 have no named test, and the whole-mandible reproducibility figures (< 0.25 mm, 75 % within 0.12 mm) come from one patient. Two of the authors (Cevidanes, Franchi) wrote the cranial-base voxel method the paper builds on, and the consultants in the room learned mandibular superimposition from Björk's 2D list, which this paper trims to two structures. Every number below is from the paper unless marked (literature), (Proffit) or (registry); "derived" means my own arithmetic from the printed values.

How to ask: name the Table or figure, keep the question to one breath, and frame anything methodological as "typo or design?" rather than as an accusation. The aim is to show you read past the abstract, not to catch anyone out.

## Tier 1 safe and sharp

### Q1. "The chin came out at 0.37 mm and the symphysis at 0.40 mm against a 0.3 mm voxel. Should we read 'stable' as 'about one voxel of residual error', and what is the smallest real remodelling this design could have detected in a year?"
- **Why it lands:** It puts the Table (p 749) and the Methods voxel size in one sentence. The Discussion says the chin and internal symphysis "have an accurate fit with 0.3 mm", but both tabulated values are above the voxel (derived: 1.23 and 1.33 voxels; derived 95 % CI 0.30–0.44 and 0.33–0.47 mm, n = 20 assumed). It shows you know the difference between sub-voxel and about-one-voxel, and it turns a precision number into a detection limit: a surface remodelling 0.3 mm a year would be invisible here.
- **What you will probably hear:** "One voxel is the floor; anything under it is noise, so 0.37 mm is as stable as the method can show." A radiology-minded attending may add that segmentation error, which the paper never measures, is part of that 0.37 mm.
- **Follow-up:** "Then is the crypt's 1.94 mm, derived 6.5 voxels and 5.2 times the chin, the only region we can actually call unstable, with the chin and symphysis 'not shown to move' rather than 'stable'?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Were the results within 0.3 mm?" The Table says no and the Discussion says yes; you would be inviting a correction instead of giving one.

### Q2. "The abstract gives the interobserver error as 0.12 ± 1.1 mm and the Results give 0.12 ± 0.11 mm; the symphysis SD is 0.15 in the text and 0.14 in the Table. Which do we quote?"
- **Why it lands:** It proves you read past the abstract. An SD of 1.1 mm around a mean absolute error of 0.12 mm is impossible for a non-negative quantity "whose range fell below" a 0.3 mm voxel (Results p 750); the abstract dropped a zero. The 0.14 versus 0.15 is a rounding difference (derived CV 35 % vs 37.5 %) that changes nothing, which is exactly why it is safe to raise.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Quote the Results and the Table; never cite from an abstract." Someone may add that exam questions are often written from abstracts, so know both values.
- **Follow-up:** "Fig 8 A is labelled 'Differences > 0.025 mm' while the caption and text say 0.25 mm. Is 0.25 mm the threshold we should carry?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The paper is full of typos." It has three that matter (1.1, 0.15, 0.025); name them and move on.

### Q3. "Björk, and the ABO workshop list, accept the third-molar crypt before root formation. Here it erred 1.94 mm. Were these crypts still pre-root at 12 years, or is the crypt a growth front that moves with the ramus?"
- **Why it lands:** The Introduction quotes Björk's condition, "before root development", and the Methods never state the crypt stage of the 20 subjects (T1 12.1 ± 1.3 y). The germ sits at the ramus–body junction, which the Discussion itself describes as resorbing anteriorly and depositing posteriorly (Fig 6), so a stable crypt would be the surprise. Fig 5 shows the error is vertical and the Discussion says it was worst "especially when the subjects exhibited vertical growth", with no data behind that sentence.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Björk used the crypt in 2D when the symphysis was unclear; in a voxel fit you do not need it." Someone will quote Ruellas 2016 as the paper does (16 of 18 registrations failed with crypts); the research notes for this topic report Ruellas's own count as 14 of 16 Björk-mask failures with the crypt named as one of several possible causes (literature).
- **Follow-up:** "Krarup's Apert children, the paper's own reference for the canal, had tooth buds that stayed stable before root formation (literature, as summarised in the topic notes). Does the crypt fail here because of age, or because a voxel registration weights a short curved contour badly?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Björk was wrong about the crypt." Björk's condition was pre-root and his method was 2D tracing by eye; the paper tests neither.

### Q4. "Are the five Class III bone-anchor patients in the reliability sample five of the twenty from aim 1, and did the plates sit inside the chin-plus-symphysis field the two observers registered on?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods p 749–750 never say. The plates at the lateral-incisor/canine apices lie between region (1), the anterior chin from pogonion to B point and laterally to the lateral incisors, and region (2), the inner symphyseal cortex up to the mental foramina. Metal inside the registration field means streak artefact and voxels that are identical at T1 and T2 by construction, which would flatter the 0.12 mm interobserver figure in 5 of 25 cases.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Probably yes, and the plates were segmented out," or "a plate with identical intensity at T1 and T2 is an anchor for an intensity-based registration, not a problem." Either answer tells you something the paper does not.
- **Follow-up:** "If they overlap, would the interobserver error in the 20 Class II patients alone be the number to quote?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The samples overlap, so the study is confounded." You do not know they overlap; that is the question.

## Tier 2 mechanics / biology of remodeling

### Q5. "Fig 4 D shows about 2 mm of deposition near menton, a few millimetres from a chin that fitted within 0.37 mm. What is the biology that lets the lower border grow while pogonion stays put?"
- **Why it lands:** It takes the paper's own anti-proximity argument (Discussion p 753) and asks for the mechanism. Björk and Skieller (refs 4–5, as cited) and Enlow and Harris (ref 26) describe periosteal apposition along the inferior symphysis and lower border; Proffit's remodelling map calls the chin "almost inactive" (Proffit Ch 2, Fig 2.31, study pages) and the lower-border apposition is part of the surface remodelling that masks internal rotation (Proffit Ch 4 [6th p88]). The paper shows the pattern (Fig 4 D, Fig 6) without explaining it.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The chin is dense cortical bone that has finished its forward drift; the lower border is where the mandible adds height and where matrix rotation is absorbed."
- **Follow-up:** "The colour bar in Fig 4 ends at +2.00 mm and the red is saturated, so is 2 mm a minimum? And the caption says that map is the T2-to-T1 symphysis registration, not the plate registration the Discussion attributes it to."
- **Avoid saying:** "Deposition at menton proves the plates did not move." It argues against proximity bias; it does not validate the plates.

### Q6. "The chin region runs from pogonion up to B point. Proffit's remodelling map shows the alveolar area above the chin as resorptive. Did the upper part of the region fit as well as pogonion, or does pogonion carry the fit?"
- **Why it lands:** It sets the paper's own region definition (Methods p 749) against the growth chapters (Proffit Ch 2, study pages: anterior chin stable, area just above it resorptive; wording UNVERIFIED against the book). The paper reports one mean per region; the Fig 3 colour map would show whether the error is uniform, and the Discussion claims "little or no remodeling" at pogonion, not at B point. In Class III elastic patients the lower incisors and B point are under load.
- **What you will probably hear:** "B point is alveolar and follows the incisors; the fit is dominated by the cortical chin and a voxel registration averages small edge error." Some will say the region should stop at mid-chin for incisor-moving mechanics.
- **Follow-up:** "For a Herbst patient, where the lower incisors procline, would you trim the chin region below B point?"
- **Avoid saying:** "B point is unstable, so the chin region is wrong." The region's mean was 0.37 mm; ask about its distribution.

### Q7. "The canal was excluded a priori as changing with growth, citing Björk and Krarup, yet Björk's 2D list and the ABO still use it. Is the canal unstable in the sagittal view, or mainly transversely?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods p 749 give the exclusion in one sentence. Krarup 2005 (literature, as summarised in the topic notes) found the canal kept its profile curvature but relocated laterally in all 10 children by buccal resorption and lingual apposition; Chen 2020 (literature) found the distance from the inner symphysis to the mandibular foramen changed 3.0–3.4 mm over 4.6 y. So the canal can be a usable 2D tracing line and a useless 3D voxel region at the same time. The question separates dimensionality from biology.
- **What you will probably hear:** "In 2D you align the smooth lower border of the canal by eye; in 3D the fit would chase a tube that is moving laterally and lengthening posteriorly."
- **Follow-up:** "Should the ABO's 2D list change, or is the 2D canal fine as long as you alternate between symphysis and canal until both fit, as the workshop deck says (literature)?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why did they leave the canal out?" The Methods say why.

### Q8. "On a chin-and-symphysis registration, Fig 6 shows the anterior ramus resorbing and the posterior ramus depositing. In a Herbst patient, how would you tell ramus remodelling from the appliance effect, and how does a growth rotation show up on this superimposition?"
- **Why it lands:** A regional superimposition shows everything else moving relative to the stable block, so condyle and ramus change is growth plus remodelling plus treatment, all in one colour map. Björk's internal rotation (Proffit Ch 4 [6th p88]: up to 10–15° internal, 2–4° in the mandibular-plane angle; about 25 % around the condyle and 75 % within the body) would appear as the condyle and ramus tipping relative to the symphysis block. The paper's only growth-pattern remark is that crypt error was worst in vertical growers, with no data.
- **What you will probably hear:** "You cannot separate them in one patient; you need an untreated comparison, which this paper did not have. Rotation shows as the ramus moving relative to the symphysis; Björk chose the symphysis because it sits inside the core that rotates as one piece."
- **Follow-up:** "Is 12.6 months at CVM 2–5 long enough for rotation to show, and would a 4-year interval like Doppel's and Nielsen's keep the chin at 0.37 mm?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does the Herbst invalidate the chin?" Chin stability was tested only in Class III patients; the Herbst patients were used for reproducibility, which cannot test stability.

## Tier 3 methodology

### Q9. "The Table's P values are 0.765, 0.698 and 0.0025, with an SD of 0.06 mm on 1.94 mm for the crypt. What test is that, and against what null: zero, the voxel, or the chin?"
- **Why it lands:** No test is named and the footnote only says "statistically significant". Derived with n = 20: a one-sample t of 1.94 ± 0.06 mm against zero gives t ≈ 145 (P < 10⁻²⁸), against the 0.3 mm voxel t ≈ 122; the chin against zero gives P < 10⁻⁸, not 0.765; against the voxel P ≈ 0.07 (chin) and 0.005 (symphysis); Welch crypt vs chin P ≈ 10⁻²⁴. Nothing printed matches any of these. One reading that fits all three values is that they are the Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality P values, which the Results cite in the next sentence (derived hypothesis, not stated by the paper). The crypt's CV of 3 % beside 43 % for the chin, in a result the Discussion says varied with growth pattern, is the giveaway.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Probably the normality test, or the 0.06 is a standard error." Most will concede that the stability conclusion rests on effect size, about one voxel versus six, which is in fact the stronger argument.
- **Follow-up:** "If the asterisk means 'non-normal' rather than 'unstable', should crypt versus chin have been tested at all, and with what?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The statistics are wrong." Say "typo or test?" and give the arithmetic.

### Q10. "The ICC of 0.998 needs two measurements of one variable per subject. Which variable was correlated: a per-patient mean distance, landmark coordinates, or the registration parameters?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods p 750 report the ICC inside the sample-size paragraph and never define the variable. The only interobserver outcome described is the surface distance between the two observers' registrations, one number per subject, which cannot be fed into a two-rater ICC. Related and derived: the stated inputs (ρ0 0.75, ρ1 0.95, α .05, power .80, 2 raters) give about 10–12 subjects by the Walter, Eliasziw and Donner 1998 formula (literature; method used by the paper UNVERIFIED), not 25; the calculation returns exactly the number of patients available.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Probably mean displacement per subject per examiner," or an honest "not sure." Someone will notice the ICC lives in the Methods, not the Results.
- **Follow-up:** "With the CI upper bound at 1.000, is the ICC at the ceiling of what two registrations started from the same observer-1-oriented T1 can show?"
- **Avoid saying:** both the ICC question and the sample-size question in one breath. Ask the ICC variable first; raise the sample size only if they engage.

### Q11. "The 'under 0.25 mm over the whole mandible, 75 % within 0.12 mm' figures are from one subject in Figs 7 and 8, and the Results say the range of the 0.12 ± 0.11 fell below the voxel without printing a range. Is there a group whole-surface figure?"
- **Why it lands:** The Discussion (p 753) says "for this subject"; the abstract and Conclusions generalise. Derived: mean + 2 SD = 0.34 mm, above 0.3 mm, so the "range fell below" claim is not checkable. Fig 8 B is labelled "> 0.12 mm" while its caption says "less than 0.12 mm"; the text's logic (75 % within 0.12 mm shown green) fits the label, so the caption is the error.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The group number is the 0.12 ± 0.11; the figures are illustrations." That is the honest version and it is fine.
- **Follow-up:** "Where was the 25 % above 0.12 mm, the condyles and coronoid as Fig 8 B suggests, and is that exactly where a Herbst effect would be read?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The figures are mislabelled, so the result is unreliable." The group figure stands; the single-case figures are decoration.

## Tier 4 Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "For the exam, when asked for the structures for mandibular superimposition: Björk's four, chin, inner symphyseal cortex, canal, crypt before roots, as the ABO lists them, or this paper's two? How do you want it answered?"
- **Why it lands:** It is exam-relevant and open, and it shows you hold both lists. Proffit's figure legend gives mandibular superimposition "on the symphysis" (Proffit Ch 6, Fig 6.60, study pages); whether Proffit prints Björk's four by name is UNVERIFIED; the ABO deck lists all four plus the anterior-inferior symphysis (literature). Nguyen confirms two (0.37 and 0.40 mm), rejects the crypt (1.94 mm) and excludes the canal a priori.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Give Björk's list first and say it is the 2D structural method; then say that 3D voxel registration keeps the chin and inner symphysis and drops the crypt and canal, citing this paper." Some attendings want only Björk's answer in a written exam.
- **Follow-up:** "For an ABO-style case, the 2D structural method is still required; should the Ricketts corpus axis be mentioned at all?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is Proffit out of date?" Proffit is the exam; the paper is a refinement for 3D.

### Q13. "Proffit lists four evidence-based CBCT indications and growth monitoring is not one of them. Where does a method like this belong clinically: research, or selected patients like the bone-anchored Class III cases who are scanned anyway?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit Ch 6 (study pages) gives the four indications (impacted or ectopic teeth; asymmetry and complex surgery; congenital deformities, syndromes and trauma; hard-tissue TMJ) and the doses: cephalogram 0.005 mSv, limited-field CBCT 0.05, both jaws 0.1, jaws plus cranium 0.15 (Proffit, study pages; check the chapter before quoting). Nguyen's scans were "derived from previous research and clinical databases" and the paper invokes ALARA only to explain why tantalum implants are impossible.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Research and selected cases; the clinical standard is still the cephalogram; 3D superimposition is for patients who already need CBCT." Someone may argue that if the scans exist, the superimposition is free.
- **Follow-up:** "If we have a T1 CBCT for an impacted canine and only a T2 cephalogram, is there an honest way to superimpose them?" (Cevidanes 2006, ref 18, describes CBCT-simulated cephalograms; literature.)
- **Avoid saying:** "Should we start taking CBCTs for growth?" The answer is no and the question marks you as not having read Proffit's chapter.

### Q14. "Our last article superimposed the mandible on the Ricketts corpus axis at Pm. Pm sits inside this paper's stable chin region, but Xi sits in ramus bone that Fig 6 shows remodelling. Is a two-point axis acceptable when the registration point is stable and the orientation point is not?"
- **Why it lands:** It links the session to the registry (Khaleel 2026: Xi–Pm registered at Pm; the authors concede Xi drifts posteriorly and superiorly with growth). Springate and Jones 1998 (registry, VERIFIED in the Khaleel files) found corpus-axis errors against implants usually under 2 mm but 4 mm or more in about 10 % of children at molars and incisors, with Björk's structural method preferred. Over Khaleel's 44–94 d intervals the rotation is probably under 0.2° (derived, UNVERIFIED), so this is a question of principle, not an attack.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Pm is better than the lower border; the orientation through Xi is the weakness; for a 2-month interval it hardly matters; for growth studies use Björk." Some will defend Ricketts on reproducibility grounds.
- **Follow-up:** "Is the 3D equivalent a best fit of thousands of chin and symphysis voxels, which gives orientation and registration from the same stable block?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Khaleel used the wrong superimposition." Someone in the room may have reviewed it; keep it to methods.

## Do not ask these

1. "Why did you not use tantalum implants?" The Discussion (p 753) answers it: ALARA and institutional review boards make Björk's method "difficult if not impossible" to repeat.
2. "Why was the mandibular canal left out?" Methods p 749 answer it with two references (Björk 1963; Krarup 2005). Ask Q7 instead, which is about what kind of instability.
3. "Is 0.12 mm interobserver error good?" The answer is yes and it tells the room nothing; ask what the whole-surface figures are (Q11).
4. "Is it ethical to take serial CBCTs in children for this?" The scans came from existing research and clinical databases under IRB 12-1496; the question reads as an accusation and derails into radiation policy. Q13 covers the same ground as a clinical question.
5. "Which software is better, Dolphin or Slicer?" Not in the paper; Bazina 2018 (literature) found ICC 0.96 and differences under 0.21 mm between them. It turns a methods discussion into product talk.

## Questions they may fire back at you

1. **"What were Björk's stable mandibular structures, and which survived here?"** Björk, as the Introduction cites him: the inner cortex of the inferior symphyseal border, the anterior chin above pogonion, the symphyseal trabecular pattern with the canal, and the inferior contour of the third-molar germ before root development. Nguyen confirmed the chin (0.37 ± 0.16 mm) and inner symphysis (0.40 ± 0.14 mm) at about one voxel, found the crypt unstable (1.94 ± 0.06 mm, P = .0025, test unnamed) and excluded the canal a priori because it changes with growth (Krarup 2005). So two of four survive in 3D voxel work.

2. **"Why absolute mean error rather than root mean square?"** The Methods say absolute mean surface error averages the magnitude of error over the whole region without direction, whereas RMS weights large errors and is skewed by outliers; the absolute mean is "more stringent" for small errors. Neither carries direction, which is why the signed colour maps (Fig 4, Fig 7, scale −2.00 to +2.00 mm) are needed to call something apposition or resorption, and the paper never states which sign is outward.

3. **"What does the voxel size do to these numbers?"** The voxel is 0.3 mm on both scanners. Derived: the chin is 1.2 voxels, the symphysis 1.3, the crypt 6.5, and the interobserver error 0.4 voxel. Nothing below a voxel can be resolved, so only the interobserver figure is genuinely sub-voxel; the stability figures are at the resolution limit, and a region remodelling less than about 0.3 mm a year could not have been detected.

4. **"How do you know the plates did not move?"** You do not. The Methods say they were "confirmed to be immobile" clinically at T1 and T2; there is no radiographic check and no plate-on-plate residual error, and the plates were loaded by Class III elastics for about a year. Björk checked his pins radiographically over years. The paper's internal evidence is indirect: about 2 mm of deposition at menton close to the plates (Fig 4 D) and smaller error at the antegonial notch than at the crypt (Fig 5), both single-subject colour maps.

5. **"Why did the crypt fail?"** The crypt erred 1.94 ± 0.06 mm against 0.37 mm for the chin, derived 5.2 times worse, and the Discussion says the error was vertical and worst in vertical growers (no data). The germ sits at the ramus–body junction where Fig 6 shows anterior ramus resorption, and it moves with eruption; the paper never states whether the crypts were pre-root, which was Björk's condition. Ruellas 2016 reported failures when crypts were included (16 of 18 as the paper cites it).

6. **"How does this sit with the two 2D papers in this session?"** Doppel 1994 put 2D tracing error at 0.5 mm (duplicate tracings ± 0.25 mm) and the best maxillary structures at 0.95–0.97 mm from implants (Table II); Nielsen 1989 showed a palatal best fit hides 30–50 % of eruption over 4 y and the structural method's interobserver differences ran 0.5–0.9 mm vertically at ANS and the incisors (Tables III–IV). Nguyen's 0.12 ± 0.11 mm between observers is about a quarter of the 2D tracing error, and the regional stability of 0.37–0.40 mm is per year, not per four years. The shared lesson is that the reference region matters more than the software: the crypt in 3D fails for the same reason the palate fails in 2D.

## Ten-second summary

Twenty Class III bone-anchor patients, plates as regional implants, 0.3 mm voxels: chin 0.37 ± 0.16 mm and inner symphysis 0.40 ± 0.14 mm are at the resolution limit, the third-molar crypt at 1.94 mm is not, and the canal was never tested. Two observers on chin plus symphysis in 25 growing patients agreed to 0.12 ± 0.11 mm, ICC 0.998. Björk's four 2D structures become two in 3D; the gold standard was checked clinically only, everyone was under treatment, and the interval was one year.
