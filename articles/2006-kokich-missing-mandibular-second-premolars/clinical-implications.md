# Clinical implications — Kokich & Kokich 2006

Kokich VG, Kokich VO. Congenitally missing mandibular second premolars: clinical options. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2006;130(4):437-44. This is a Clinician's Corner with no data of its own: five selected patients (Figs 1-5) illustrate a framework for deciding whether to close or keep the space of a missing lower second premolar, and, if the space is kept, how to protect the ridge until an implant can go in. Every clinical message below is therefore expert opinion (Oxford CEBM level 5), checked against the cited and later evidence where marked.

On this page, "the paper says" means printed in Kokich & Kokich 2006, with the journal page. "The literature says" means another source checked in this run's research pass, marked VERIFIED (abstract or primary source read) or UNVERIFIED. "Proffit (notes)" means the user's Proffit study pages, not the printed book (chapters 1-17 are numbered the same in the 6th and 7th editions). "My read" is my interpretation. "E" is the deciduous (primary) second molar.

## What this changes or confirms at the chair

1. **Profile and crowding decide open or closed, not the missing tooth itself.** The paper says: close the space when the opposing arch is crowded or the profile is protrusive; with no crowding and a normal profile, closure "could produce an undesirable facial profile", so either keep the space or close it with extra anchorage (p 443). The literature says: extracting the E in children retroclined the lower incisors 4.5° and retruded them 1.8 mm, with overjet +1.2 mm and overbite +1.5 mm (Nawaia 2025, split-mouth RCT plus 29 untreated controls; VERIFIED). Proffit (notes, Ch 12): with one missing 5, a restoration is usually indicated unless there is true space loss or crowding on the other side. Hub link: in `2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction` the upper lip fell a further 1.5-1.6 mm behind the E-line from about 15 to 30 y in both groups, so a profile that is flat at debond flattens further. My read: write the profile (lips vs chin) and the crowding in each arch on the problem list before deciding anything about the space.

2. **A healthy E is the best space maintainer for a future implant: keep it, narrow it and cover it.** The paper says (Patient 1; pp 437, 441-442):
   - Patient 1's E was 13 mm wide against 7.5 mm for an average lower 5, so it was narrowed to premolar size.
   - Measure the E's width at the CEJ on a bitewing, mark it on the occlusal surface, and cut a wafer of enamel and dentin off the mesial and distal with a carbide fissure or diamond bur: about 2 mm per side, leaving about 7-8 mm, without reaching the pulp.
   - Cover the exposed dentin with light-cured composite and build up the short crown so the opposing tooth cannot supererupt; then close the spaces.

   Caveat: 13 − 2 × 2 = 9 mm, not 7-8 mm (derived). The paper's guide cuts the crown down to its own CEJ width, so the amount per side is (crown width − CEJ width) ÷ 2 (derived). Reaching premolar size takes (E width − premolar width) ÷ 2: about 1 mm for an average E that is 2 mm wider than the 5 (Proffit notes, Ch 3) and 2.75 mm for Patient 1 (both derived). The literature says a retained healthy E lasts: Bjerklin & Bennett 2000 lost 7 of 59 Es by a mean age of 20 y 6 mo and none after 20 y; Sletten 2003 found 24 of 28 (86 %) still functioning in adults after a mean 12.4 y (both VERIFIED). Hvaring 2014: infraocclusion was judged a more critical prognostic factor for a retained E than root resorption (VERIFIED).

3. **Expect the reduced E's roots to resorb, and decide whether that is good or bad for this patient.** The paper says: the diverging roots resorb as the 4 and 6 reach them and bone replaces them, an "ideal" way to prepare an implant site (p 442). Proffit (notes, Ch 12) lists the same resorption as a risk of reducing the E. My read: good if the E is a placeholder until growth stops; bad if the plan is to keep the E for decades. Take a periapical during space closure and test vitality, which the paper does not report.

4. **Diagnose ankylosis from the bone level, not from infraocclusion alone.** The paper says: the most reliable sign is the interdental bone between the E and the 6 and 4: flat means the teeth are erupting together; oblique, lower around the E, confirms ankylosis (p 443; Fig 1B vs Fig 2B, ref 1). Patient 1's E was "submerged" but had flat bone and was kept (p 437). The literature says: not all infraoccluded primary molars are ankylosed (Otsugu 2026 SR, VERIFIED). Caveat: no accuracy study supports the bone-level sign; it comes from the authors' own review.

5. **An ankylosed E in a child with significant growth left comes out early; with little growth left and slight submergence it can stay.** The paper says: with growth ahead, the E submerges, the ridge could be compromised vertically and need a graft, and vertical grafting is "often unpredictable" and expensive (refs 10-11); so extract it while the face is still growing (pp 442-443). Proffit (notes, Ch 12): the same early extraction with no space maintainer, or **decoronation**, "increasingly indicated" when the growth spurt is still ahead. The paper never mentions decoronation. Exam link: a 2020 promotion recall (ankylosed lower E, no successor, mild crowding) is keyed "decoronation" on the Ch 12 study page and "extraction" on the high-yield page; the paper supports extraction while the face is growing (see the last section).

6. **After extracting the E, do not place a space maintainer; let the 4 and 6 come together and push them apart later.** The paper says: in most cases, especially when implants are planned, no space maintainer; the 6 and 4 erupt together, bringing bone and tissue up (Patient 2's vertical defect disappeared, Fig 2D); separating them later leaves bone "that equals the width of the premolar and molar": orthodontic implant-site development (pp 437-438, 443). Caveat: the paper says this could require longer treatment. Proffit (notes, Ch 12) agrees: the drifting 6 "brings bone with it". The literature says: the edentulous ridge loses 25 % of its width within 3 y of E extraction (Ostler & Kokich 1994, VERIFIED), and spontaneous drift is partial (80 % of the lower space closed at 4 y, residual 2 mm, with a midline shift; Mamopoulou 1996, VERIFIED).

7. **If the ridge has already collapsed, move a tooth through it before reaching for a graft.** The paper says: in Patient 5 (14 y 6 mo) the ankylosed E was extracted and the ridge narrowed vertically and buccolingually; the 4 was pushed distally into the 5 position, and the implant went into the old 4 site at 17 y without a graft (pp 440, 443; Fig 5). The literature says: distalising the 4 into the 5 site increased ridge width by 28.5 % and height by 1.1 mm, with only −4.2 % width at 2-5 y (Eliášová 2014, 71 sites, VERIFIED). The paper's own support is indirect: in Hom & Turley 1984 (ref 13), closing lower-6 spaces in 14 adults widened the ridge 1.2 mm but most lost about 1.3 mm of crestal bone (VERIFIED). Proffit (notes, 6th Ch 19 / 7th Ch 20): closing into an old narrowed ridge is slow, about 3 y. Hub link: unlike the labial movement out of the ridge that `2004-kokich-impacted-maxillary-canines` warns against, this is mesiodistal translation inside the alveolus.

8. **Place the implant when serial cephalograms show vertical growth has stopped, not at a fixed age.** The paper says: the timing is the end of vertical facial growth, judged on serial cephs (end of ramus growth); on average about 17 y in girls and about 21 y in boys (citing Fudalej); Patient 5's implant went in at 17 y after superimpositions showed growth had finished (pp 440-441). The literature says: Fudalej's published abstract (142 males, 159 females) reports growth continuing after puberty and becoming clinically insignificant after the second decade, without the 17 y / 21 y cut-offs (VERIFIED; cut-offs UNVERIFIED). Proffit (notes): serial cephs are the most accurate test (Ch 6); an implant placed too soon behaves like an ankylosed tooth and appears to intrude (6th Ch 19 / 7th Ch 20). Hub link: best-fit maxillary superimposition under-reads vertical change by about 30-50 % (`1989-nielsen-maxillary-superimposition-three-methods`), so use structural registration when deciding that growth has stopped. My read: take two cephs at least a year apart from about 17 y in girls and 21 y in boys and superimpose them properly. Small steps beside implants have been reported even in adults, especially in long- or short-face patients (Bernard 2004, Heij 2006; UNVERIFIED, from the Q&A sheet), so warn that a crown may one day need remaking.

9. **Expect a lingually placed implant if the ridge was left edentulous for years, and tell the restorative dentist.** The paper says: the ridge narrows 25 % in 4 y and 30 % by 7 y but still takes an implant; it resorbs more facially, so the implant sits lingually and the cusp loading of the crown must be adjusted to avoid abutment or crown fracture (p 441, refs 7-8). The literature says the source reported 25 % within 3 y, then 4 % over the next 3 y; the facial-lingual difference is not in its abstract (VERIFIED; facial detail UNVERIFIED).

10. **In an adult missing the 5 and 6, one implant can be both the restoration and the anchor.** The paper says (Patient 3; pp 438-439, 443): when drift leaves a space too large for one tooth and too small for two, set the implant position on a diagnostic wax-up, restore it with a provisional crown, bond a bracket, and use it to bring the lower 7 forward from Class II to Class I without moving the anterior teeth; one restoration instead of two. Caveats:
    - The upper 7 was left without an antagonist; if it supererupts, extract it or place an implant distal to the lower 7 (p 438).
    - The Discussion says the implant goes in "before orthodontic treatment", but Patient 3 was aligned first (p 438). My read: place it after initial alignment, before space closure.
    - The caption calls it a "first premolar position" implant; the text and Fig 3E/H show the second-premolar site (see `conflicts.md`).

11. **Do not cite this paper as proof that orthodontic force helps an implant integrate.** The paper says: early or immediate loading is possible, especially in orthodontic patients; a continuous one-direction load "stimulates bone formation" (p 443, ref 18). The literature says: ref 18 is a 14-day rabbit tibia study (10 animals) in which static load looked the same as no load and excessive dynamic load caused crater defects (Duyck 2001, VERIFIED). It shows no harm from static load, not stimulation. Patient 3's provisional crown went on "after integration" (p 438). My read: follow the implant surgeon's integration time before loading with orthodontic force.

12. **To close without changing a normal profile, the molars must come forward: that needs anchorage and time.** The paper says: Patient 4 (13 y 8 mo, Class II, 5-mm overjet, minor arch-length deficiency in both arches, three missing 5s) had an ideal profile, so closing all spaces conventionally would have over-retracted the lips; a chincup and elastics slid the 6s forward on a continuous archwire over 4 years, to Class I molars, with no restorations and the lower incisors held (pp 439-440; Fig 4). Today: miniscrews or mini-implants (p 443, ref 20). Caveats:
    - Proffit (notes, Ch 13): a chincup mainly rotates the mandible down and back; the vertical effect in Patient 4 is not reported.
    - How the 5-mm overjet was reduced, and what the upper incisors did, are not reported: Fig 4F reports only the lower incisors.

13. **Hemisection is an option for a child seen early, not the standard.** The paper says: hemisect the E early so the 6 erupts mesially without moving the lower incisors; "especially attractive" if the child is seen early and monitored (pp 443-444, refs 21-22). The literature says: Northway 2004 found less retraction of the upper incisor and lips than with four-premolar extraction (VERIFIED); a split-mouth RCT in 40 children found residual space 2.04 ± 1.67 mm vs 2.39 ± 1.86 mm after plain extraction (P = .053), no angulation difference, more complications (P = .0176) and higher costs, and advised against it (Abdul Jabbar 2025, VERIFIED); hemisection was also recalled as more painful (Naoumova 2026, P < .001, VERIFIED). Proffit (notes, Ch 12) adds pulp therapy for the retained half.

14. **The implant is the default replacement, but not the only one.** The paper says: a full-coverage bridge in a young patient risks the pulp (ref 2); resin-bonded posterior bridges have questionable survival (refs 3-5); the single-tooth implant is the first choice (p 441, ref 6). The literature says: autotransplantation, which the paper does not discuss, is a tested alternative; premolars transplanted with incomplete roots survived 95 % with 96 % pulp healing (Andreasen 1990, 370 premolars, VERIFIED). My read: unlike an implant, a transplanted tooth can erupt with its neighbours, so it does not have to wait for growth to stop. Proffit (notes, 6th Ch 18 / 7th Ch 19): hold the space with a fixed retainer, or a temporary bonded bridge if the wait for growth is long.

## Practical protocol notes (as the paper describes them)

**Reducing and keeping the E (pp 441-442; Fig 1)**
1. Take a bitewing; measure the E's mesiodistal width at the CEJ.
2. Mark that width on the occlusal surface with a pencil or marking pen.
3. With a sharp carbide fissure bur or a diamond bur, follow the line toward the gingiva and remove a wafer of enamel and dentin from the mesial and the distal: about 2 mm each, aiming at a crown about 7-8 mm wide, without causing pulpal necrosis.
4. Apply light-cured composite to the cut mesial and distal surfaces, and build up the occlusal surface of the short E so it occludes.
5. Close the interproximal spaces; the E then functions as a premolar until the implant.

**Ankylosed E (pp 442-443; Fig 2)**
1. Compare the interdental bone mesial and distal to the E on a radiograph: flat (not ankylosed) or oblique, lower around the E (ankylosed).
2. Judge growth remaining. Significant: extract. Little, with slight submergence: keep.
3. After extraction, no space maintainer; let the 4 and 6 drift and erupt together.
4. In the comprehensive phase, push the 4 and 6 apart to the implant width (orthodontic implant-site development).

**Narrow ridge after a late extraction (pp 440, 443; Fig 5)**
1. Bracket the 4 and push it distally into the 5 position (Fig 5B-C show an archwire with a coil spring; my reading of the images, as the paper does not describe the mechanics).
2. At flap elevation, expect bone distal to the moved premolar, where the ridge had been deficient (Fig 5F).
3. Place the implant in the 4 site once serial cephalometric superimpositions show that growth has stopped.

**Implant as anchor (pp 438-439, 443; Fig 3)**
1. Initial alignment.
2. Diagnostic wax-up with the restorative dentist to set the implant position for all three disciplines.
3. Implant; provisional crown after integration; bracket on the crown.
4. Protract (or retract) the adjacent teeth against the implant; final porcelain crown.

**Closing with anchorage (pp 439-440, 443; Fig 4)**
- Patient 4: chincup and elastics; upper and lower 6s slid mesially along a continuous archwire; about 4 years. Today: miniscrews or mini-implants.
- Check the result with a cephalometric superimposition: the lower incisors should not have moved lingually (Fig 4F).

## What the paper does NOT tell you

- **Any data of its own.** Five selected, successful patients, all female; no failures, follow-up durations, survival or bone levels for any implant.
- **How agenesis was confirmed** in the 8-year-old (Patient 2), or whether the left side of her bilateral case received an implant.
- **How the 13 mm was measured** (the cited Fig 1C is an intraoral photograph without a scale), or how much was actually removed from Patient 1's E.
- **Pulp outcome data after reduction:** the caption says the pulp "was not damaged", but no vitality test is reported; no data on how long a reduced E lasts or how fast its roots resorb.
- **How much space to open for an implant** (no millimetre target), root parallelism, or how to retain the space while waiting for growth (Proffit notes: fixed retainer or temporary bonded bridge).
- **Implant ages for Patients 1 and 2**, and loading times for any patient.
- **Patient 4's overjet mechanics, upper incisor change, vertical change from the chincup,** and whether the upper left 5 was extracted. The superimposition method behind Fig 4F is not named.
- **Patient 5's growth evidence:** the text cites cephalometric superimpositions, but none is shown (Fig 5G is an intraoral photograph).
- **Thresholds:** how much growth is "significant", how much submergence is "slight", or how much crowding favours closure.
- **Options outside the five cases:** decoronation, autotransplantation, modern resin-bonded bridges as interim or definitive restorations, long-term retention of an unreduced E, and male patients.
- **Evidence for several rules:** the 2 mm reduction rule, the no-space-maintainer policy, the bone-level sign and the crowding and profile rule carry no reference or only the authors' own review.

## One sentence for a patient or a junior

- **To a junior:** "Profile and crowding decide whether you close a missing lower 5 space; if you keep it, keep a healthy E (narrowed, with composite), pull an ankylosed one early while the child is growing, skip the space maintainer, let moving teeth build the ridge, and wait for serial cephs before the implant."
- **To a parent:** "Your child's baby molar can hold the bone for the future implant, so we'll slim it down and keep it; the implant itself has to wait until the face has finished growing, usually the late teens for girls and early twenties for boys."
