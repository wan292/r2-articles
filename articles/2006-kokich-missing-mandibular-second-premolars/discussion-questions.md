# Questions to ask about this paper

Kokich VG, Kokich VO. Congenitally missing mandibular second premolars: clinical options. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2006;130(4):437-44. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2006.05.025. Department of Orthodontics, University of Washington, Seattle. It is a Clinician's Corner article, submitted March 2006 and accepted May 2006, and it is expert opinion illustrated by five patients (Figs 1 to 5, 42 panels, derived). It has a structured abstract (Introduction, Implications, Significance, Purpose) but no methods, no tables, no statistics and no funding statement. There are 22 references.

The paper has no data, so good questions about it are not "what did they find". They are "where does the rule break" and "what was the rule built on". What lasts is the decision framework. Growth sets the clock for the implant. Ridge preservation decides whether you keep, narrow or extract the E. Profile and crowding decide whether you open or close. You get your edge in the room in three ways:
- by checking the numbers behind the E reduction
- by asking how reliable the bone-level sign for ankylosis is
- by knowing what the cited sources (Ostler, Fudalej, Duyck) and the later trials actually show

Every page reference below was checked on the page images. Page numbers are journal pages (437 to 444). Anything from outside the paper carries a label:
- (literature): VERIFIED means the abstract was read in this run; UNVERIFIED means not checked.
- (Proffit, notes): verified in the user's Proffit study pages (6th edition with 7th-edition boxes) in this run, not against the printed book. Chapters 1 to 17 have the same numbers in both editions; 6th-edition Ch 18 and 19 are 7th-edition Ch 19 and 20.
- (hub): a paper already filed in this study hub, named by its slug.
- (my read): my own interpretation.

How to ask: keep each question to one breath and open with the patient or the figure ("In Patient 4 ..."). Frame any wording problem as "did I misread it?". Kokich's interdisciplinary teaching is standard in most programmes, and some attendings will quote this paper word for word. Let the captions and the follow-up studies make the point, not you.

Past exams have already used this paper. Its Fig 1 appeared in the R2 promotion exam 2023 (Q8, "reduce width and maintain") and again in 2024 (Q34, effect of extracting the E: "large bony defect"). The ankylosed-E stem has been keyed both ways (see Q12). All three are worked through at the top of `exam-questions.md` (PE1 to PE3).

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "Patient 1's E was 13 mm, and on p 442 about 2 mm comes off each side to leave 7 to 8 mm. From 13 mm that leaves 9. Do you work from the 2 mm, or only from the CEJ width on the bitewing? And how close to the pulp do you go?"
- **Why it lands:** You have done the arithmetic on pp 437 and 442 (all derived):
  - 13 − 7.5 = 5.5 mm too wide.
  - 13 − 2 × 2 = 9 mm left; reaching 7.5 mm needs about 2.75 mm a side.
  - A 7 to 8 mm crown after 4 mm of reduction fits an E of 11 to 12 mm.

  The guide the paper actually gives is the CEJ width on a bitewing, marked on the occlusal surface (p 441), so "2 mm" is a typical figure, not a rule. Patient 1's E was also unusually wide:
  - Bjerklin & Bennett 2000 measured a mean lower E width of 10.53 ± 0.51 mm (literature, VERIFIED).
  - Proffit puts the lower E about 2 mm wider than the 5 on average (notes, Ch 3).

  On an average E, 2 mm a side would overshoot: 10.53 − 4 = 6.53 mm (derived).
- **What you will probably hear:** Cut to the CEJ line, not to a number; check the pulp horns on the bitewing; separate the contacts first; some reduce in two visits.
- **Follow-up:** "The Fig 1 caption says the pulp was not damaged after closure, but no vitality test is reported. Do you test vitality after narrowing an E?"
- **Avoid saying:** "How do you reduce the E?" It is on pp 441-442.

### Q2. "Patient 1's E was below the occlusal plane, but flat bone meant 'not ankylosed'. On p 442 submersion is explained by ankylosis. In clinic, is infraocclusion alone ever enough to call it, or do you only trust the bone level? And how do you follow a low E with flat bone?"
- **Why it lands:** The diagnostic rule (p 443) is flat crest = erupting together, oblique crest = ankylosis. It is cited to the authors' own review (ref 1), with no accuracy data. Patient 1 shows that a low E is not necessarily ankylosed: the paper treats a short E as typical and simply builds it up with composite (p 442). The literature agrees that infraocclusion and ankylosis are not the same thing (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Two 2026 systematic reviews (Otsugu, 14 studies; Baril, 37 studies): infraocclusion prevalence 1.3 to 24.8 %; not every infraoccluded molar is ankylosed.
  - Hvaring 2014: 188 Es in 111 hypodontia patients. Significant infraocclusion in 43.6 %, severe in 18.8 %; infraocclusion predicted prognosis better than root resorption did.
  - Kurol 1981: infraocclusion in 8.9 % of 1059 children, peaking at 14.3 % at 8 to 9 years, more than 10 times commoner in the mandible.

  Proffit (notes, Ch 3) gives the same mechanism: an early-ankylosed tooth submerges because its neighbours erupt and carry bone with them.
- **What you will probably hear:** Percussion note, no physiological mobility, and serial bitewings to see whether the step grows. A low but stable E with flat bone is watched, not extracted.
- **Follow-up:** "If the step grows but the first film showed flat bone, how soon would you take the next one?"
- **Avoid saying:** "How do you diagnose an ankylosed E?" It is on p 443.

### Q3. "The Discussion says to place the anchor implant 'before orthodontic treatment', but Patient 3 was aligned first, then had the wax-up, then the implant. What do we need done orthodontically before the surgeon places an anchor implant? And whose wax-up sets its position, ours or the restorative dentist's?"
- **Why it lands:** You are reading p 443 against the case on p 438. Three details support it:
  - The provisional crown went on after integration and the bracket after that (p 438; Fig 3E-F). That is conventional (delayed) loading, although p 443 says early or immediate loading is possible, especially in orthodontic patients.
  - The Fig 3 caption puts the implant in the "first premolar position". The text says a second-premolar implant, and Fig 3E-H show the crown directly distal to the bracketed first premolar. Summaries in circulation copy the caption.
  - The upper 7 was left unopposed. If it supererupts, the paper offers extraction or a second implant distal to the lower 7 (p 438).
- **What you will probably hear:** Align and level first, so the neighbouring roots are where they will finish. Set the position on a set-up or wax-up with the restorative dentist, use a surgical guide, and leave the space distal to the implant for the 7 to close.
- **Follow-up:** "If the upper 7 does overerupt, which is better for her over a lifetime: extracting it, or a second lower implant?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why use an implant as anchorage?" p 443 answers it: fewer restorations and an immobile anchor.

### Q4. "Patient 4 has a 5-mm overjet in the text and an 'end-to-end malocclusion' in the caption, and the text reads Fig 4F only for the lower incisors and lower molars. How did the overjet close? And what did 4 years of chincup do to the mandibular plane?"
- **Why it lands:** You are reading p 439 against the Fig 4 caption on p 441. "End-to-end" may describe the buccal segments; Fig 4A shows a visible overjet (checked on the page image). The paper reports no upper incisor change, no vertical measure and no superimposition method. The tracing labels read 13-8 and 17-11, a span of 4 y 3 mo (derived), against 4 years of treatment in the text (p 439).

  Proffit (notes, Ch 13) says a chincup mainly rotates the mandible down and back rather than restraining it, and tends to tip the lower incisors lingually. Here the lower incisors were held, which is the paper's point. p 443 cites Fig 4 for "a protraction facemask and a chincup", although only a chincup and elastics were used (p 439).
- **What you will probably hear:** Some upper incisor retraction while the upper spaces closed, plus growth between 13 y 8 mo and 17 y 11 mo. The chincup served as anchorage here, not as orthopaedics.
- **Follow-up:** "With miniscrews today, would you still protract the upper molar too, or close only the lower spaces and finish the upper right in a Class II molar?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why a chincup?" p 439: she was treated before mini-implants were available.

## Tier 2. Mechanics and biology

### Q5. "After the E comes out with no space maintainer, the 4 and 6 erupt together and are later pushed apart to make the implant site. Do they drift bodily, or tip and rotate? And do we know the ridge we build is better than one held open with a maintainer?"
- **Why it lands:** p 443 says the separating roots deposit bone equal to the widths of the premolar and molar, with no reference. What has been measured (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Mamopoulou 1996: 11 children after lower E extraction. 46 % of the space had closed at 1 year and 80 % at 4 years, leaving 2 mm; the lower midline shifted, and overjet, overbite and incisor position were unchanged.
  - Eliášová 2014: 55 patients, 71 sites, first premolar distalised into the 5 position. Ridge width rose 28.5 % and height 1.1 mm; 2 to 5 years later the loss was only 4.2 % in width and 0.07 mm in height.

  Proffit (notes, Ch 12) calls the drift "highly variable and unpredictable". His 7th-edition box in Ch 11 says a space maintainer will not stop ridge resorption, which supports Kokich's choice.
- **What you will probably hear:** The 6 tips mesially and rotates mesiolingually, the 4 tips distally, and both need uprighting; in unilateral cases the midline wanders.
- **Follow-up:** "In a unilateral case, how do you stop the lower midline drifting while the space closes?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why no space maintainer?" p 443 answers it.

### Q6. "Patient 5's implant waited until 17, after superimpositions showed growth had finished, but none is shown: Fig 5G is a photo of the implant. What do we superimpose, and over what interval, before we call vertical growth finished? And for a boy, do we really wait until about 21?"
- **Why it lands:** p 440 cites Fig 5G for the superimpositions; the panel is an intraoral photo (checked on the page image). The 17 and 21 figures come from Fudalej (ref 9, "in press"), published in 2007 under a different title. Its abstract (literature, VERIFIED): 142 males and 159 females followed to 10 years after retention; growth continued after puberty but was clinically insignificant after the second decade. The abstract gives no 17 or 21 cut-offs, so those two figures are UNVERIFIED in the source.

  Proffit (notes):
  - Ch 6: serial cephalograms are the most accurate way to tell whether growth has stopped; hand-wrist and vertebral staging are not accurate for this.
  - Ch 19 (7th ed Ch 20): an implant placed too early behaves like an ankylosed tooth and appears to intrude.

  The hub adds a method warning. `1989-nielsen-maxillary-superimposition-three-methods` found that best-fit maxillary superimposition under-reads vertical dental change by about 30 % at the molars and 50 % at the incisors, while Björk-Skieller structural registration showed no significant vertical difference from the implants.
- **What you will probably hear:** Two cephalograms about a year apart with no change in lower face height or ramus height, superimposed on the anterior cranial base. A bonded temporary in the meantime.
- **Follow-up:** "If a best-fit method under-reads vertical change, which registration should we use for this decision?"
- **Avoid saying:** "When do you place the implant?" p 441 answers it.

### Q7. "In Patient 5 a wide first-premolar root was pushed distally through the narrowed ridge. In the canine paper, Kokich warned that moving a tooth labially out of the ridge causes dehiscence. Is this safe because the movement stays inside the alveolus, mesiodistally?"
- **Why it lands:** It links two papers in the topic. `2004-kokich-impacted-maxillary-canines` (hub) warns against labial traction because of dehiscence and recession. This paper (p 443) cites refs 12 and 13 for moving a wide root through a narrow ridge without losing support. Ref 13 (literature, VERIFIED), Hom & Turley 1984: 14 adults, lower 6 spaces closed by 6.2 mm (2.7 to 11.5); most lost about 1.3 mm of crestal bone, and ridge width rose 1.2 mm. Eliášová 2014 (Q5) is the closest modern match.

  A detail worth knowing: the bone was found distal to the premolar, where the ridge had been deficient (p 440), and the implant went into the old 4 site, which never had the extraction defect (my read). The text cites "Fig 3, C-E" for this; Fig 5 C-E is meant.
- **What you will probably hear:** Bodily movement with light continuous force remodels the cortical plates; expect it to be slow, especially in adults; consider CBCT.
- **Follow-up:** "The paper gives no time for this. Proffit's notes say closing into a buccolingually narrowed ridge can take about 3 years, against about 6 months for uprighting (Ch 19, 7th ed Ch 20). Would you warn the family about that?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Can a tooth be moved through a narrow ridge?" p 443 says yes.

### Q8. "On p 443 a continuous one-direction orthodontic load 'stimulates bone formation' around an implant, citing Duyck 2001. Duyck's abstract is a rabbit study: static loading looked like the unloaded controls, and excessive dynamic loading caused crater-shaped defects. Is 'does no harm' the fairer reading? And how soon do our surgeons let us load an anchor implant?"
- **Why it lands:** Duyck 2001 (literature, VERIFIED): 10 rabbits, implants in the tibiae, 14 days of loading. Static loads gave bone similar to unloaded controls; excessive dynamic loads gave crater-shaped defects and lower bone density (P < .007). The source shows no harm, not stimulation. None of the five cases shows early loading: Patient 3 was loaded after integration (p 438).
- **What you will probably hear:** Wait for the surgeon's clearance, then use light forces. The point is that orthodontic loads are tolerated.
- **Follow-up:** "Then is the early-loading paragraph illustrated anywhere in the paper?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Can you load an implant with orthodontic force?"

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q9. "All five patients are female, four of them aged 8 to 14 when they presented, and all five outcomes are good. The hardest timing case, a boy who grows until about 21, isn't shown. Do we have a series of these options that includes boys and failures?"
- **Why it lands:**
  - **Evidence level.** Five selected successes (derived: 5/5 female) with no selection rule, outcome definition, follow-up or failures. Oxford CEBM Level 5 (expert opinion), or Level 4 at most if read as a case series; GRADE very low.
  - **Retained-E outcomes are the best follow-up data on the "keep the E" option** (literature, VERIFIED):
    - Bjerklin & Bennett 2000: 59 Es in 41 people, followed to a mean of 20 y 6 mo. 2 exfoliated, 5 were extracted, and none were lost after 20 years. Infraocclusion rose from 0.47 ± 1.13 to 1.43 ± 1.13 mm.
    - Sletten 2003: 28 Es in 20 adults, followed 12.4 ± 7.7 years. 24 (86 %) were still functioning, with 0.16 mm of root shortening.
- **What you will probably hear:** A Clinician's Corner is meant to teach a framework, not to prove it. The retained-E data support keeping a healthy E.
- **Follow-up:** "So in a boy, would you keep a healthy E into his twenties and decide on the implant then?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is this evidence-based?"

### Q10. "The paper quotes Ostler and Kokich as 25 % narrowing in the first 4 years and 30 % by 7. Their 1994 abstract gives 25 % within 3 years and 4 % over the next 3, and the reference list titles it 'second molars'. For the exam, which numbers do I quote?"
- **Why it lands:** You have checked the source behind the paper's headline number. Ostler & Kokich 1994 (literature, VERIFIED, J Prosthet Dent 1994;71(2):144-9):
  - 35 sites in 22 patients; the published title says second premolars.
  - Width fell 25 % within 3 years, then 4 % over the next 3 years, about 29 % by 6 years (derived).
  - No correlation with time or age.

  The claims that facial resorption exceeds lingual and that the ridges stayed implantable are not in the abstract (UNVERIFIED). The message survives either way: most of the loss comes early.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Quote the paper's 25 % and 30 % when the question says 'according to Kokich 2006'; remember that most of it is early."
- **Follow-up:** "If most of the narrowing happens in the first 3 years, does that make keeping a healthy E until growth ends even more important?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Are the numbers wrong?"

### Q11. "The paper calls early hemisection 'especially attractive' for a child seen early. The 2025 split-mouth trial found almost the same residual space as plain extraction, more complications and higher cost. Do we still hemisect here?"
- **Why it lands:** pp 443-444 against the trial (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Abdul Jabbar 2025 (split-mouth RCT, n = 40, bilateral agenesis, mean age 10.03 years, 4.2 years' follow-up): residual space between the 6 and the 4 was 2.04 ± 1.67 mm with hemisection vs 2.39 ± 1.86 mm with extraction (P = .053); no difference in the angulation of the 6 or the 4; more complications (P = .0176) and higher cost. The authors advise against hemisection as an interceptive option.
  - Nawaia 2025 (same trial vs 29 untreated children; overall effect of removing both lower Es, whichever side was hemisected): the lower incisors retroclined 4.5° and moved back 1.8 mm, overjet rose 1.2 mm and overbite 1.5 mm, with no skeletal harm.
  - Naoumova 2026 (same trial): no difference in quality of life (CPQ, P = .218); 82.5 % did not want the residual spaces treated.

  Northway's own studies, refs 21 and 22 (literature, VERIFIED), compared 23 hemisection cases with 30 four-first-premolar and 30 four-second-premolar extraction cases: hemisection gave less distal movement of the upper incisor and lips, and more lower molar protraction.
- **What you will probably hear:** Rarely done now; simple extraction and monitoring is enough. Proffit (notes, Ch 12 and 16) still lists it: hemisect with pulp therapy, or remove only the distal root, let the 6 come halfway, then remove the rest.
- **Follow-up:** "The same trial measured 4.5° of lower incisor retroclination after both Es came out, with one side hemisected in every child. So hemisection on one side did not protect the incisors. Isn't that exactly the incisor retraction Kokich warns about in a normal profile?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is hemisection?"

## Tier 4. Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "For an ankylosed E in a growing child, Kokich says extract early with no space maintainer. Proffit's chapter 12 gives decoronation as the alternative, increasingly when the growth spurt is still ahead, and the paper never mentions it. Past exam recalls key this stem both ways. Which would you choose, and what in the stem decides it?"
- **Why it lands:** The comparison is p 443 against Proffit (notes, Ch 12, "Ankylosed primary molars without successors"). Proffit gives two options:
  - Early extraction before the vertical defect advances, with no space maintainer. The 6 drifts mesially, shrinks the implant space toward a premolar's width and brings bone with it.
  - Decoronation, after which new bone may form over the buried roots.

  The past-exam recalls disagree:
  - The study pages' Ch 12 recall tagged 2020 (ankylosed lower E, mild lower crowding, no successor; options extraction, decoronation, retain) keys **decoronation**.
  - The study pages' high-yield list keys **extraction** for the same stem, because the crowding uses up the space.
  - The R3 Promotion 2021 crash-course recall, Q99 (primary molars with no successors, shown with a Proffit Ch 12 figure), also keys **decoronation**.

  See `exam-questions.md`, PE3.
- **What you will probably hear:** If the space will be closed (crowding, protrusion), extract and use the drift. If an implant is planned and the E is deeply submerged with growth ahead, decoronate to keep the ridge.
- **Follow-up:** "So the plan for the space, close or implant, decides between them?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is decoronation?"

### Q13. "Kokich closes the space when the opposing arch is crowded or the profile is protrusive. Proffit says that with one missing second premolar a restoration is usually indicated, unless there is true unilateral space loss or considerable crowding on the other side, and that unless all four 5s are missing you may need extractions in the opposing arch. With one lower 5 missing, what do you do with the upper arch and the molar relationship?"
- **Why it lands:** The paper (p 443) and Proffit (notes, Ch 12) agree on the principle but frame it differently: the paper by profile and crowding, Proffit by symmetry and occlusion. Proffit adds that unopposed second molars can overerupt, which is what Patient 3 was left with (p 438). Closing a single lower 5 space without touching the upper arch moves the lower 6 forward by up to a premolar width, toward a Class III molar on that side (derived). Patient 4's spaces were closed in both arches to finish in Class I (p 439).
- **What you will probably hear:** Accept a Class III molar with Class I canines if the occlusion is functional; or extract an upper premolar if the upper arch is crowded; otherwise keep the space for an implant.
- **Follow-up:** "Does the midline matter more than the molar here?"
- **Avoid saying:** "When do you close the space?" p 443 answers it.

### Q14. "Patient 2 was 8 years 3 months when she presented, and her Es were then extracted for agenesis. Proffit says good premolars seldom form after 8. Would you extract at that age on one film, or repeat the radiograph first?"
- **Why it lands:** p 437 does not say how agenesis was confirmed, and the extraction is irreversible.
  - Proffit (notes, Ch 12): a premolar that starts forming very late is likely to erupt very late and may be abnormal, so careful observation is needed. Proffit (notes, Ch 5): the most distal tooth of a type is the one that goes missing.
  - Aasheim & Ogaard 1993 (literature, VERIFIED, PubMed 8248724): panoramic films of 1953 Norwegian 9-year-olds. Of the children recorded at 9 as missing a second premolar, 11.3 % of boys and 2.9 % of girls showed late mineralisation between 9 and 12. Hypodontia (third molars excluded) was 6.5 % (girls 7.2 %, boys 5.8 %), and the lower 5 was the tooth most often missing.
  - Polder 2004 (literature, VERIFIED): in Europe 4.6 % of males and 6.3 % of females, with the lower 5 the most affected tooth.

  Patient 2 was a girl, so her chance of a late 5 was small (2.9 % at 9), but she was younger than Aasheim's 9-year-olds (my read).
- **What you will probably hear:** Repeat the panoramic before an irreversible step, especially in a boy or if the contralateral 5 is late.
- **Follow-up:** "If the E is ankylosed and submerging at 8, does a 6- to 12-month wait for a second film cost too much bone?"
- **Avoid saying:** "How common is premolar agenesis?"

### Q15. "Neither the paper nor its five cases mentions autotransplantation. In a growing child with a missing lower 5 and crowding in the upper arch, an upper premolar transplanted at about three-quarters root development keeps the ridge growing, which an implant cannot. Why isn't it among the options?"
- **Why it lands:** It is the main gap in the paper. The outcome data are good (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Andreasen 1990: 370 transplanted premolars. Survival 95 % with open apices and 98 % with closed; pulp healing 96 % vs 15 %.
  - Atala-Acevedo 2017, a systematic review of open-apex teeth: success 89.68 %, survival 98.21 %; premolars failed less often than molars (OR 0.46).

  Proffit's study pages carry a 2024 recall on the ideal donor: about three-quarters root development (notes, Ch 12).
- **What you will probably hear:** It needs an experienced surgeon, a donor tooth that is being removed anyway, and the right stage of root development, so the window is narrow.
- **Follow-up:** "If we are extracting an upper premolar for crowding anyway, is transplanting it better than waiting until 17 for an implant?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is autotransplantation?"

## Do not ask these

Items 1 to 6 are answered in the paper itself (page given), so asking them shows it was not read.

1. "What are the options for a missing lower 5?" Keep and narrow the E, extract and develop the site, move the 4, use an anchor implant, close with anchorage, hemisect (pp 441-444).
2. "How much wider is the E than the premolar?" 13 mm against 7.5 mm in Patient 1 (p 437).
3. "How do you know an E is ankylosed?" Flat versus oblique bone (p 443).
4. "Why no space maintainer?" Teeth that erupt together carry bone; they are pushed apart later (p 443).
5. "When does growth stop?" About 17 in girls and 21 in boys (p 441).
6. "Why not a bridge?" Pulp risk with full coverage in the young; questionable survival of posterior resin-bonded bridges (p 441).
7. "Aren't most references his own?" Five of 22 include V. Kokich (refs 1, 7, 8, 9, 14; 22.7 %, derived). Asked that way it is an accusation, and a Clinician's Corner is meant to distil the authors' own work. Q10 makes the same point with data.
8. "Is this a study?" It does not claim to be one. Ask about the evidence behind one specific claim instead.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Walk me through how you manage a congenitally missing lower second premolar."**
1. **Close or keep?** Close if the opposing arch is crowded or the profile is protrusive. With no crowding and a normal profile, keep the space for an implant, or close it only with extra anchorage: miniscrews, mini-implants, or a facemask or chincup (p 443; Patient 4).
2. **If keeping, and the E is healthy:** keep it, narrow it and cover it with composite, and place the implant when growth has stopped (Patient 1).
3. **If the E is ankylosed:**
   - With significant growth left, extract it early with no space maintainer, then push the 4 and 6 apart to build the ridge (Patient 2).
   - With little growth left and slight submersion, keep it (p 443).
4. **If extraction came late and the ridge is narrow:** move the 4 distally and place the implant in its old site (Patient 5).
5. **If the 6 is missing too:** one implant used as an anchor to close the excess space (Patient 3).
6. **In a young child where closure is wanted:** consider early hemisection (pp 443-444).

**"How do you narrow an E, and why the composite?"**
1. Take a bitewing and measure the E's width at the CEJ.
2. Mark that width on the occlusal surface.
3. With a sharp carbide fissure or diamond bur, cut toward the gingiva to remove a wafer of enamel and dentin on the mesial and distal. That is about 2 mm a side, leaving a crown of about 7 to 8 mm, without risking the pulp.
4. Cover the cut dentin with light-cured composite: the contacts will close and the surfaces are hard to clean. The same composite builds up the short crown so it occludes and the opposing tooth does not supererupt (pp 441-442).
5. Close the spaces; the E then works as a premolar. Its diverging roots usually resorb as the neighbouring roots reach them, and bone replaces them (p 442).

**"How do you tell an ankylosed E, and what do you do?"**
- Look at the interdental bone between the E and the 6 and the 4. Flat bone means they are erupting together. Oblique bone, lower around the E, confirms ankylosis (p 443).
- Then decide by growth. With significant growth left, extract to prevent a vertical ridge defect that would need an unpredictable graft. With little growth left and slight submersion, keep it to hold the ridge width (pp 442-443).
- I would add percussion and serial films, because infraocclusion alone is not ankylosis, and mention decoronation as Proffit's alternative (notes, Ch 12).

**"What is orthodontic implant-site development? Give two examples from the paper."**
Moving teeth carry alveolar bone with them, so tooth movement can build an implant site without a graft (p 443).
- **Patient 2:** after the Es were extracted with no maintainer, the 4 and 6 drifted together and their eruption filled the vertical defect. The space was then reopened and the separating roots left a robust ridge.
- **Patient 5:** the 4 was pushed distally through a narrowed ridge, regenerating bone, and the implant went into the 4's old site with no graft.

**"When can the implant go in, and how do you know?"**
- When vertical facial growth has stopped, shown by superimposing serial cephalograms: no further ramus growth or vertical change (p 441).
- On average about 17 years in girls and 21 in boys (Fudalej, as cited). Patient 5's implant went in at 17.
- An implant placed too early behaves like an ankylosed tooth and ends up infraoccluded as the neighbours erupt (Proffit, notes, Ch 19, 7th ed Ch 20). Hand-wrist and vertebral staging are not accurate for this (Proffit, notes, Ch 6).

**"Why not a bridge?"**
In young patients full-coverage abutments risk pulp devitalisation and root canal treatment (ref 2), and posterior resin-bonded bridges have questionable survival (refs 3-5). So the paper names the single-tooth implant as the first choice (p 441, ref 6). While waiting for growth to finish, the space is held with a fixed retainer; that is Proffit's advice (notes, Ch 18, 7th ed Ch 19), not the paper's.

**"How much does the ridge shrink after you extract the E?"**
- **As the paper cites Ostler & Kokich:** 25 % in the first 4 years and a further 5 % by 7 years, 30 % in all. The ridge is still wide enough for an implant, but resorption is greater facially, so the implant sits lingually and the crown's cusp loading must be adjusted (p 441).
- **The source abstract:** 25 % within 3 years, then 4 % over the next 3 (literature, VERIFIED).

**"Tell me the five patients in one line each."**
1. **Patient 1:** a healthy E kept, narrowed and covered with composite.
2. **Patient 2:** ankylosed Es (girl of 8 y 3 mo) extracted with no maintainer, then the site was reopened for an implant.
3. **Patient 3:** missing 5 and 6, one implant as anchor to bring the 7 forward.
4. **Patient 4:** three missing 5s, all spaces closed by protraction with a chincup and elastics over 4 years, profile kept.
5. **Patient 5:** ankylosed E (girl of 14 y 6 mo) extracted, the 4 moved distally to build bone, implant at 17 with no graft.

**"What level of evidence is this?"**
A Clinician's Corner: expert opinion illustrated by five selected cases. Oxford Level 5, or Level 4 at most as a case series. There are no methods, measured outcomes, follow-up or failures, and all five patients are female (derived). Its value is the framework: growth, ridge, profile and crowding, teamwork. The best data behind "keep the E" are the long-term follow-ups of retained Es (Bjerklin & Bennett 2000; Sletten 2003; literature, VERIFIED).

## Ten-second summary

"Kokich and Kokich 2006 is a Clinician's Corner, five female patients and no data. Close the space if the opposing arch is crowded or the profile protrusive; otherwise keep it for an implant, or close it with extra anchorage. A healthy E is kept, narrowed about 2 mm a side and covered with composite until growth stops: about 17 in girls and 21 in boys, on serial cephs. Flat bone means a healthy E; oblique bone means ankylosis, and with growth ahead it comes out early, with no space maintainer, because moving teeth build the ridge."
