### PE1. R2 promotion exam 2023, "Literature review articles" section, Q8

**Recalled question:** the stem is recalled only as "Kokich missing premolar article (Fig 1)?", shown with Fig 1A-C: the submerged E, its radiograph and the occlusal view.

**Recalled answer:** reduce its width and maintain it.

**Answer per the paper:** the same (p 437; Fig 1; technique pp 441-442). Patient 1 was a girl of 12 y 4 mo:
- The E was submerged, but the bone beside it was flat, so it was not ankylosed.
- It was 13 mm wide against 7.5 mm for an average lower 5.
- An implant was planned, but she was still growing.
- The E was narrowed (Fig 1D-E) and restored with composite (Fig 1F-G), and the remaining space was closed to Class I molars and canines (Fig 1H-I).

**Why:** keeping the E preserves the buccolingual bone for the eventual implant (p 437). The implant waits for the end of vertical growth (p 441), and the E is too wide to leave as it is (p 441).

**Flag:** none. The recall agrees with the paper.

**Practice version** (options written for this file, not recalled). A 12-year-old girl is missing her mandibular right second premolar. The E sits slightly below the occlusal plane, the interdental bone beside it is flat on the radiograph, and it measures 13 mm. A single-tooth implant is planned. What is the best management?

A. Extract the E and place a lingual holding arch.
B. Extract the E and let the first molar drift mesially.
C. Keep the E unaltered until growth is complete.
D. Narrow the E mesially and distally, cover the cut surfaces with composite, close the remaining space and keep the E until the implant.
E. Decoronate the E.

**Answer:** D (p 437; pp 441-442).
- A and B: the E is not ankylosed, and extraction loses ridge width (p 441).
- C: the E is too wide and would affect the fit of the posterior teeth (p 441).
- E: decoronation is for an ankylosed tooth (Proffit, notes, Ch 12), and this E is not ankylosed (flat bone, p 437).

### PE2. R2 promotion exam 2024, Q34

**Recalled question:** a 14-year-old girl presents with a congenitally missing lower 5 and a retained primary molar, and the doctor decides to extract it. What is the effect? The stem is shown with Fig 1A-B of this paper, and the recall file names this article beside it.
- a) Large bony defect
- b) Excessive residual space
- c) Tipping of adjacent teeth
- d) Supra-eruption of the opposing tooth

**Recalled answer:** a (underlined in the recall).

**Answer per the paper:** a.
- Patient 1's E was kept "to preserve the buccolingual bone" for the implant (p 437).
- As the paper cites Ostler & Kokich, after E extraction the ridge narrows 25 % in the first 4 years and 30 % in all by 7 years, and resorbs more facially (p 441).
- In Patient 5, a girl of 14 y 6 mo, extraction of the ankylosed E was followed by substantial resorption, with vertical and buccolingual narrowing (p 440; Fig 5B). The defect would probably have needed a graft (p 440), and vertical grafting is often unpredictable (p 442).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- b: without a space maintainer the 4 and 6 drift and erupt together and close the space (Fig 2C-D; p 443).
- c: the paper presents that drift as useful (it carries bone), not as harm. Drift does involve some tipping in the literature: Mamopoulou 1996 measured the closure, and Proffit (notes, Ch 12) calls the drift unpredictable. So c is the strongest distractor, but the paper's point about extraction is bone.
- d: supereruption appears in the paper only as the reason to build up a short E with composite (p 442), and as the risk for Patient 3's unopposed upper 7 (p 438).

**Flag (recall vs paper):** the images are Patient 1's, and her E was **not** extracted: the bone was flat, so it was kept and narrowed. The stem's age and extraction match Patient 5, whose E was ankylosed. "Large" fits Patient 5's ankylosed, submerged E best. In the Ostler sample the paper cites, the loss after E extraction was 25 % to 30 % of the ridge width, and the ridges stayed wide enough for an implant without a graft (p 441). In the exam, answer a.

### PE3. Ankylosed lower E, no successor, mild crowding: extraction or decoronation?

**Recalled questions:**
- R3 promotion 2021 crash-course recall, Q99: primary molars with no successors (a Proffit Ch 12 figure), best treatment? Recalled answer: **decoronation**.
- Proffit study pages, Ch 12 past-year recall tagged 2020: ankylosed lower primary second molar, mild lower crowding, no successor. Options: extraction, decoronation, retain. Keyed **decoronation**.
- Proffit high-yield page, same stem: keyed **extraction**, reasoning that the crowding uses up the space.

**Answer per the paper:** extraction. With significant facial growth remaining, Kokich extracts the ankylosed E, with no space maintainer, to prevent a vertical ridge defect (p 443). With little growth left and slight submersion, he keeps it (p 443). Decoronation is not mentioned anywhere in the paper.

**Answer per Proffit (notes, Ch 12, "Ankylosed primary molars without successors"):** either.
- Early extraction before the vertical defect advances, with no space maintainer: the first molar drifts mesially, shrinks the space toward a premolar's width and brings bone with it.
- Decoronation, increasingly indicated when the growth spurt is still ahead; new bone may form over the buried roots.

**Flag:** the recalls disagree with each other, and both options are in Proffit. Read the stem:
- Crowding, closure or "use the space" in the stem points to extraction (Kokich p 443; Proffit's drift).
- "Preserve the ridge for an implant" with the growth spurt still ahead points to decoronation (Proffit).
- If the question names Kokich 2006, the answer is extraction.

**Rules from the Discussion (pp 440-444)**
- **Restoration:**
  - Full-coverage bridges in the young can devitalise pulps (ref 2).
  - Posterior resin-bonded bridges have questionable survival (refs 3-5).
  - First choice: a single-tooth implant (ref 6).
- **Ridge after E extraction** (Ostler & Kokich, ref 7):
  - Narrows 25 % in the first 4 years, then another 5 % by 7 years, 30 % in all.
  - Still wide enough for an implant.
  - More facial than lingual loss, so the implant sits lingually and the cusp loading is adjusted to protect the abutment and crown (ref 8).
- **Implant timing:**
  - The end of vertical facial growth, on serial cephalograms (ramus growth stopped).
  - Fudalej (ref 9): about 17 years in girls and 21 in boys.
  - So keep the E until then.
- **Narrowing the E:**
  1. Use a carbide fissure or diamond bur.
  2. Measure the CEJ width on a bitewing and mark it on the occlusal surface.
  3. Cut a wafer of enamel and dentin mesially and distally, about 2 mm each, leaving a crown of about 7 to 8 mm.
  4. Cover with composite (caries) and build up the short crown (occlusion, no supereruption).
  5. Close the spaces.
  6. The diverging roots usually resorb and are replaced by bone.
- **Ankylosis:**
  - Diagnose it by the interdental bone level: flat = erupting together; oblique, more apical around the E = ankylosis (ref 1).
  - Significant growth left: extract. Little growth and slight submersion: keep.
  - Vertical grafting is often unpredictable and costly (refs 10, 11).
- **Space maintainers:**
  - None in most cases, especially if an implant is planned.
  - The 4 and 6 erupt together and are later pushed apart: orthodontic implant-site development.
- **Late extraction with a narrow ridge:** graft, or push the 4 into the 5 position and place the implant at the 4 site (refs 12, 13; Fig 5).
- **5 and 6 missing:**
  - One implant from a wax-up, used as an anchor to close the excess space (ref 14).
  - Fewer restorations; an immobile anchor.
- **Loading:**
  - Early or immediate loading is possible (refs 16, 17), especially with orthodontic loads (ref 18), which are continuous and one-directional.
  - Load soon after the temporary restoration.
- **Closing instead (ref 19):**
  - Close if the opposing arch is crowded or the profile protrusive.
  - With no crowding and a normal profile, add anchorage: a facemask or chincup, miniscrews (ref 20) or mini-implants.
  - Or hemisect the E early so the 6 erupts mesially without moving the lower incisors (refs 21, 22).

**Numbers to know**
- **E width:** 13 mm (Patient 1), against 7.5 mm for an average lower 5.
  - Trap: 13 mm is one patient's tooth, not an average, although some summaries in circulation present it as one.
  - Proffit puts the lower E about 2 mm wider than the 5 on average (notes, Ch 3).
  - Bjerklin & Bennett 2000 measured a mean of 10.53 ± 0.51 mm (literature, VERIFIED).
- **Reduction:** about 2 mm per surface, leaving about 7 to 8 mm.
- **Ridge narrowing after E extraction:** 25 % in 4 years; +5 %; 30 % by 7 years.
- **End of vertical growth:** about 17 years in girls, about 21 in boys.
- **Ages:**
  - Patient 1: 12 y 4 mo.
  - Patient 2: 8 y 3 mo.
  - Patient 4: 13 y 8 mo, with a 4-year treatment and a 5-mm overjet.
  - Patient 5: 14 y 6 mo, with the implant at 17 y.
- **Derived:**
  - The E is 5.5 mm wider than the premolar.
  - 13 − 2 × 2 = 9 mm, and 7.5 mm needs about 2.75 mm per side.
  - Patient 5's implant came about 2 y 6 mo after she presented.
  - The Fig 4 tracings are labelled 13-8 and 17-11 (4 y 3 mo apart).
  - 5 of 22 references include V. Kokich (22.7 %).
  - All 5 patients are female.

**Literature to keep beside these** (all VERIFIED from abstracts in this run unless marked)
- **Ostler & Kokich 1994 (the paper's ref 7)** (J Prosthet Dent 1994;71(2):144-9; the published title says second premolars):
  - 35 sites in 22 patients.
  - Width fell 25 % within 3 years, then 4 % over the next 3.
  - "Facial more than lingual" and "still implantable" are not in the abstract (UNVERIFIED).
- **Fudalej 2007 (the paper's ref 9, cited as in press; published under a different title):**
  - 142 males and 159 females.
  - Growth was clinically insignificant after the second decade.
  - The abstract gives no 17 or 21 cut-offs (UNVERIFIED in the source).
- **Duyck 2001 (the paper's ref 18):** rabbit tibiae, 10 animals, 14 days. Static load looked like unloaded controls; excessive dynamic load caused crater defects.
- **Retained Es:**
  - Bjerklin & Bennett 2000: 59 Es followed to a mean of 20 y 6 mo; none lost after 20 years; mean E width 10.53 ± 0.51 mm.
  - Sletten 2003: 28 adult Es; 86 % still functioning after 12.4 ± 7.7 years.
- **Hvaring 2014:** significant infraocclusion in 43.6 % of 188 Es; infraocclusion predicted prognosis better than root resorption.
- **Mamopoulou 1996:** after lower E extraction, 46 % of the space closed at 1 year and 80 % at 4 years.
- **Eliášová 2014:** first premolar moved distally into the 5 site (71 sites). Ridge width +28.5 %, height +1.1 mm; stable at 2 to 5 years.
- **Hemisection trial (Abdul Jabbar 2025; split-mouth RCT, n = 40):**
  - Residual space 2.04 vs 2.39 mm against extraction (P = .053); more complications.
  - Nawaia 2025, same trial: after both lower Es were removed (one side extracted, the other hemisected), the lower incisors retroclined 4.5° and moved back 1.8 mm.
- **Aasheim & Ogaard 1993:** of 9-year-olds recorded as missing a second premolar, 11.3 % of boys and 2.9 % of girls showed late mineralisation by 12.

## Part 1. Multiple-choice questions (single best answer)

### Q1 (R). Narrowing the E

According to Kokich & Kokich 2006, which statement about narrowing a retained mandibular deciduous second molar is correct?

A. Patient 1's E was 13 mm wide against 7.5 mm for an average mandibular second premolar. Its width at the CEJ on a bitewing is marked on the occlusal surface, and about 2 mm is removed from each of the mesial and distal surfaces.
B. Only the distal surface is reduced, so that the contact with the first premolar stays intact.
C. About 4 mm is removed from each surface, and the cut dentin is left uncovered so it can be monitored for caries.
D. The E is narrowed until its roots no longer diverge beyond the crown, so they cannot block space closure.
E. The E is narrowed only after the implant has been placed, so that it acts as a space maintainer until then.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A: p 437 gives 13 mm and 7.5 mm. pp 441-442 give the method: bitewing CEJ width, about 2 mm off both surfaces, a crown of about 7 to 8 mm.
- B: both the mesial and the distal surfaces are reduced (p 442).
- C: composite is applied to the cut mesial and distal dentin to prevent decay (p 442).
- D: the roots are left alone. In most cases they resorb as the neighbouring roots reach them, and bone replaces them (p 442).
- E: in Patient 1 the E was narrowed and the spaces closed during orthodontic treatment, years before the implant (p 437).
- Number trap: 13 − 2 × 2 = 9 mm, not 7 to 8 mm; reaching 7.5 mm needs about 2.75 mm a side (derived). The CEJ width on the bitewing is the real guide.

**Tests:** Recall of the numbers and the technique.

### Q2 (A). The ankylosed E in a young child

A 9-year-old girl is missing her mandibular right second premolar. The E is 2 mm below the occlusal plane, and on the bitewing the alveolar crest slopes apically toward the E on both its mesial and distal sides. Her profile is normal, neither arch is crowded, and a single-tooth implant is the long-term plan. According to Kokich & Kokich, what should be done now?

A. Keep the E; it will hold the buccolingual width of the ridge until growth stops.
B. Extract the E now with no space maintainer, let the first premolar and first molar erupt together, and later push them apart to develop the implant site.
C. Extract the E and place a lingual holding arch to keep 7.5 mm for the implant.
D. Build the E up to the occlusal plane with composite and keep it until about 17 years.
E. Narrow the E by about 2 mm on each surface and close the spaces around it.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- B: p 443 gives the steps. Oblique bone, more apical around the E, confirms ankylosis. With significant growth remaining the E must be extracted to prevent a ridge defect. No space maintainer is used, especially when an implant is planned. The adjacent teeth erupt together, and moving them apart later builds the ridge (orthodontic implant-site development). This is Patient 2 (Fig 2).
- A: keeping the E applies only when little growth remains and the E is only slightly submerged (p 443).
- C: the paper avoids space maintainers (p 443). Proffit agrees that a maintainer does not stop ridge resorption (notes, Ch 11, 7th-edition box).
- D and E: Patient 1's approach for a healthy E with flat bone (p 437), not for an ankylosed one that will keep submerging.
- Decoronation is not offered here because the paper does not discuss it. Proffit lists it as the alternative (see PE3).

**Tests:** Applying the bone-level sign and the growth rule.

### Q3 (A). Closing the spaces in a good profile

A 13-year-old girl is congenitally missing her maxillary right second premolar and both mandibular second premolars. She has a minor arch-length deficiency in both arches, well-related jaws, incisors in a normal anteroposterior position and an ideal profile. The family wants to avoid restorations. Which plan matches the paper?

A. Extract the remaining maxillary second premolar and the deciduous second molars, and close all spaces with conventional mechanics.
B. Close the spaces by protracting the first molars in both arches with additional anchorage, so that the incisors are not retracted. Use mini-implants today; extraoral anchorage such as a chincup was used before they existed.
C. Open all the spaces now and place implants at 14 years.
D. Hemisect the deciduous second molars and let the first molars drift mesially.
E. Extract four first premolars to resolve the arch-length deficiency, and keep the deciduous molars.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- B is Patient 4 (pp 439-440) and the rule on p 443. With no crowding and a normal profile, closure needs extra anchorage, extraoral (facemask, chincup) or intraoral (miniscrews, mini-implants), to avoid unwanted facial change. Her chincup and elastics took 4 years, but she needed no restorations.
- A: the authors say this would have harmed her profile by overly retracting the lips relative to the chin (p 439).
- C: the family wants no restorations, and implants wait for the end of vertical growth (p 441).
- D: hemisection is described for an early age in a child monitored regularly (pp 443-444); she is 13.
- E: not discussed, and it would retract the incisors.

**Tests:** Applying the profile and crowding rule.

### Q5 (A). When to place the implant in a boy

A 15-year-old boy is missing his mandibular left second premolar. The E was lost, the space has been opened to 7.5 mm, and his orthodontic treatment is ending. According to Kokich & Kokich, when should the implant be placed?

A. Now, because facial growth in boys is complete by 15.
B. When serial cephalometric radiographs show that ramus growth and vertical facial change have stopped, on average at about 21 years in boys.
C. When a hand-wrist radiograph shows that the epiphyses have fused.
D. At about 17 years, the average for both sexes.
E. Immediately after debonding, because a retainer cannot hold the space.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- B: p 441. The age is set by the cessation of vertical facial growth, determined by comparing serial cephalograms. Fudalej (ref 9): about 17 years in girls and 21 in boys. Patient 5's implant went in at 17, after superimpositions (p 440).
- C: Proffit (notes, Ch 6) says serial cephalograms are the most accurate test, and hand-wrist and vertebral staging are not accurate for this.
- D: 17 is the figure for girls.
- E: Proffit (notes, Ch 18, 7th ed Ch 19) names a fixed retainer as the best way to hold an implant space. An implant placed too early behaves like an ankylosed tooth and appears to intrude (Ch 19, 7th ed Ch 20).
- Fudalej's published abstract supports late vertical growth but gives no 17 or 21 cut-offs (literature, VERIFIED abstract; the cut-offs UNVERIFIED). In the exam, quote 17 and 21 as the paper does.

**Tests:** Applying the timing rule and the method.

### Q6 (R). The ridge after E extraction

According to Kokich & Kokich, citing Ostler and Kokich, what happens to the alveolar ridge after a deciduous mandibular second molar is extracted?

A. It narrows by 25 % in the first 4 years and by 30 % in all by 7 years. It remains wide enough for an implant, but it resorbs more facially, so the implant sits lingually.
B. It narrows by about 50 % in the first year and always needs a graft before an implant.
C. It narrows equally on the facial and lingual sides, so the implant is centred in the ridge.
D. It does not narrow if a space maintainer is placed straight after the extraction.
E. It narrows by 30 % in the first 4 years and by a further 25 % by 7 years.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A: p 441. Because the implant sits lingually, the restorative dentist must alter the loading of the buccal and lingual cusps to avoid fracturing the abutment or crown (ref 8).
- B: the cited ridges were still broad enough for an implant without a graft (p 441).
- C: the paper says resorption is greater on the facial side.
- D: the paper does not use maintainers (p 443), and Proffit says a maintainer does not prevent ridge resorption (notes, Ch 11, 7th-edition box).
- E: the numbers are swapped and added.
- The source abstract gives 25 % within 3 years, then 4 % over the next 3 (literature, VERIFIED). When the question says "according to Kokich 2006", quote the paper's numbers.

**Tests:** Recall of the ridge numbers and their clinical consequence.

### Q7 (A). Missing second premolar and first molar

An adult woman is missing her mandibular right second premolar and first molar. The second molar is in Class II to the maxillary first molar, and the edentulous space is too large for one tooth and too small for two. What does the paper recommend?

A. Open the space enough for two implants, one for the premolar and one for the molar.
B. Close the whole space by protracting the second and third molars with Class III elastics.
C. Align the teeth, make a diagnostic wax-up, place one implant at the second-premolar position, and after integration use its provisional crown with a bracket as an anchor to bring the second molar mesially into Class I.
D. Replace both teeth with a three-unit bridge from the first premolar to the second molar.
E. Move the first premolar distally into the space and place the implant at the first-premolar site.

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:**
- C is Patient 3 (pp 438-439; Fig 3) and p 443. The patient gets fewer restorations, and the orthodontist gets an immobile anchor that moves the molar without disturbing the anterior teeth or the profile. The cost is an upper 7 without an antagonist. If it supererupts, it can be extracted, or an implant placed distal to the lower 7 (p 438).
- A: the space was too small for two teeth (p 438).
- B: closure on dental anchorage risks the anchorage, the anterior teeth and the profile; the implant protected all three (p 438).
- D: the paper's first choice is a single-tooth implant (p 441).
- E: this is Patient 5's solution for a narrow ridge after a late E extraction, not for a two-tooth gap.
- The Fig 3 caption puts the implant at the "first premolar position". The text (p 438) says second-premolar implant, and Fig 3E-H show the crown directly distal to the bracketed first premolar.

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q8 (R). Keeping the E

(a) Why keep a healthy retained E in a growing patient who will receive an implant? (b) Describe the narrowing and restoration, with the numbers. (c) What happens to the E's roots as the spaces close, and how do the paper and Proffit view that?

**Model answer:**

(a) **Why keep it.**
- Patient 1 was 12 y 4 mo, and her E was submerged but had flat bone, so it was not ankylosed (p 437).
- An implant was planned, but she was too young and still growing. Implant age is set by the end of vertical facial growth on serial cephalograms: about 17 years in girls and 21 in boys (p 441).
- After E extraction the ridge narrows 25 % in 4 years and 30 % by 7 (p 441). Keeping the E preserves the buccolingual bone.
- The E is too wide (13 mm against 7.5 mm for an average lower 5, p 437), which would affect the fit of the posterior teeth, so it is narrowed to a premolar's size (p 441).

(b) **Technique (pp 441-442).**
1. Use a sharp carbide fissure bur or a diamond bur. Remove enough to create space, but not enough to cause pulpal necrosis.
2. Measure the E's mesiodistal width at the CEJ on a bitewing and mark it on the occlusal surface with a pencil or marker.
3. Follow the line toward the gingiva, removing a wafer of enamel and dentin from the mesial and distal surfaces, about 2 mm each, leaving a crown of about 7 to 8 mm.
4. Apply light-cured composite to the cut mesial and distal dentin. These surfaces are hard to clean once the contacts close and could decay.
5. Use the composite to build up the occlusal surface of the typically short E so it occludes and the opposing tooth does not supererupt.
6. Close the spaces. The E then functions as a premolar (Fig 1).

(c) **Roots.**
- The paper (p 442): the divergent roots extend beyond the crown and might seem to block closure. In most cases they resorb as the socket walls of the neighbouring teeth reach them, and bone replaces them. The authors call this an ideal way to prepare the implant site (ref 1).
- Proffit (notes, Ch 12) describes the same resorption as a risk of narrowing an E.
- Both are right. Resorption is useful when the E is a placeholder for an implant, and harmful when the E is meant to last.
- Proffit also notes that many Es last into the early twenties or beyond.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Bone preservation as the reason, with the ridge numbers.
2. Growth as the timing, with 17 and 21 years and serial cephalograms.
3. 13 mm against 7.5 mm, and why width matters.
4. The CEJ bitewing guide and about 2 mm per surface, leaving 7 to 8 mm (credit noting that 13 − 4 = 9 mm, derived).
5. Composite, with both reasons (caries and occlusion or supereruption).
6. Root resorption replaced by bone, with the Proffit contrast.

### Q9 (A). The ankylosed E

(a) How do you diagnose ankylosis of an E? (b) How does growth decide between keeping and extracting it? (c) Contrast Patients 2 and 5. (d) Give two ways, from the paper, to obtain an implant site without a vertical bone graft.

**Model answer:**

(a) **Diagnosis.**
- The most reliable indicator is the alveolar bone level between the E and the adjacent first molar and first premolar (p 443; ref 1).
- Flat bone means the teeth are erupting evenly; compare Fig 1B, which was kept.
- Oblique bone, more apical around the E, confirms ankylosis (Fig 2B).
- Infraocclusion alone is not enough: Patient 1's E was submerged but not ankylosed (p 437).
- I would add percussion and serial films (my read). Hvaring 2014 found significant infraocclusion in 43.6 % of Es in hypodontia patients (literature, VERIFIED).

(b) **Growth.**
- Ankylosis while the face is still growing submerges the E relative to the erupting neighbours. The ridge becomes vertically deficient and may need a graft (ref 10), and vertical grafting is often unpredictable and an added expense (ref 11) (pp 442-443).
- With significant growth remaining, extract the E to prevent a significant ridge defect.
- With little growth remaining and only slight submersion, keep it to preserve ridge width (p 443).

(c) **Patients 2 and 5.**
- **Patient 2, 8 y 3 mo** (pp 437-438):
  - The deciduous molars were ankylosed (oblique bone) and were extracted early, with no space maintainer.
  - A significant vertical bony defect was present immediately after extraction, but eruption of the neighbouring teeth brought bone and tissue up to normal levels (Fig 2D). The 4 and 6 drifted together.
  - The lower incisors were lingual relative to the chin, so they were proclined and the 5 space reopened. The separating roots built the ridge for an implant: orthodontic implant-site development.
- **Patient 5, 14 y 6 mo** (p 440):
  - Her ankylosed E was extracted with less eruption ahead. The ridge narrowed substantially, vertically and buccolingually (Fig 5B), and would probably have needed a graft.
  - Instead, the 4 was pushed distally into the 5 position. At surgery there was enough bone where the ridge had been deficient (Fig 5F), and the implant went into the old 4 site at 17 years with no graft.
- **The lesson:** the earlier the ankylosed E comes out, the more eruption remains to repair the defect. Late extraction needs a second strategy.

(d) **Two ways without a vertical graft** (p 443):
1. No space maintainer: let the 4 and 6 erupt together, then push them apart. The separating roots deposit bone and make a robust ridge, at the cost of longer treatment.
2. Move the first premolar distally into the second-premolar position and place the implant at the first-premolar site (refs 12, 13; Fig 5). Eliášová 2014 measured +28.5 % ridge width and +1.1 mm height with this approach (literature, VERIFIED).

Decoronation is Proffit's third option for a growing child (notes, Ch 12); the paper does not mention it.

**Marking points (6):**
1. The flat versus oblique sign, with which teeth are compared.
2. Infraocclusion is not the same as ankylosis.
3. The growth rule in both directions.
4. Why the defect matters (graft; vertical grafting unpredictable).
5. The Patient 2 versus Patient 5 contrast, with ages and outcomes.
6. Both graft-free strategies.

### Q10 (N). Appraise the paper as evidence

Appraise Kokich & Kokich 2006 as evidence: design, bias, measurement, confounding, how well its cited sources support it, and what survives.

**Model answer:**

**Design**
- A Clinician's Corner: expert opinion illustrated by five selected cases.
- There is no methods section, selection rule, outcome definition, follow-up period or failure reporting.
- Oxford CEBM Level 5, or Level 4 at most as a case series; GRADE very low.

**Bias**
- Selection and publication bias: only successes are shown, and all five patients are female (derived). The hardest timing case, a boy whose vertical growth ends at about 21, is not shown.
- Confirmation bias: each case illustrates the framework.
- Five of 22 references include V. Kokich (22.7 %, derived).
- Several key rules have no reference, or rest only on the authors' own review (ref 1):
  - the 2 mm reduction
  - no space maintainers
  - the flat versus oblique bone sign
  - the crowding and profile rule for closure
- Moving the 4 distally into the 5 site rests on the authors' own experience ("on several occasions", p 443). Refs 12 and 13 (by title, space closure in the mandible, ref 13 in the first-molar area) are cited only for pushing a wide root through a narrow ridge.

**Measurement**
- The only measured values are the 13-mm E (method not stated) and the 5-mm overjet.
- Ridge narrowing in Patient 5 and the "sufficient bone" at surgery are described, not measured.
- The superimposition method in Fig 4F is not stated, and Patient 5's superimpositions are cited to Fig 5G, which is a photograph.
- Not reported: vitality tests (Fig 1 says the pulp was not damaged), implant bone levels, survival, follow-up.

**Confounding**
- Every case combines several interventions with growth. Patient 4 was treated between 13 y 8 mo and 17 y 11 mo (tracing labels), so molar protraction, upper incisor change and growth cannot be separated.

**Cited sources against their abstracts (literature, VERIFIED)**
- **Ostler & Kokich:** 25 % within 3 years, then 4 % over the next 3. The paper gives 25 % in 4 years and 30 % by 7, and its reference list titles the study "second molars".
- **Duyck 2001:** a rabbit study in which static load looked like unloaded controls. That shows no harm, not the "stimulation" the paper describes.
- **Fudalej:** published in 2007 under a different title. Its abstract has no 17 or 21 cut-offs.
- **Refs 10 and 11:** by title, these concern augmentation at immediate implants and buccal grafts in the anterior maxilla, not vertical grafts in mandibular premolar sites.

**Internal slips** (raise them as reading questions)
- Patient 3's caption says first-premolar position; the text says second premolar.
- Patient 4's caption says end-to-end; the text says Class II with a 5-mm overjet.
- Patient 5's text cites Fig 3 C-E where Fig 5 is meant.
- 13 − 2 × 2 = 9 mm, not 7 to 8 mm (derived).

**What survives**
- The framework: growth as the clock, the ridge as the asset, profile and crowding as the deciding factors, and interdisciplinary planning.
- Later data support its parts (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Retained Es last: Bjerklin & Bennett 2000, none lost after 20 years; Sletten 2003, 86 % functioning in adults.
  - Moving teeth builds ridge: Eliášová 2014.
  - Removing the lower Es retroclines the lower incisors: Nawaia 2025, 4.5°.
- The trials do not support hemisection over extraction (Abdul Jabbar 2025).

**Marking points (6):**
1. Design and level.
2. At least two biases, with the all-female series.
3. Measurement gaps.
4. Growth as a confounder.
5. At least two cited sources that say less than the text.
6. A balanced verdict.

### Q11 (N). Close the space or keep it?

(a) State the paper's rule for closing a missing lower second premolar space, and the anchorage options. (b) Explain, using Patient 4, why closure can harm the profile. (c) Compare the rule with Proffit and with later evidence, including the hub's Stephens 2005.

**Model answer:**

(a) **The rule (p 443).**
- Close the space if the opposing arch is crowded or the profile is protrusive (ref 19).
- With no crowding and a normal profile, closure could produce an undesirable profile, so it needs extra anchorage:
  - extraoral: a protraction facemask or a chincup (Fig 4)
  - intraoral: miniscrews (ref 20) or mini-implants
- Alternatively, hemisect the E at an early age so that the 6 erupts mesially without moving the lower incisors (refs 21, 22). This is attractive if the child is seen early and monitored regularly.

(b) **Patient 4 (pp 439-440).**
- Her jaws were well related, her incisors were in a normal position and her profile was ideal.
- Extracting the remaining upper 5 and the Es and closing every space would have retracted the lips too far relative to the chin.
- Instead, a chincup and elastics slid the upper and lower 6s forward on a continuous wire. The lower incisors did not move lingually.
- The result took 4 years: Class I molars, no restorations, and the profile kept despite three missing premolars.

(c) **Comparison.**
- **Proffit (notes, Ch 12):**
  - If space, profile and jaws are good or somewhat protrusive, extract the Es at 7 to 9 years and let the 6s drift. The drift is highly variable and unpredictable.
  - Unless all four 5s are missing, opposing-arch extractions may be needed, and unopposed 7s can overerupt.
  - With one missing 5, a restoration is usually indicated unless there is true unilateral space loss or considerable contralateral crowding.
  - Same principle, framed by symmetry and occlusion rather than profile.
- **Proffit (notes, Ch 13):** a chincup mainly rotates the mandible down and back and tends to tip the lower incisors lingually. Patient 4's vertical change was not reported (my read: worth asking).
- **Later evidence (literature, VERIFIED):**
  - Nawaia 2025 (from the split-mouth RCT, overall effect of removing both lower Es, one side hemisected): the lower incisors retroclined 4.5° and moved back 1.8 mm; overjet rose 1.2 mm and overbite 1.5 mm. This is the incisor retraction Kokich warns about.
  - Abdul Jabbar 2025: hemisection left a similar residual space to extraction (2.04 vs 2.39 mm, P = .053), with more complications and cost.
  - Northway (refs 21, 22): hemisection gave less incisor and lip retraction than premolar extractions.
- **Hub:** `2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`.
  - When incisor positions were matched at debond, extraction and nonextraction profiles did not differ about 15 years later.
  - From about 15 to 30 years, the lips fell a further 1.3 to 1.6 mm behind the E-line in both groups.
  - This supports Kokich's premise: what changes the profile is incisor retraction, not the missing tooth. The lips flatten with age anyway, so a normal profile should not be over-retracted.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Both closure indications.
2. Both anchorage families plus hemisection.
3. Patient 4's reasoning and outcome.
4. At least two Proffit points, including symmetry or opposing-arch extractions.
5. At least one later trial result with numbers.
6. The Stephens link, or another profile argument.

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q12 (A). "The low E in a 10-year-old"

**Case.** A 10-year-old girl, Class I molars and canines, straight profile, competent lips, lower incisors upright.
- Upper arch: 2 mm of crowding. Lower arch: none.
- The panoramic shows no lower left second premolar; the lower right 5 is developing normally, and the upper second premolars are present.
- The lower left E is 2 mm below the occlusal plane. On the bitewing, the crest slopes apically toward the E on both its mesial and distal sides.

**Examiner question 1. What is happening to the E, and how sure are you?**

Model answer:
- The oblique bone on both sides of the E confirms ankylosis by Kokich's sign (p 443; compare Fig 2B). A flat crest would have meant it was erupting with its neighbours (Fig 1B).
- Infraocclusion alone would not have convinced me, because Patient 1's E was low but not ankylosed (p 437).
- I would add a percussion test and compare with an earlier film to see whether the step is growing (my read).
- At 10, agenesis is likely, because Proffit says good premolars seldom form after 8 (notes, Ch 12). In girls a late 5 is rare: Aasheim & Ogaard 1993 found late mineralisation by 12 in 2.9 % of girls recorded as missing a 5 at 9, against 11.3 % of boys (literature, VERIFIED). I would still check the film for a late crypt before anything irreversible.

**Examiner question 2. Keep it, extract it, or decoronate it?**

Model answer:
- She is 10, with most of her vertical growth ahead (about 17 for girls, p 441). Kokich would extract the ankylosed E now, before it submerges further and leaves a vertical defect that needs an unpredictable graft (pp 442-443).
- Proffit gives decoronation as the alternative, increasingly when the growth spurt is still ahead (notes, Ch 12). The past exam recalls key this stem both ways (PE3).
- Because no lower crowding needs the space, and an implant is likely, I would discuss both with the surgeon. If the question says "according to Kokich", the answer is extraction.

**Examiner question 3. Will you place a space maintainer after the extraction?**

Model answer:
- No. In most cases Kokich places none, especially when an implant is planned (p 443).
- The first premolar and first molar erupt together, and their eruption repairs the vertical defect, as in Patient 2 (Fig 2D).
- Later I push them apart, and the separating roots build a robust ridge: orthodontic implant-site development.
- Proffit agrees that a maintainer will not stop ridge resorption (notes, Ch 11, 7th-edition box).
- The costs are longer treatment (p 443) and, in a unilateral case, possible midline drift and tipping (Mamopoulou 1996, literature, VERIFIED).

**Examiner question 4. Will you close the space or keep it?**

Model answer:
- The paper closes when the opposing arch is crowded or the profile protrusive (p 443). Her profile is straight and her lower arch uncrowded, so conventional closure would risk retracting the lower incisors and flattening the lips.
- Her upper crowding is only 2 mm, which is not a strong reason to close.
- Proffit says that with a single missing premolar a restoration is usually indicated, unless there is true unilateral space loss or considerable crowding on the other side, and that unilateral closure leaves an occlusion problem in the opposing arch (notes, Ch 12).
- So I keep the space for an implant. If the family refuses an implant, I close with miniscrew anchorage, as in Patient 4's principle (p 443).

**Examiner question 5. When will the implant go in, and how will you hold the space until then?**

Model answer:
- When superimposed serial cephalograms show that ramus growth and vertical facial change have stopped (p 441). That is on average about 17 years in a girl, so about 7 years away (derived).
- Hand-wrist and vertebral staging are not accurate for this (Proffit, notes, Ch 6).
- An implant placed earlier behaves like an ankylosed tooth and ends up below the occlusal plane (Proffit, notes, Ch 19, 7th ed Ch 20).
- I would use structural or cranial-base registration rather than a best-fit method. The hub's `1989-nielsen-maxillary-superimposition-three-methods` shows best-fit superimposition under-reads vertical change.
- I would hold the space with a fixed retainer (Proffit, notes, Ch 18, 7th ed Ch 19). The past exams key a fixed retainer for exactly this "implant in 4 to 5 years" stem. The paper itself says nothing on retention.

**Examiner question 6. At 15 the ridge looks narrow on CBCT. What now?**

Model answer:
- A bone graft is one option, but vertical grafting is often unpredictable and costly (p 442).
- Kokich's alternative is to push the first premolar distally into the second-premolar position and place the implant at the first-premolar site (p 443; Patient 5). The moving root regenerates the deficient ridge.
- Eliášová 2014 measured +28.5 % width and +1.1 mm height with this approach, stable at 2 to 5 years (literature, VERIFIED).
- I would warn that moving a root into a narrowed ridge can be slow (Proffit, notes, Ch 19, 7th ed Ch 20).

**Examiner question 7. Her parents ask for a bridge now instead of waiting.**

Model answer:
- In a young patient, full-coverage abutment preparations risk the pulps and root canal treatment (ref 2).
- Posterior resin-bonded bridges have questionable survival (refs 3-5).
- The paper's first choice is a single-tooth implant once growth has finished (p 441).
- Until then the space is held, and the ridge protected, by the steps above.

**Examiner question 8. What does this paper not tell you?**

Model answer:
- how agenesis was confirmed in Patient 2 at 8 years
- which registration or film interval was used to judge that growth had stopped (it names only serial cephalograms and ramus growth, p 441)
- any measured ridge widths
- vitality testing after narrowing an E
- how long the moved premolar in Patient 5 took
- how Patient 4's overjet closed, and her vertical change
- implant survival, bone levels or follow-up after restoration
- anything on boys, failures, decoronation, autotransplantation or retention
