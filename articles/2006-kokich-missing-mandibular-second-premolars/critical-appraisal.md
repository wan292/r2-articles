# Critical appraisal — Kokich & Kokich 2006

Kokich VG, Kokich VO. Congenitally missing mandibular second premolars: clinical options. *Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop* 2006;130(4):437-44, doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2006.05.025 (volume, issue and pages confirmed on page 1 and the running heads). Two authors: Vincent G. Kokich (Professor) and Vincent O. Kokich (affiliate assistant professor), Department of Orthodontics, School of Dentistry, University of Washington, Seattle. Published in the **Clinician's Corner** section; submitted March 2006, revised and accepted May 2006 (p 437). No funding or conflict-of-interest statement is printed. The structured abstract has four headings (Introduction, Implications, Significance, Purpose) and no Methods or Results. 8 pages, 5 figures (42 panels, derived), 0 tables, 22 references.

The paper has no methods section, no tables, no statistics and no measured outcomes. This appraisal therefore grades it as expert opinion illustrated by five cases, and grades each recommendation by the evidence it cites and the evidence published since. Page numbers are journal pages. Facts about other papers are tagged **VERIFIED** (abstract or primary source seen in this run) or **UNVERIFIED**; their details are in `conflicts.md` (sections B and C). Derived numbers are labelled. The Oxford CEBM 2011 and GRADE-style ratings are my own mapping, not an official grading.

## Verdict in three lines

1. **Design:** a Clinician's Corner, that is, expert guidance built around five selected, successful cases (Figs 1-5): four girls aged 8 y 3 mo to 14 y 6 mo and one adult woman. There is no research question, no case-selection rule, no outcome definition, no follow-up period and no statistics. Its contribution is a decision framework: growth stage, ridge preservation, crowding and profile, and interdisciplinary planning.
2. **Level of evidence:** Oxford CEBM 2011 Level 5 (expert opinion and mechanism-based reasoning; my mapping), Level 4 at most if the five cases are counted as a case series. GRADE-style certainty for each recommendation as the paper presents it: very low. Later cohorts raise the certainty of "keep a healthy E" and "moving teeth build ridge" to low; the one RCT on hemisection lowers the case for it.
3. **Biggest limitation:** the paper's practical rules rest on the authors' experience or their own earlier narrative (ref 1), illustrated only by successes: about 2 mm off each side of the E, no space maintainers, the flat-versus-oblique bone sign, the crowding-and-profile closure rule, and moving a 4 through a narrow ridge. Two cited supports also say less than the text: Ostler's abstract gives different time points, and Duyck's rabbits show that static load is harmless, not that it stimulates bone.

## Design, case selection and the direction of bias

**What kind of paper it is.** A Clinician's Corner: practical guidance from experts, revised and accepted within about two months. Whether it was externally peer-reviewed is not stated (UNVERIFIED). The literature is cited narratively, with no search, no inclusion rule, no risk-of-bias assessment and no statement of the evidence behind each recommendation. Systematic-review items (search, eligibility, synthesis, certainty grading) are not applicable.

**Selection of cases.** All five cases end in success:
- Fig 1: Class I with the reduced E working as a premolar.
- Fig 2: a reopened space and a well-placed implant.
- Fig 3: a normal-width implant crown in Class I.
- Fig 4: Class I with the profile kept and no restorations.
- Fig 5: an implant result described as hard to tell from a natural tooth.

No case shows an E whose roots did not resorb, a ridge that did not regenerate, an implant that ended in infraposition, a chincup side effect, relapse of a closed space, or a hemisection. Each figure shows that a strategy *can* work; none shows how often it does.

**Who is missing.** All five patients are female (5 of 5, derived). None is a boy, although boys' later end of vertical growth (about 21 y as cited, p 441) is the harder timing problem. Only one adult is shown, and she is missing a 6 as well as a 5. No patient has hypodontia beyond three premolars, a Class III or long-face pattern, or a retained E in adulthood.

**Allegiance and support.** 5 of 22 references include V. Kokich (refs 1, 7, 8, 9, 14; 22.7 %, derived), and ref 9 was in press when cited. The two numbers most readers remember, ridge loss and the end of growth, both come from the authors' own work. Several citations are indirect (`conflicts.md` B1-B2):
- Grafting: immediate implants in the maxilla (ref 10) and buccal grafts at upper central incisors (ref 11).
- Moving a root through a narrow ridge: adult first-molar spaces (ref 13) and a study with no abstract (ref 12).
- Loading: splinted full-arch implants (ref 16), monkeys (ref 17) and rabbits (ref 18).
- Closure and miniscrews: single case reports (refs 19, 20).

**Rules with no source at all.** About 2 mm per side and a 7-8 mm crown (p 442, col 1); composite on the cut surfaces (p 442, col 1); no space maintainers in most situations (p 443, col 1); "we performed this type of tooth movement on several occasions" (p 443, col 1); the crowding-and-profile criteria for closure (p 443, col 2; ref 19 is a case report whose abstract does not state them, VERIFIED); the average lower 5 width of 7.5 mm (p 437).

**Blinding, allocation, comparator.** Not applicable: there is no allocation and no comparison group. Every C in the PICO table is implied by the authors' reasoning, never observed.

## Sample, power, attrition

- **Patients:** 5 (pp 437-440). Ages at presentation: 12 y 4 mo, 8 y 3 mo, adult (not given), 13 y 8 mo and 14 y 6 mo. The four young patients average about 12.2 y (derived).
- **Missing teeth in the cases:** 8 second premolars (7 mandibular, 1 maxillary) and 1 mandibular first molar (derived from the case descriptions).
- **Implants:** placed in 3 of 5 patients (2, 3 and 5) and planned for Patient 1 (derived). The age at implant placement is given only for Patient 5 (17 y).
- **Durations:** 4 years of treatment for Patient 4 (p 439, col 2; tracings 13-8 to 17-11, a span of 4 y 3 mo, derived); about 2 y 6 mo from presentation to implant for Patient 5 (derived). No other treatment time is reported.
- **Follow-up after treatment:** not reported for any case. "Long-term" appears only in the citation of Ostler (p 441).
- **Power:** not applicable. With n = 5 heterogeneous cases and no comparator, no treatment effect can be estimated and no failure rate can be bounded in any useful way. For orientation only: 0 failures in 5 has an exact 95 % confidence interval of 0-52 % (derived, Clopper-Pearson), and because the cases were chosen as successes even that interval does not apply.
- **Attrition and unreported sides:** Patient 2 is bilateral, but only one reopened space and one implant are shown (`conflicts.md` A6).

## Confounding and growth

1. **Growth runs through every adolescent case.** In Patient 2 the bone rose as the 4 and 6 erupted, which is the mechanism the paper relies on, but its size cannot be separated from normal alveolar growth without a comparator. In Patient 4 the profile was "maintained" from 13 y 8 mo to 17 y 11 mo, while the nose and chin keep growing (Stephens 2005 in the hub records further soft-tissue change into adulthood). The mechanics cannot be credited with the profile on their own.
2. **Several interventions at once.**
   - Patient 1: reduction, composite and space closure.
   - Patient 2: extraction, drift, incisor proclination and reopening.
   - Patient 4: chincup, elastics and a continuous archwire.
   - Patient 5: extraction, distalisation and a wait for growth to stop.
   No single step can be credited with the result.
3. **Timing of extraction.** Ostler's abstract reports a weak association between ridge change and age at extraction (VERIFIED). Age at extraction, time since extraction and ridge width are not reported for any case.
4. **Sex.** All five patients are girls. Fudalej (VERIFIED) found a sex difference in growth in the second decade, and Aasheim & Øgaard (VERIFIED) found late-forming 5s about four times as often in boys (11.3 % vs 2.9 %). Both affect the decisions the paper illustrates.
5. **Severity.** Degree of infraocclusion, root resorption of the E, ridge width and crowding in millimetres are not given, so mild and severe cases cannot be told apart.
6. **Facial type.** No case is classified as long-, normal- or short-faced, although Heij 2006 (VERIFIED) singles out long and short faces as still at risk of an implant step after 20.
7. **Operator and setting.** Specialist orthodontists (university faculty, p 437) working with surgeons and restorative dentists (p 444, Summary); where the cases were treated is not stated. The results depend on that team.
8. **Era.** Patient 4 was treated before mini-implants were available (p 439), and implant designs and bonded-bridge materials have changed since (Thoma 2017, Jung 2012, VERIFIED).

## External validity: who it does NOT apply to

- **Boys.** All cases are girls. Implant timing in boys (about 21 y as cited) is not illustrated, and late-forming premolars are commoner in boys (Aasheim & Øgaard, VERIFIED).
- **Adults with a long-lost E and an atrophic ridge.** Only one adult is shown (5 and 6 missing). Closing into an old, narrowed ridge is slow (Proffit 6th Ch 19 [7th Ch 20], VERIFIED notes), and adult protraction usually costs crestal bone (Hom & Turley, VERIFIED).
- **Patients with severe hypodontia or oligodontia.** Hvaring's interdisciplinary-clinic sample (8.4 missing teeth on average, VERIFIED) behaves differently, and multiple agenesis changes the space and anchorage arithmetic.
- **Retained Es with advanced root resorption, caries or severe infraocclusion.** The paper gives no criteria for when an E is no longer worth keeping.
- **Class III, open-bite or long-face patients.** The chincup case is a Class II girl with an ideal profile; Proffit Ch 13 (VERIFIED notes) warns that a chin cup rotates the mandible down and back and tips the lower incisors lingually. Long and short faces also carry a late implant-step risk (Heij 2006, VERIFIED).
- **Settings without an implant team, or patients who decline or cannot afford implants.** The framework assumes a surgeon and restorative dentist from the start (p 444).
- **Contemporary alternatives.** Autotransplantation (Josefsson 1999, Andreasen 1990, VERIFIED), decoronation (Proffit Ch 12, VERIFIED notes), modern zirconia or single-retainer bonded bridges (Thoma 2017, VERIFIED) and miniscrew protraction protocols (mentioned, not shown) are outside its scope.
- **Very young children before agenesis is certain.** Patient 2 was 8 y 3 mo; the paper does not say how agenesis was confirmed.

## Strengths

1. A clear, teachable decision framework: keep or close, decided by growth stage, ridge condition, crowding in the opposing arch and the profile, with the surgeon and restorative dentist involved from the start.
2. Implant timing tied to a biological event (the end of vertical facial growth on serial cephalograms), not to chronological age, which Proffit and the published Fudalej data support.
3. A practical, reproducible technique for narrowing the E: measure the CEJ width on a bitewing, mark it on the occlusal surface, cut a wafer mesially and distally, protect the dentine with composite and build up the short crown to prevent overeruption.
4. A simple radiographic rule for ankylosis (flat vs oblique interdental bone), linked to its consequence (a vertical defect) and to an action (extract while growing).
5. The concept of orthodontic implant-site development, later quantified by Eliášová 2014 (ridge width +28.5 %, mostly stable, VERIFIED).
6. Five distinct strategies, each illustrated by a full case: photographs for all five, radiographs for Patients 1-4 (none for Patient 5), a lateral cephalogram for Patient 2 (Fig 2E) and cephalometric tracings for Patient 4 (Fig 4C, 4F). Two past promotion exams (2023, 2024) were built on Fig 1 alone.
7. Candour about costs: longer treatment when teeth are pushed apart, 4 years for Patient 4, and an unopposed upper 7 in Patient 3.
8. Several of its claims were later tested: retained Es (Bjerklin, Sletten), site development (Eliášová) and hemisection (Abdul Jabbar). The first two broadly confirmed them; the hemisection trial did not.

## What would have made it better

1. State how the five cases were chosen, and include at least one failure or complication.
2. Give the age at implant placement, the total treatment time and the post-treatment follow-up for every case.
3. Measure what is asserted: E width (with a scale), millimetres of infraocclusion, ridge width before and after site development, implant bone levels, and the step beside each implant at follow-up.
4. Show Patient 5's superimpositions and state the registration method (`conflicts.md` A8; Nielsen and Nguyen in the hub).
5. Report Patient 4's upper-incisor, overjet and vertical changes, and explain how a chincup protracted the maxillary molars.
6. Report both sides of Patient 2.
7. Give the reduction as a rule (E width at the CEJ minus the target premolar width, split between mesial and distal) rather than as "about 2 mm" (`conflicts.md` A2).
8. Quote Ostler's figures as the source gives them, and describe Duyck's finding as "no harm from static load" rather than "stimulates bone".
9. Add thresholds: how much infraocclusion, how much growth remaining and how much crowding change the decision.
10. Discuss autotransplantation and decoronation, include a boy, and warn that adult eruption can still leave a step beside an implant.
11. Correct the legends (Fig 3 D-E, Fig 5 H-I), the Patient 2 wording "second molars" (p 437) and the citation of Fig 3 for Patient 5, and flag the self-citations.
