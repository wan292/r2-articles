# Exam questions: Kokich 2004, surgical and orthodontic management of impacted maxillary canines

Source: Kokich VG. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2004;126(3):278-83. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2004.06.009. University of Washington.

The paper is an invited narrative review from the AAO/AAOMS Symposium (February 6-8, 2004), illustrated by five single-patient cases (Figs 1 to 5). It has no tables, no sample and no statistics. Level of evidence: Oxford CEBM Level 5, expert opinion.

There are twelve questions:
- Q1 to Q7: single-best-answer MCQs
- Q8 to Q11: short answer
- Q12: case-based oral defence

Tags: R = recall, A = application, N = analysis (3 R, 6 A, 3 N).

Every question is based on a statement in the article. Page numbers are journal pages (278 to 283); figure numbers refer to the case figures. Where the text and a figure legend differ, both are given.

Anything from outside the paper carries a label:
- (literature): VERIFIED means the abstract was read in this run; VERIFIED-secondary means the number was seen only in later papers citing it; UNVERIFIED means not checked.
- (Proffit, notes): verified in the user's Proffit study pages in this run, not checked against the printed book.
- (my read): my own interpretation.

## Reference card

**Prevalence and distribution (p 278)**
- After the third molars, maxillary canines are the most commonly impacted permanent teeth (Bass 1967, ref 1).
- About one third lie labially or within the alveolus, and two thirds palatally (Johnston 1969, ref 2). Palatal to labial is 2 : 1, about 67 % vs 33 % (derived).
- p 281: the palatal canine is the most common impaction "encountered by orthodontists".
- Causes of labial impaction: the crown migrates over the lateral incisor root, or the maxillary dental midline shifts and leaves too little space.

**Interception**
- Williams (ref 3, p 278): extract the primary canine as early as 8 or 9 years for a labial or intra-alveolar canine.
- Olive (ref 4, p 278): open space with routine mechanics, and the canine may erupt spontaneously.
- Ericson & Kurol (ref 10, pp 281-282): if the crown is over the lateral incisor root but not past its mesial surface on periapicals, self-correction after primary canine extraction occurs with "high predictability". Well beyond the mesial surface, it does not occur, and the canine must be uncovered. No percentage is given.

**Labial uncovering: three techniques, four criteria (pp 278-279)**

| Criterion | Finding | Consequence | Figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Labiolingual position | Labial crown (little if any bone over it) | Any of the three techniques | Figs 1-3 |
| | Crown in the centre of the alveolus | Excision and APF more difficult (extensive labial bone removal) | Fig 4 |
| 2. Vertical position vs MGJ | Most of the crown coronal to the MGJ | Any of the three | Fig 1 |
| | Crown apical to the MGJ | Excision inappropriate (no gingiva over the labial surface after eruption) | Figs 2, 3 (as the text cites them) |
| | Crown *significantly* apical | APF also inappropriate (crown instability, possible reintrusion; ref 7). Use closed eruption: adequate gingiva, no long-term reintrusion (ref 8) | Fig 4 |
| 3. Amount of gingiva | Insufficient | APF is the only technique that predictably produces more gingiva | Fig 3 |
| | Enough to leave at least 2 to 3 mm of attached gingiva over the crown after eruption | Any of the three | |
| 4. Mesiodistal position | Crown mesial, over the lateral incisor root | APF (complete exposure); closed eruption or excision generally not recommended | Fig 2 |

**Mechanics (p 279)**
- Mimic normal eruption.
- After closed eruption, erupt the crown into the centre of the alveolar ridge. A ballista loop gives a vertical vector and is simple, convenient and unobtrusive.
- Avoid drawing the tooth labially: risk of bony dehiscence, faster migration of the labial gingival margin, and recession.
- APF on a mesial crown: elastomeric chain toward the centre of the edentulous ridge.

**Cited labial evidence (pp 279-281)**
- Vermette (ref 7), closed eruption vs APF: no significant difference in gingival index, plaque index, pocket depth or bone level.
  - Significant esthetic differences: APF crowns longer than normal (apical migration of the gingival margin); closed-eruption crowns similar to the contralateral teeth.
  - High labial APF canines reintrude.
  - Mechanism: the flap heals to the adjacent mucosa, the mucosa is drawn coronally during eruption, then it pulls the crown apically.
- Becker (ref 8): similar favourable esthetics with closed eruption of maxillary central incisors.

**Localisation (p 281)**
- Buccal object rule (Richards, ref 9): two adjacent periapicals at slightly different horizontal angles.
- A buccal object moves opposite to the beam; a palatal object moves in the same direction.
- Mnemonic SLOB: same lingual, opposite buccal.

**Palatal canine (pp 282-283)**
- Conventional approach: uncover after the first 6 to 9 months of alignment, with space created; traction toward the edentulous site starts soon after surgery.
- The crown is often in intimate contact with the lingual surfaces of the central and lateral incisor roots.
- If the canine does not move, ankylosis is rare (incidence "low", ref 11). The usual problem is too little bone removal: once the follicle is removed, enamel has no cells to resorb bone, so pressure necrosis does it slowly.
- Woloshyn (ref 12): 32 patients, dragged across the lingual surfaces of the lateral roots.
  - Bone more apical on the distal of the lateral incisor and the mesial of the canine than on contralateral controls.
  - Root resorption of the lateral and the canine was typical.
  - Judges could identify the impacted side. The sentence is printed "compared favorably"; in context it reads as "unfavorably".
- Early technique (Kokich & Mathews, ref 11):
  1. Uncover before orthodontic treatment, in some cases in the late mixed dentition.
  2. Raise a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap.
  3. Remove all bone over the crown down to the CEJ.
  4. Reposition the flap with a hole cut through it. Occasionally place a dressing if the canine is high in the palate.
  5. The canine erupts on its own, in about 6 to 8 months to the occlusal plane.
  6. Then bond a bracket and move the root through bone while the crown is translated.
- Schmidt (ref 13, unpublished thesis 2004): bone and attachment levels improved on the canine and lateral; little to no root resorption on the laterals; hard to tell which canine had been impacted.
- Conclusion (p 283): overall treatment time is reduced, and periodontal and esthetic results are superior.

**Figures**

**Not given in the paper:** a sample for any technique; a threshold in mm for "significantly apical"; a priority order among the criteria; an ankylosis rate; the Ericson & Kurol percentage; traction or total treatment time on either palatal pathway.

**Literature to keep beside these:**
- Ericson & Kurol: 91 % normalised with the crown distal to the midline of the lateral root, 64 % mesial to it (VERIFIED-secondary; the same figures are in Proffit, notes, Ch 11).
- Interceptive extraction RCTs: Naoumova 2015, 69 % vs 39 %; Bazargani 2014, 67 % vs 42 % (VERIFIED).
- Open vs closed exposure: Parkin 2013, attachment MD 0.1 mm (95 % CI −0.2 to 0.5); Cochrane 2017, success RR 0.99 (0.93 to 1.06); Björksved 2021, total time MD −0.1 months (95 % CI −3.2 to 2.9, P = .93) (all VERIFIED).
- Schmidt & Kokich 2007, the published thesis: treated canine identified 78.9 % of the time; shorter roots of the treated canine and adjacent lateral; lower crestal bone at the lateral (VERIFIED).

## Part 1. Multiple-choice questions (single best answer)

### Q1 (R). Epidemiology

Which statement about impacted maxillary canines is made in Kokich 2004?

A. They are the most commonly impacted permanent teeth, ahead of the third molars, and two thirds lie labially.
B. After the third molars they are the most commonly impacted permanent teeth; about one third lie labially or within the alveolus and two thirds palatally.
C. About half lie labially and half palatally; labial impaction is caused mainly by retained primary canines.
D. After the mandibular second premolars they are the most commonly impacted teeth; two thirds lie within the alveolus.
E. Palatal impaction is uncommon in orthodontic practice because most palatal canines self-correct after extraction of the primary canine.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- B is p 278 (Bass, ref 1; Johnston, ref 2). Palatal to labial is 2 : 1 (derived).
- p 281 also calls the palatal canine the most common impaction "encountered by orthodontists". That is compatible: it describes the orthodontist's case mix, not the population.
- C is wrong: labial impaction is attributed to the crown migrating over the lateral root, or to a midline shift that leaves too little space (p 278).
- E contradicts p 281 and the limit on self-correction on pp 281-282.
- The paper gives no prevalence percentage. Proffit's figure is 1 to 2 % for canine ectopia or impaction (Proffit, notes, Ch 11).

**Tests:** Recall of rank, distribution and cause.

### Q2 (A). The high, intra-alveolar labial canine

A 13-year-old had space opened for an unerupted maxillary right canine 9 months ago, and the canine has not moved. The primary canine was lost earlier. The crown lies within the alveolus rather than prominent labially, well apical to the MGJ, and not over the lateral incisor root. Which approach does Kokich recommend?

A. Excisional uncovering, then a bracket and an elastomeric chain to the archwire.
B. An apically positioned flap, then labially directed traction to bring the crown out of the alveolus quickly.
C. Closed eruption: elevate a flap, remove enough bone around the crown for it to erupt without impinging on bone, and replace the flap; then apply a vertical force, such as a ballista loop, to erupt the crown into the centre of the alveolar ridge.
D. Closed eruption, then traction directed labially and distally to keep the crown away from the lateral incisor root.
E. No surgery; keep the space open for another 12 months and wait for spontaneous eruption.

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** The sources are criteria 1 and 2 (pp 278-279), the mechanics (p 279) and the Fig 4 legend (p 281), which describes this patient.
- A: excision apical to the MGJ leaves no gingiva over the labial surface.
- B: an APF on a significantly apical crown risks instability and reintrusion (ref 7). An intra-alveolar crown also makes the APF harder, because extensive labial bone must be removed. The labial vector is wrong as well.
- D: drawing the tooth labially risks a bony dehiscence and labial recession (p 279).
- E: opening space "might" allow eruption (Olive, p 278). When it does not, the canine must be uncovered, which is the Fig 4 sequence.

**Tests:** Applying criteria 1 and 2 and the eruption vector.

### Q3 (A). The mesially displaced labial canine

A labially impacted maxillary left canine has its cusp tip above (apical to) the MGJ, and the crown is displaced mesially over the root of the lateral incisor. The teeth have been aligned. Which plan matches Kokich's recommendation?

A. Closed eruption with a ballista loop delivering a purely vertical force.
B. Excisional uncovering of the cusp tip and a bracket bonded at once.
C. An apically positioned flap exposing the whole crown, then an elastomeric chain toward the centre of the edentulous ridge, moving the tooth gradually distally into the arch.
D. Closed eruption with an attachment and chain pulled distally under the replaced flap.
E. Extraction of the lateral incisor and canine substitution.

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:**
- Criterion 4 (p 279): a crown mesial over the lateral root is hard to move through the alveolus unless it is completely exposed with an APF. Closed eruption and excision are generally not recommended, which rules out A, B and D.
- The mechanics are on p 279. The Fig 2 legend (p 280) is this case: left canine, cusp tip above the MGJ and displaced mesially, APF chosen, moved distally after healing.
- The cusp tip above the MGJ also excludes excision (criterion 2). The paper gives no millimetre threshold for "significantly apical", the height at which the APF itself would be excluded, so in this case the mesial position decided (my read).
- E is not discussed in the paper.

**Tests:** Applying criterion 4 when it sits alongside a height problem.

### Q4 (N). Reading Fig 3 against the text

In Fig 3, the crown of a labially impacted maxillary right canine lay between the lateral incisor and the first premolar, partially below the MGJ. Excisional uncovering was rejected. Which statement best explains the decision?

A. The crown was significantly apical to the MGJ, so closed eruption was used to avoid reintrusion.
B. There was insufficient gingiva. An APF was used because it is the only technique that predictably produces more gingiva, and gingiva counts as sufficient only if at least 2 to 3 mm of attached gingiva would remain over the crown after eruption.
C. The crown was mesial over the lateral incisor root, so an APF was needed to move it distally.
D. The crown lay in the centre of the alveolus, so excision would have needed extensive bone removal; a ballista loop was used.
E. Excision needs at least 5 mm of keratinised gingiva before surgery, which was not present.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- The Fig 3 legend (p 280) says excision could not be used because of insufficient gingiva. The mucosa was stained with Schiller's iodine, and an APF exposed the tooth and widened the gingiva. Criterion 3 (p 279) gives the 2 to 3 mm threshold, measured after eruption.
- A: "partially below" in a maxillary legend means partly coronal to the MGJ, because the legends use above/below where the text uses apical/coronal; and an APF, not closed eruption, was used. The text cites Fig 3 under criterion 2 (p 278, apical to the MGJ) and criterion 3 (p 279, insufficient gingiva). The legend's stated reason is the gingiva.
- C describes Fig 2. A crown between the lateral and the premolar is not over the lateral root.
- D describes Fig 4.
- E invents a number.

**Tests:** Analysis: reconciling a legend with the text and naming the deciding criterion.

### Q5 (A). The buccal object rule

Two periapical radiographs of an unerupted maxillary canine are taken at slightly different horizontal angles; for the second, the tube is moved mesially. Relative to the lateral incisor root, the canine crown shifts mesially on the second film. Where is the crown?

A. Labial to the lateral incisor root.
B. Palatal to the lateral incisor root.
C. In the same plane as the lateral incisor root.
D. It cannot be determined without a CBCT.
E. Labial, because buccal objects move in the same direction as the tube.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- p 281, buccal object rule (Richards, ref 9): a buccal object moves opposite to the beam, and a palatal object moves the same way (SLOB). Here the crown moved with the tube, so it is palatal.
- C would show no relative shift (my read).
- E states the rule backwards.
- D: Kokich says the orthodontist must rely on this rule for the labiolingual position. CBCT is now preferred when the views are equivocal, in Proffit (notes, Ch 6, 11, 15) and in Eslami 2017 (literature, VERIFIED: localisation accuracy 50 to 95 % with CBCT vs 39 to 85 % with conventional films). The rule still answers this question.

**Tests:** Applying the parallax rule.

### Q6 (R). Ericson and Kurol as Kokich cites them

According to Kokich's account of Ericson and Kurol (ref 10), when will extraction of the primary canine lead to self-correction of a palatally displaced canine with high predictability?

A. When the crown overlaps the lateral incisor root on periapical radiographs but has not passed its mesial surface.
B. When the crown lies well beyond the mesial surface of the lateral incisor root.
C. When the patient is younger than 8 years.
D. When the extraction is combined with rapid maxillary expansion.
E. When the canine is labial or intra-alveolar and space has been opened with routine mechanics.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A is pp 281-282.
- B: in that position self-correction "does not occur", and the canine must be uncovered and positioned orthodontically.
- C: 8 or 9 years is Williams (ref 3), cited for labial or intra-alveolar canines (p 278).
- E mixes Williams and Olive (p 278).
- D is not in the paper. Araujo 2023 (literature, VERIFIED) found RME better than extraction alone (RR 3.07), at low certainty.
- Kokich gives no percentage and says extraction "will result in normal eruption" (p 281). The source figures are 91 % with the crown distal to the midline of the lateral root and 64 % mesial to it (literature, VERIFIED-secondary; Proffit, notes, Ch 11). The RCTs give 69 % vs 39 % (Naoumova 2015) and 67 % vs 42 % (Bazargani 2014) (literature, VERIFIED). Extraction raises the probability of eruption; it does not guarantee it.

**Tests:** Recall of the threshold, and keeping the three interceptive sources apart.

### Q7 (A). The palatal canine that will not move

A palatally impacted canine was uncovered conventionally after 6 to 9 months of alignment, and traction began soon after surgery. Four months later it has not moved. According to Kokich, what is the most likely explanation?

A. Ankylosis, the commonest cause of failure in palatal canines.
B. Insufficient bone removal. Once the follicle has been removed, the enamel has no cells to resorb bone, so bone left over the crown goes only slowly, by pressure necrosis.
C. The traction force is too light; increasing it will resorb the bone over the crown.
D. The crown is locked against the labial cortical plate.
E. The canine root has dilacerated because the primary canine was extracted late.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- B is p 282.
- A: the incidence of ankylosed maxillary canines is "low" (ref 11).
- C: the paper's mechanism is the absence of resorbing cells in enamel, not the size of the force, and its remedy is adequate bone removal. More force on an enamel-bone contact does not create resorbing cells (my read).
- D: the palatal crown often lies against the lingual surfaces of the central and lateral incisor roots, not the labial plate (p 282).
- E is not in the paper.
- Proffit (notes, Ch 15) gives the same mechanism and adds luxation with immediate loading for partial ankylosis.

**Tests:** Applying the biology of eruption without a follicle.

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q8 (R). Uncovering the labial canine

(a) Name the three techniques for uncovering a labially impacted maxillary canine and the figure that illustrates each. (b) List Kokich's four selection criteria and state what each allows or excludes. (c) State the rules for orthodontic mechanics after closed eruption, and after an APF on a mesially displaced crown.

**Model answer:**

(a) The three techniques:
- excisional uncovering (Fig 1)
- apically positioned flap (Vanarsdall & Corn, ref 5; Figs 2 and 3)
- closed eruption (Kokich & Mathews 1993, ref 6; Fig 4)

(b) The four criteria (pp 278-279):
1. **Labiolingual position.** A labial crown has little or no bone over it, so any technique works. A crown in the centre of the alveolus makes excision and the APF more difficult, because extensive labial bone would have to be removed.
2. **Vertical position relative to the MGJ.**
   - Most of the crown coronal to the MGJ: any technique.
   - Apical to it: no excision, because no gingiva would cover the labial surface after eruption.
   - Significantly apical: no APF either, because of crown instability and possible reintrusion after treatment (ref 7). Closed eruption gives adequate gingiva and does not reintrude in the long term (ref 8).
3. **Amount of gingiva.** If insufficient, only the APF predictably produces more. If enough remains for at least 2 to 3 mm of attached gingiva over the crown after eruption, any technique.
4. **Mesiodistal position.** A crown mesial over the lateral incisor root is hard to move through the alveolus unless completely exposed with an APF. Closed eruption and excision are generally not recommended.

(c) Mechanics (p 279):
- The mechanics should mimic normal eruption.
- After closed eruption, erupt the crown into the centre of the alveolar ridge to produce normal labial gingival relationships. A ballista loop delivers a simple, unobtrusive vertical vector (Fig 4).
- Avoid drawing the tooth labially: risk of bony dehiscence, accelerated migration of the labial gingival margin, and labial recession.
- With an APF on a mesial crown, attach an elastomeric chain directed toward the centre of the edentulous ridge to guide the crown gradually into the arch (Fig 2).

**Marking points (6):**
1. All three techniques named with the correct figures.
2. Criterion 1 with its reason (bone over an intra-alveolar crown).
3. Criterion 2 with both tiers (apical excludes excision; significantly apical excludes the APF too, so closed eruption).
4. Criterion 3 with the 2 to 3 mm threshold measured after eruption, and the APF as the only gingiva-producing technique.
5. Criterion 4 (mesial over the lateral root, so APF).
6. Mechanics: centre of the ridge, a vertical vector, avoid a labial pull, with the stated harms.

### Q9 (A). Two pathways for the palatal canine

Compare the conventional approach to the palatally impacted canine with the early approach of Kokich and Mathews. Cover timing, surgery, mechanics and the outcomes the paper cites for each, and explain why the conventional approach can stall.

**Model answer:**

**Conventional approach (p 282)**
- The canine is uncovered after the first 6 to 9 months of alignment of the maxillary arch. Space is created for the crown, the patient is referred, and soon after surgery the orthodontist starts dragging the crown toward the edentulous site.
- The crown is often in intimate contact with the lingual surfaces of the central and lateral incisor roots.
- If too little bone was removed, the canine stalls. Once the follicle is deflated and removed, the tooth cannot resorb the bone over its crown efficiently, because enamel has no cells to resorb bone.
- The bone eventually goes by pressure necrosis, but slowly, so the canine can look ankylosed. Ankylosis itself is "low" in incidence (ref 11).

**Outcome cited for the conventional approach: Woloshyn (ref 12, p 283)**
- 32 patients, with canines dragged across the lingual surfaces of the lateral roots.
- Bone more apical on the distal of the lateral and the mesial of the canine than on contralateral controls.
- Root resorption of the lateral and the canine was typical.
- Judges could identify the impacted side. The printed "compared favorably" reads as "unfavorably" in context, probably a typesetting slip.

**Early approach (p 283; Fig 5)**
1. Uncover before orthodontic treatment, in some cases in the late mixed dentition.
2. Raise a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap; in Fig 5B the crown was still covered by bone.
3. Remove all bone over the crown down to the CEJ (Fig 5C).
4. Reposition the flap with a hole cut through it over the crown (Fig 5D). Occasionally place a dressing if the canine is high in the palate.
5. The canine erupts without orthodontic force (Fig 5E-F) and reaches the occlusal plane in about 6 to 8 months.
6. Bond a bracket, then move the root through bone as the crown is translated into the arch. In Fig 5G the root was moved labially.

**Outcome cited for the early approach: Schmidt (ref 13, thesis)**
- Bone and attachment levels improved on the canine and lateral.
- Little to no root resorption on the laterals.
- Hard to tell which canine had been impacted (Fig 5H shows the same).

The paper claims that the early approach shortens overall treatment and gives superior periodontal and esthetic results.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Conventional timing (after 6 to 9 months of alignment) and traction soon after surgery.
2. The crown's contact with the incisor roots.
3. The bone-removal biology: no follicle, no resorbing cells in enamel, slow pressure necrosis, ankylosis "low".
4. Woloshyn n = 32 with bone and resorption findings.
5. The early technique steps, including bone to the CEJ, the hole in the flap and the dressing for high canines.
6. 6 to 8 months of autonomous eruption to the occlusal plane, then bracket and root movement, with Schmidt's claimed outcomes.

### Q10 (N). APF or closed eruption for the high labial canine

Kokich advises against an APF for a labial canine significantly apical to the MGJ. (a) Explain the mechanism of relapse he gives. (b) Summarise the evidence he cites for preferring closed eruption. (c) Appraise that evidence, including what the cited sources themselves report.

**Model answer:**

(a) **Mechanism (pp 280-281).** The apically positioned flap heals to the mucosa next to the impacted tooth. As the tooth is erupted, that mucosa is drawn coronally. After treatment, the mucosal attachment pulls the crown back apically, so the canine reintrudes. This was not observed with closed eruption.

(b) **Cited evidence.**
- Vermette (ref 7, pp 279-281): no significant difference between the techniques in gingival index, plaque index, pocket depth or bone level, but significant esthetic differences.
  - APF crowns were longer than normal, because the gingival margin migrated apically.
  - Closed-eruption crowns matched the contralateral teeth.
  - High labial APF canines reintruded.
- Becker (ref 8, p 281): similar favourable esthetics with closed eruption. Ref 8 is also cited for the claim that closed eruption does not reintrude in the long term (p 279).

(c) **Appraisal.**
- **Design.** The paper reports no numbers. Vermette is a retrospective, contralateral-controlled study co-authored by Kokich.
- **Vermette's abstract (literature, VERIFIED).**
  - Sample: 30 labially impacted maxillary *anterior* teeth (APF 18, closed eruption 12), not canines alone; recall at least 3 months after treatment.
  - APF teeth vs the contralateral tooth: more apical margins, greater crown length, worse facial attachment level, wider facial attached gingiva, worse probing bone level at three sites, scarring, and vertical relapse.
  - Closed-eruption teeth vs the contralateral tooth: less distal attached gingiva and worse facial probing bone level.
  - Both groups: shorter roots.
  - So "no difference between techniques" holds for the indices, but both techniques cost some bone. The wider gingiva with the APF fits criterion 3.
- **Becker (literature, VERIFIED).**
  - Sample: 21 maxillary central incisors, median 4.5 years after retention.
  - Bone support reduced by 5 to 6 %; abnormal gingival contour in one third.
  - The abstract does not measure reintrusion, so the long-term claim for canines is extrapolated from incisors.
- **The algorithm.** It has no millimetre threshold for "significantly apical" and no priority among the criteria. Figs 2 and 4 are both "above" the MGJ, yet were treated differently.
- **Later systematic reviews (literature, VERIFIED).**
  - Incerti-Parenti 2016: no canine-only closed-eruption study; evidence insufficient.
  - Xenou 2025: closed eruption and APF are similar to untreated teeth; excision had the worst soft-tissue outcomes, though the differences were not clinically significant.
- **What survives.** Avoid excision without gingiva, and expect long crowns and vertical relapse after an APF on a high canine. The periodontal differences are small. The evidence is Level 5 opinion resting on small retrospective cohorts.

**Marking points (6):**
1. The relapse mechanism (healing to mucosa, coronal drag, apical pull).
2. Vermette's indices vs esthetics, with the direction of each.
3. Becker as incisor evidence.
4. Vermette's mixed anterior-tooth sample, and bone or root costs in both groups.
5. No threshold or priority in the algorithm.
6. A level-of-evidence statement, with what later reviews support.

### Q11 (N). Appraise the palatal conclusion

The paper concludes that early uncovering with autonomous eruption reduces overall treatment time and gives superior periodontal and esthetic results (p 283). Appraise this claim: design, evidence, confounding, and what later studies found.

**Model answer:**

**Design.** An invited narrative symposium paper, Oxford Level 5. It has no methods section and no sample for the early technique; one illustrative case is shown (Fig 5).

**Evidence behind the claim**
- The time claim has no cited source. The paper gives 6 to 9 months of alignment before conventional uncovering and 6 to 8 months of autonomous eruption, but no traction or total time for either pathway. A saving therefore cannot be derived.
- The Fig 5 legend says the canine was uncovered before orthodontic treatment to reduce time in appliances. Yet Fig 5E-F show brackets on the adjacent teeth while the canine erupts (checked on the page image).
- The periodontal and esthetic claim rests on Schmidt's thesis, unpublished in 2004, with no n or numbers stated. It is compared with Woloshyn's conventional cohort (n = 32). That is a historical comparison: different eras, operators and selection.

**Confounding**
1. Early-uncovered canines belong to younger patients in the mixed dentition, who have more eruptive potential.
2. The protocol changes two things at once: the timing, and the surgery and mechanics (bone removed to the CEJ, autonomous eruption before traction). The paper itself blames conventional failure on too little bone removal (p 282), so the effect of timing cannot be separated.
3. The severity of impaction (depth, angulation, sector) is not described.

**Later studies (literature, all VERIFIED)**
- **Schmidt & Kokich 2007**, the published thesis. Contralateral-controlled, 16 unilateral cases.
  - Treated canine and adjacent lateral roots shorter than controls.
  - Lower crestal bone mesial and distal of the lateral.
  - The treated canine identified 78.9 % of the time.
  - Concluded: better for the canine than closed exposure with early traction, similar for the adjacent teeth. The authors asked for a randomised comparison.
  - Whether the samples are identical to the thesis is UNVERIFIED.
- **Parkin 2013 RCT:** attachment MD 0.1 mm (95 % CI −0.2 to 0.5), open vs closed.
- **Cochrane 2017:** success RR 0.99 (0.93 to 1.06); no differences in esthetics, pain, periodontal health or time; low-quality evidence.
- **Björksved 2021 RCT, Kokich-like open technique:** total treatment time MD −0.1 months (95 % CI −3.2 to 2.9, P = .93).

**What survives**
- The biological rationale holds: remove the bone to the CEJ, and let the canine erupt vertically away from the incisor roots before translating it.
- The outcomes of the early technique are acceptable.
- "Superior" and "reduced time" are not supported. The best evidence shows open and closed exposure as roughly equivalent.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Level 5 design with no sample for the claim.
2. The time claim has no source and no derivable numbers (and the Fig 5E-F brackets).
3. Schmidt unpublished, and the Woloshyn comparison historical.
4. At least one confounder (age or root development, or the two simultaneous changes).
5. At least two later results with numbers.
6. A balanced verdict (rationale holds, superiority does not).

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q12 (A). "Two canines, two problems"

**Case.** A 12-year-old girl, Class I, mild crowding, late mixed dentition. Both maxillary primary canines are retained and firm.
- Right side: on two periapicals the crown is buccal to the lateral incisor root but sits within the alveolus rather than prominent labially, well above the MGJ, and not over the lateral incisor root. The band of attached gingiva in the region is narrow.
- Left side: no labial bulge. The crown is palatal and lies well beyond the mesial surface of the left lateral incisor root. The lateral incisor roots look intact on the periapicals.

**Examiner question 1. How did you decide which canine is labial and which palatal, and is that enough?**

Model answer:
- I used the buccal object rule: two adjacent periapicals at slightly different horizontal angles (p 281).
- The right crown moved opposite to the tube shift, so it is buccal. The left crown moved with the tube, so it is palatal (SLOB).
- Kokich relies on this for the labiolingual position. Today I would add a small-field CBCT for the left canine, because it is palatal and close to the incisor roots and resorption has to be excluded. That follows Proffit (notes, Ch 6 and 15; impacted teeth are one of the four justified CBCT indications) and Eslami 2017 (literature, VERIFIED).

**Examiner question 2. Should we just extract the primary canines and wait?**

Model answer:
- **Left (palatal).** By Kokich's account of Ericson and Kurol, a crown well beyond the mesial surface of the lateral root does not self-correct after primary canine extraction. It must be uncovered surgically and brought in orthodontically (pp 281-282). Later work is less absolute, but the further mesial the crown, the lower the odds. Naoumova 2015 Part II found that canines with the cusp tip about 6 mm from the midline, about 5 mm from the arch plane, or a mesioangular angle of about 116° needed exposure despite extraction (literature, VERIFIED).
- **Right (labial).** The paper gives two non-surgical options: Williams' extraction of the primary canine, proposed as early as 8 or 9 years (p 278; no upper age is given, and she is 12), and opening space with routine mechanics (Olive, p 278). If these do not work, the labial canine is uncovered surgically.

**Examiner question 3. Which uncovering technique for the right canine, and how will you move it?**

Model answer:
- Closed eruption (Fig 4). The crown is intra-alveolar, so excision and the APF are more difficult, because extensive labial bone might need to be removed (criterion 1). It is well apical to the MGJ, so excision gives no labial gingiva and an APF risks instability and reintrusion (criterion 2). It is not mesial over the lateral root, so criterion 4 does not demand an APF.
- Surgery: elevate a flap, remove enough bone around the crown for it to erupt without impinging on bone, and replace the flap.
- Mechanics: a vertical force into the centre of the ridge, for example a ballista loop. Avoid pulling it labially, because of the risk of dehiscence and recession (p 279).

**Examiner question 4. The gingiva on the right is narrow. Doesn't criterion 3 force an APF?**

Model answer:
- This is where the criteria collide. The paper gives no order of priority.
- Criterion 3 says the APF is the only technique that predictably produces more gingiva.
- Criterion 2 says an APF on a significantly apical crown risks reintrusion, and that in that situation closed eruption "will provide adequate gingiva" over the crown (p 279).
- I would follow the height rule and use closed eruption, erupting the canine through the crest into the centre of the ridge. That is the same principle as Proffit's statement that the tooth should erupt through attached gingiva, not mucosa (Proffit, notes, Ch 15).
- I would monitor the gingiva and refer for grafting if the band ends up under 2 to 3 mm. The grafting step is my read; the paper does not discuss it.

**Examiner question 5. How and when would Kokich uncover the left canine?**

Model answer:
1. Early, before full appliances, which fits her late mixed dentition (p 283).
2. Raise a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap.
3. Remove all bone over the crown down to the CEJ.
4. Reposition the flap with a hole cut through it over the crown, plus a dressing if the canine is high in the palate.
5. Let the canine erupt on its own, about 6 to 8 months to the occlusal plane.
6. Bond a bracket and move the root through bone as the crown is translated into the arch (Fig 5).

The rationale: avoid dragging the crown across the lingual surfaces of the lateral roots. Woloshyn's 32 patients with dragged canines had more apical bone on the distal of the lateral and the mesial of the canine, and typical root resorption (p 283).

**Examiner question 6. Four months after uncovering, the left canine has not moved. Is it ankylosed?**

Model answer:
- Probably not: Kokich calls ankylosis "low" in incidence (p 282).
- The usual cause is bone left over the crown. With the follicle gone, enamel has no cells to resorb bone, and pressure necrosis is slow (p 282).
- I would look for signs of ankylosis (percussion note, anchor teeth moving) and check the bone over the crown on CBCT. If bone remains, I would ask for re-exposure with bone removed to the CEJ.
- Proffit (notes, Ch 15) adds that a partially ankylosed canine is luxated and loaded at once.

**Examiner question 7. The CBCT shows early resorption on the distal of the left lateral incisor root. Does that change your plan?**

Model answer:
- Yes. Kokich's 6 to 8 months of autonomous eruption was described for displaced canines, not for active resorption.
- Proffit (notes, Ch 11 and the Ch 12 7th-edition box) says to expose the canine and move it away from the root promptly. That stops the resorption but does not reverse it.
- I would expose it and apply traction directed away from the lateral root, then monitor the root (my read on sequencing).
- Ericson and Kurol's 1988 resorption study (literature, VERIFIED) found resorption about three times more common in girls, with the risk highest when the cusp lies medial to the midline of the lateral root.

**Examiner question 8. Her parents ask whether the early open method is better than the closed method their friend's child had. What do you tell them?**

Model answer:
- Kokich's claim that it shortens treatment and gives superior periodontal and esthetic results is expert opinion (Level 5). It rests on an unpublished thesis and a comparison with an older cohort (p 283).
- The randomised trials since then found the two methods about equal (literature, VERIFIED):
  - success: Cochrane 2017, RR 0.99
  - gum attachment: Parkin 2013, difference 0.1 mm
  - total treatment time: Björksved 2021, −0.1 months
  - cost: equal in Björksved
- Pain differs rather than favouring one method: the closed method hurt more during traction in Björksved, and the open method scored higher on a pain VAS in Færøvig 2025 (literature, VERIFIED from a partial abstract).
- So the choice depends on the depth of the canine, the surgeon's experience and the family's preference. Both work, and neither is clearly superior.

**Examiner question 9. What does this paper not tell you?**

Model answer:
- A millimetre threshold for "significantly apical", or which criterion wins when they conflict.
- The Ericson and Kurol success rate.
- The ankylosis rate.
- Any traction or total treatment time.
- The sample size or numbers behind Schmidt's thesis.
- The age or root stage at which autonomous eruption stops working.
- How to manage a canine already resorbing an incisor root.
- Anything about mandibular or transmigrated canines, adults, or dilacerated teeth.

**Inconsistencies in the paper an examiner may raise** (all visible in the paper itself; phrase each as a reading question):
1. p 283 says judges could identify the impacted canine because its tissue "compared favorably" with the control; in context it reads as "unfavorably".
2. The text cites Fig 3 for both a crown apical to the MGJ (p 278) and insufficient gingiva (p 279), while the Fig 3 legend describes the crown as partially below (partly coronal to) the MGJ and gives only the gingiva reason for rejecting excision.
3. The legends use "above/below" where the text uses "apical/coronal".
4. Figs 2 and 4 are both "above" the MGJ, yet were treated with an APF and closed eruption respectively. There is no threshold for "significantly apical" and no priority when criteria conflict.
5. The long-term "no reintrusion" claim for closed eruption is cited to Becker, which concerns central incisors (ref 8).
6. Olive (ref 4), whose title concerns palatal canines, is cited in the labial section.
7. Early uncovering is described as "in some cases" in the late mixed dentition (p 283, column 1), then generalised to "during the mixed dentition" in the conclusion.
8. The Fig 5 legend says the canine was uncovered before orthodontic treatment, but panels E-F show brackets on the adjacent teeth.
9. Extraction "will result in normal eruption" (p 281) against "high predictability" (p 282).
10. The time and superiority claims have no numbers behind them.

None of these changes the labial algorithm, which is the paper's lasting contribution. The palatal superiority claim is the part later trials did not confirm.
