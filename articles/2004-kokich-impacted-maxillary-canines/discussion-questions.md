# Questions to ask about this paper

Kokich VG. Surgical and orthodontic management of impacted maxillary canines. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2004;126(3):278-83. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2004.06.009. University of Washington. An invited symposium paper (AAO/AAOMS Symposium, Palm Springs, February 6-8, 2004) in the section "Advances in Orthodontics & Dentofacial Surgery". It is a narrative expert review illustrated by five single-patient cases (Figs 1 to 5) and has no data of its own.

The paper has no tables, no sample, no measurements and no statistics. Good questions about it are therefore not "what did they find". They are "where does the rule break" and "what was the rule built on". The four criteria for labial canines are the part everyone uses. The palatal recommendation (uncover early, remove the bone down to the CEJ and let the canine erupt on its own) is the part that later trials tested. You get your edge in the room by reading the figure legends against the text and by knowing what the cited sources actually say.

Every page reference below was checked on the page images. Anything from outside the paper is labelled (literature), (Proffit, notes) or (my read). "Proffit, notes" means VERIFIED in the user's Proffit study pages in this run but not checked against the printed book. Literature items are VERIFIED (abstract read in this run), VERIFIED-secondary (number seen only in later papers citing it) or UNVERIFIED.

How to ask: the session is a casual discussion, so keep each question to one breath and open with the figure or page ("In Fig 3 ..."). Frame any wording problem as "did I misread it?". Kokich trained a generation of orthodontists, and some attendings will teach his algorithm word for word. Let the legends and the follow-up papers make the point, not you.

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "In the second criterion, a crown apical to the MGJ rules out excision, and a crown *significantly* apical rules out the APF as well, because of reintrusion. But in Fig 2 the cusp tip is above the MGJ and he still chose an APF, because the crown was mesial over the lateral root. Where do you draw the line between apical and significantly apical? And when the height and the mesiodistal position pull opposite ways, which one wins?"
- **Why it lands:** You have read the text (pp 278-279) against two legends. Fig 2 (p 280): cusp tip above the MGJ and displaced mesially, so APF. Fig 4 (p 281): crown above the MGJ and in the alveolus, so closed eruption. Two legends say "above", yet the techniques differ. In Fig 4 the legend gives height plus intra-alveolar position (criteria 1 and 2), and the text cites Fig 4 as the "significantly apical" crown; in Fig 2 mesial displacement (criterion 4) decided. The paper gives no millimetre cut-off and no order of priority.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Clinical judgement." Some will judge height against the lateral incisor root on the radiograph or the CBCT. Some will say the mesial crown wins, because the tooth cannot be steered past the lateral root blind. Proffit (notes, Ch 15) describes a flap from the crest that carries gingiva for an apical labial tooth, and a tunnel approach for a very high labial canine. That approach is close to Kokich's closed eruption.
- **Follow-up:** "For a crown that is both high and mesial, would anyone do closed eruption and direct the chain distally and toward the ridge first?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What are the four criteria?" They are on pp 278-279.

### Q2. "The Fig 3 legend says the crown was partly below the MGJ, which in the maxilla means partly coronal, and that excision was rejected because there was too little gingiva. The text cites Fig 3 twice, once for a crown apical to the MGJ and once for insufficient gingiva. When height and gingiva both point away from excision, which one do you write in the referral? And how do you judge, before the tooth is out, that there is enough gingiva to leave 2 to 3 mm attached after eruption?"
- **Why it lands:** The text cites Fig 3 under criterion 2 (p 278, apical to the MGJ) and criterion 3 (p 279, insufficient gingiva); the legend (p 280) gives only the gingiva reason. The legends say "above/below" where the text says "apical/coronal", and in the maxilla above means apical. The 2 to 3 mm threshold is a number measured *after* eruption (p 279), so choosing the technique means predicting it. In Fig 3B the mucosa was stained with Schiller's iodine. The legend does not say why; my read is that it marks the MGJ, because iodine stains the glycogen in non-keratinised mucosa and not keratinised gingiva (UNVERIFIED).
- **What you will probably hear:** Most will estimate it by eye, with a roll test or with iodine. They will treat gingiva width as a criterion that can override the others, and they may note that a crown sitting between the lateral and the premolar often has a narrow band.
- **Follow-up:** "If the band is narrow but the crown is high, the two rules collide. Criterion 3 says APF and criterion 2 says closed eruption. On p 279 he says closed eruption 'will provide adequate gingiva' in the high case. Is that enough for you?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is Schiller's iodine for?"

### Q3. "On p 283 the Woloshyn sentence says judges could identify the previously impacted canine because its tissue 'compared favorably' with the other side. I read that as 'unfavorably', since otherwise they could not have told the sides apart. Did I misread it?"
- **Why it lands:** The two halves of the sentence point in opposite directions, and the meaning of the esthetic finding depends on the word. Woloshyn's abstract (literature, VERIFIED) has the impacted side identified on slides in about 75 % of cases (32 patients, mean age 22 y 11 m, mean 3 y 7 m after treatment). "Unfavorably" fits that. Asked this way, it reads as a typesetting slip, which it probably is.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Typo; the message is that dragged canines look different afterwards." Someone may add that the bone loss and resorption findings matter more than the photographs.
- **Follow-up:** "With chance at 50 %, is about 75 % identification a big esthetic difference, or a modest one?"
- **Avoid saying:** "There is a mistake in the paper." Ask about your reading, not about the paper.

### Q4. "He cites Ericson and Kurol as 'not past the mesial surface' of the lateral root and says extraction 'will result in normal eruption'. Proffit gives their rule by the midline of the root, 91 % if less than half is overlapped and 64 % if more. Which landmark do you use? And what do you tell the parents: 'will', or 'probably'?"
- **Why it lands:** You are comparing pp 281-282 with Proffit (notes, Ch 11). Ericson & Kurol's 91 % vs 64 % is VERIFIED-secondary: the 1988 abstract is not on PubMed, and the figures come from later papers that cite it. The randomised trials put the effect lower than "will" (literature, VERIFIED):
  - Naoumova 2015: spontaneous eruption 69 % with extraction vs 39 % without at 24 months.
  - Bazargani 2014: 67 % vs 42 % at 18 months.
  
  About one canine in three still fails after extraction. Kokich also reads the position on periapicals, whereas Proffit's rule uses the panoramic.
- **What you will probably hear:** Extract anyway, because it is cheap and low-risk; age 10 to 13; check again at 6 to 12 months. Someone may add space maintenance: Bazargani lost arch perimeter on the extraction side and recommends a palatal arch (literature, VERIFIED).
- **Follow-up:** "When you extract the primary canine for this, do you hold the space with a palatal arch?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Should we extract the primary canine?"

## Tier 2. Mechanics and biology

### Q5. "He says a palatal canine that will not move is usually not ankylosed. The follicle is gone, enamel has no cells to resorb bone, and any bone left over the crown goes only slowly, by pressure necrosis. After three months of traction with nothing happening, how do you tell too little bone removal from ankylosis? What do you do next?"
- **Why it lands:** You are asking for the clinical test behind p 282's mechanism, where ankylosis is called "low" incidence with no number. Proffit (notes, Ch 15) gives the same biology: the exposed crown has lost its follicle cells, so enough bone must be removed. Proffit adds that a wire ligature around the crown raises the risk of ankylosis, and that a partially ankylosed tooth should be luxated and loaded at once.
- **What you will probably hear:** Signs of ankylosis: a high-pitched percussion note, anchor teeth intruding or tipping toward the canine, CBCT. Then re-exposure to clear bone, then luxation if it still does not move.
- **Follow-up:** "If you re-expose, do you ask the surgeon to take the bone down to the CEJ, as in Fig 5C?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why do palatal canines move slowly?" p 282 answers it.

### Q6. "He explains APF relapse this way: the flap heals to the adjacent mucosa, the mucosa is pulled coronally as the tooth erupts, and after treatment that attachment pulls the crown back up. If we inherit a high labial canine that was already uncovered with an APF, can anything prevent the relapse: a longer bonded retainer, a fiberotomy, mucogingival surgery?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns the mechanism on pp 280-281 into a management question the paper does not answer. Vermette's abstract (literature, VERIFIED) found the following on APF teeth compared with the contralateral tooth:
  - more apical gingival margins
  - greater crown length
  - gingival scarring
  - vertical relapse on photographs
- **What you will probably hear:** A bonded retainer, slight overcorrection, perhaps a periodontal referral. No one will cite data, because the paper gives none.
- **Follow-up:** "And the opposite error: a closed-eruption canine pulled labially. He warns of dehiscence and recession. Do we still use a ballista loop, or a cantilever spring?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is an apically positioned flap?"

### Q7. "Woloshyn's canines were dragged across the lingual surfaces of the lateral roots. With early uncovering the canine first erupts vertically by itself, and only when it reaches the occlusal plane is the root moved labially (Fig 5G). Is the benefit really the timing? Or is it that the bone was removed to the CEJ, so the tooth comes out of the bone before it is moved sideways?"
- **Why it lands:** The early protocol changes two things at once:
  - the timing: before appliances, in some cases in the late mixed dentition
  - the surgery and the mechanics: all bone removed to the CEJ, then autonomous eruption before any traction (pp 282-283)
  
  On p 282 the paper itself blames the conventional failure on too little bone removal, so the two changes cannot be separated (my read).
- **What you will probably hear:** "Both." Autonomous eruption takes the crown away from the incisor roots before anyone pulls on it. Younger patients also have more eruptive potential. Proffit (notes, Ch 15) agrees that a palatal canine often drifts down after open exposure, so traction can wait, but adds that spontaneous movement is rare once the root is complete.
- **Follow-up:** "So in a 15-year-old with a complete root, would a conventional exposure with the bone taken to the CEJ and a wait before traction do just as well?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why uncover early?"

### Q8. "The Fig 5 legend says the canine was uncovered before orthodontic treatment, to reduce time in appliances. In Fig 5E and F, though, the other teeth already have brackets while the canine erupts. Do we band the arch during the 6 to 8 months of autonomous eruption? If we do, where is the time saved?"
- **Why it lands:** The panels (p 282, checked on the page image) do not match the legend. The time claim in the conclusion (p 283) has no source. The paper gives 6 to 9 months of alignment before conventional uncovering (p 282) and 6 to 8 months of autonomous eruption (p 283), but no total for either pathway, so the saving is plausible but not quantified. In Björksved 2021, a multicentre RCT that used a Kokich-like open technique (literature, VERIFIED), total treatment time differed by −0.1 months (95 % CI −3.2 to 2.9, P = .93).
- **What you will probably hear:** Many arches need alignment or space anyway, so brackets go on regardless. The saving comes from not dragging a canine through bone.
- **Follow-up:** "Does an autonomously erupting canine need its space opened first? Fig 5 does not say."
- **Avoid saying:** "How long does it take to erupt?" The answer is 6 to 8 months (p 283).

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q9. "The early-uncovering recommendation rests on Schmidt's thesis, which was unpublished in 2004. The published version, Schmidt and Kokich 2007, reports shorter roots on the treated canine and the adjacent lateral than on controls, and lower crestal bone at the lateral. Orthodontists identified the treated canine 78.9 % of the time, and the paper concludes that the adjacent teeth fare about the same as with closed exposure. How should we cite the palatal conclusion today?"
- **Why it lands:** You followed the thesis through to its peer-reviewed version (literature, VERIFIED abstract: 16 unilateral cases analysed against the contralateral canine, mean 2 y 11 m after treatment). The 2007 paper still found advantages *for the canine* over closed exposure with early traction, and its authors asked for a randomised comparison. Whether the thesis sample and the 2007 sample are identical is UNVERIFIED. Ask it this way and it becomes a question about how evidence matures, not about the author.
- **What you will probably hear:** A thesis abstract is preliminary. The canine benefit held up, and the claims about the lateral did not. The 2004 paper is still taught for the technique.
- **Follow-up:** "So is the honest summary 'better for the canine, neutral for the lateral'?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did he overstate his own thesis?" That is an accusation, and you cannot know which sample the 2004 sentence summarised.

### Q10. "He concludes that early open exposure shortens treatment and gives superior periodontal and esthetic results. Since then, Parkin 2013 found a 0.1 mm attachment difference between open and closed exposure, the 2017 Cochrane review a success RR of 0.99, and Björksved 2021 a total-time difference of −0.1 months. Is open exposure with autonomous eruption still our default here, and what decides it for you?"
- **Why it lands:** Each result is VERIFIED from its abstract:
  - Parkin 2013 (RCT, 62 analysed): attachment MD 0.1 mm (95 % CI −0.2 to 0.5).
  - Cochrane 2017 (3 RCTs, 146 participants): success RR 0.99 (0.93 to 1.06); no difference in esthetics, pain, periodontal health or treatment time; low-quality evidence.
  - Björksved 2021 (multicentre RCT): total treatment time MD −0.1 months (95 % CI −3.2 to 2.9, P = .93).
  
  Two caveats keep you fair:
  - Björksved's open arm used a flap, bone removal and autonomous eruption, but exposure was not necessarily done before appliances in the late mixed dentition.
  - The details of Parkin's open-arm protocol are UNVERIFIED.
  
  The trials test "open vs closed", which is close to Kokich's comparison, but not identical to it.
- **What you will probably hear:** Surgeon preference, depth in the palate (Kokich uses a dressing only "occasionally" for a high canine) and patient comfort. In Björksved the closed group had more pain during traction, and Færøvig 2025 (a two-centre RCT) found more pain on a VAS with open exposure (literature, VERIFIED from a partial abstract).
- **Follow-up:** "Björksved 2026 found that orthodontists rated closed-technique canines more appealing at 4 years (OR 4.3, 95 % CI 1.8 to 10.2). Does that reverse the esthetic argument?" (literature, VERIFIED)
- **Avoid saying:** "Which is better, open or closed?"

### Q11. "For labial canines the reintrusion warning rests on Vermette, whose abstract describes 30 labially impacted maxillary anterior teeth, not canines alone. The long-term 'no reintrusion' claim for closed eruption is cited to Becker, which studied central incisors. Does incisor evidence carry over to a longer-rooted canine at the corner of the arch?"
- **Why it lands:** Citation scope (p 279; ref 8 title; p 281).
  - Vermette (literature, VERIFIED): APF 18, closed eruption 12, recall at least 3 months after treatment. Both groups showed worse probing bone levels than the contralateral tooth, at more sites with the APF, and both had shorter roots. That fits "no difference between techniques" but not "no harm".
  - Becker (literature, VERIFIED): 21 central incisors; bone support reduced 5 to 6 %; abnormal gingival contour in one third.
  - Incerti-Parenti 2016, a systematic review (literature, VERIFIED): no canine-only closed-eruption study; evidence insufficient.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The biology is the same." The canine's root length and its prominence at the corner of the arch may in fact make the long-crown problem more visible.
- **Follow-up:** "Is a 3-month recall long enough to see reintrusion?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Was Vermette a good study?"

## Tier 4. Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "He relies on the buccal object rule with two periapicals to get the 'exact' labiolingual position. Proffit lists impacted teeth as one of four justified CBCT indications and suggests a panoramic first, then small-field CBCT. For the exam, and for the referral letter to the surgeon, is SLOB still enough?"
- **Why it lands:** p 281 against Proffit (notes, Ch 6, 11, 15). A parallax shift gives relative position, not depth or the state of the incisor root (my read). In a systematic review, Eslami 2017 (literature, VERIFIED) found:
  - localisation accuracy of 50 to 95 % for CBCT and 39 to 85 % for conventional radiography
  - treatment-planning kappa between modalities of 0.36 to 0.72
  - a recommendation of CBCT for difficult cases
- **What you will probably hear:** SLOB first, then CBCT when the views disagree, when the canine is palatal and close to the incisor roots, or when resorption is suspected.
- **Follow-up:** "Do we take a CBCT for every impacted canine here, or only for those?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What does SLOB stand for?"

### Q13. "He cites Williams for extracting the primary canine as early as 8 or 9 years for labial or intra-alveolar canines, and Ericson and Kurol for palatal canines. Proffit's trigger is about age 10, with no bulge and a firm primary canine. For the exam, which age do I quote? And does the labial versus palatal split come from the sources?"
- **Why it lands:** p 278 and pp 281-282 against Proffit (notes, Ch 11). The literature (all VERIFIED):
  - Williams' abstract: intervene from dental age 8; selective primary canine removal in **uncrowded Class I** cases. It is not restricted to labial canines.
  - Bazargani 2014: extraction works better in younger patients.
  - Cobourne 2025 guideline: a favourable outcome needs age 10 to 13, the mixed dentition, space, and a crown not beyond the midline of the lateral root.
- **What you will probably hear:** Palpate from 9 to 10, extract between 10 and 13 if the canine is mesial and space is available, and gain space if the arch is crowded. That fits Olive's space-opening point (p 278).
- **Follow-up:** "In a crowded arch, is extraction alone enough? Araujo's 2023 network meta-analysis found every interceptive option beat no treatment, at low certainty." (literature, VERIFIED)
- **Avoid saying:** "When do we extract the primary canine?"

### Q14. "Proffit's 7th edition says laser exposure suits a labial canine with adequate attached tissue, and that the tooth should erupt through attached gingiva, not mucosa. That is Kokich's second and third criteria in one line. Yet Proffit also says that once the lateral root is resorbing you expose the canine and move it away now, while Kokich's early approach waits 6 to 8 months for autonomous eruption. With active resorption, which do you follow?"
- **Why it lands:** One point of agreement and one point of tension, both from Proffit (notes: Ch 15 "Surgical exposure" and its 7th-edition box on revised criteria; Ch 11 and the Ch 12 7th-edition box on complicated ectopic eruption) against pp 278-279 and p 283. Proffit says that moving the canine away stops resorption but does not reverse it.
- **What you will probably hear:** With active resorption, expose and move the canine away now, with traction directed away from the root. Kokich's wait suits a canine that is displaced but has not yet resorbed anything.
- **Follow-up:** "Ericson and Kurol's 1988 resorption paper put the risk where the cusp lies medial to the midline of the lateral root. Is that the same zone where extraction stops working?" (literature, VERIFIED)
- **Avoid saying:** "What does Proffit say about impacted canines?"

## Do not ask these

1. "What are the three techniques and the four criteria?" They are on pp 278-279, with a figure for each.
2. "What proportion are palatal?" Two thirds (p 278).
3. "How do you tell labial from palatal?" The buccal object rule, SLOB (p 281).
4. "Why is a palatal canine so slow?" Too little bone removal: enamel cannot resorb bone (p 282).
5. "How long until an early-uncovered canine reaches the occlusal plane?" About 6 to 8 months (p 283).
6. "Isn't he mostly citing his own work?" Four of 13 references come from the author or his department (Kokich & Mathews 1993 and 2001, Vermette, Schmidt; derived), including the two studies behind his main recommendations (Vermette, Schmidt). Put that way, it is an accusation. Q9 and Q10 make the same point with data.
7. "Is this evidence-based?" It is an invited expert review and does not claim to be a study. Ask about the evidence behind a specific claim instead.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Walk me through how you choose the uncovering technique for a labial canine."**
There are four criteria (pp 278-279):
1. **Labiolingual position.** A labial crown has little bone over it, so any of the three techniques works. An intra-alveolar crown makes excision and the APF harder, because a lot of labial bone would have to come off.
2. **Height against the MGJ.** If most of the crown is coronal to it, any technique works. If it is apical, excision is out, because no gingiva would cover the labial surface. If it is significantly apical, the APF is out too, because of instability and reintrusion; use closed eruption.
3. **Gingiva.** If too little, only the APF predictably makes more. If enough to leave at least 2 to 3 mm attached after eruption, any technique works.
4. **Mesiodistal position.** A crown mesial over the lateral root needs full exposure with an APF; closed eruption or excision is generally not recommended.

The figures map onto the criteria: Fig 1 is the simple excision case, Fig 2 the mesial crown, Fig 3 the gingiva case, and Fig 4 the high intra-alveolar canine.

**"Why does an APF on a high canine relapse?"**
The flap heals to the adjacent mucosa. As the tooth is erupted, that mucosa is drawn coronally, and after treatment the mucosal attachment pulls the crown back apically (pp 280-281). Vermette found that APF crowns were longer than normal, because the gingival margin migrated apically, and that high labial APF canines reintruded. Closed-eruption crowns matched the contralateral teeth, and the periodontal indices did not differ between techniques (pp 279-281).

**"How do you erupt a closed-eruption canine?"**
The mechanics should mimic normal eruption, bringing the crown down into the centre of the alveolar ridge with a vertical force. A ballista loop is simple and unobtrusive (Fig 4). Avoid drawing the tooth labially, because that risks a bony dehiscence, faster migration of the labial gingival margin and recession (p 279).

**"Explain the buccal object rule."**
Take two adjacent periapicals at slightly different horizontal angles. A buccal object moves opposite to the direction of the beam shift; a lingual (palatal) object moves the same way. That is "same lingual, opposite buccal" (p 281). I would add a CBCT when the views are equivocal or resorption is suspected, as Proffit's notes and Eslami 2017 support.

**"When does extracting the primary canine work?"**
As Kokich cites Ericson and Kurol: when the crown overlaps the lateral root but has not passed its mesial surface, self-correction is highly predictable; well beyond it, it does not occur, and the canine must be uncovered (pp 281-282). For the exam I would add the midline version with 91 % vs 64 % (VERIFIED-secondary) and the RCTs, 69 % vs 39 % and 67 % vs 42 % (VERIFIED). Extraction raises the chance; it does not guarantee eruption.

**"Describe Kokich's early palatal technique, step by step."**
1. Uncover before orthodontic treatment, in some cases in the late mixed dentition.
2. Raise a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap.
3. Remove all bone over the crown down to the CEJ.
4. Replace the flap with a hole cut through it over the crown. Place a dressing occasionally, if the canine is high in the palate.
5. Let the canine erupt on its own, about 6 to 8 months to the occlusal plane.
6. Then bond a bracket and move the root through bone while the crown is translated into the arch (p 283; Fig 5).

**"What did Woloshyn show, and why does it matter?"**
Thirty-two patients had palatal canines uncovered conventionally and dragged across the lingual surfaces of the lateral roots. Compared with the contralateral side, bone was more apical on the distal of the lateral and the mesial of the canine. Root resorption of the lateral and the canine was typical, and judges could identify the treated side (p 283). It is the paper's case against the conventional approach. Woloshyn's own abstract lists shorter lateral incisor and premolar roots (literature, VERIFIED).

**"What level of evidence is this paper?"**
Oxford CEBM Level 5: expert opinion with mechanism-based reasoning, an invited symposium paper with five illustrative cases and no data of its own. The studies it leans on are:
- Vermette and Woloshyn: retrospective, contralateral-controlled.
- Ericson and Kurol: a prospective series without an untreated control (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).
- Schmidt: a thesis not yet peer reviewed.

The labial algorithm has held up in later systematic reviews (Incerti-Parenti 2016, Xenou 2025, both VERIFIED). The palatal superiority claim did not hold up in the trials (Q10).

## Ten-second summary

"Kokich 2004 is an expert review, not a study. For labial canines he chooses between excision, an apically positioned flap and closed eruption using four criteria: labiolingual position, height against the MGJ, amount of gingiva and mesiodistal position. Canines significantly apical to the MGJ go closed, because an APF gives a long crown and reintrudes. For palatal canines he says uncover early, take the bone down to the CEJ and let the tooth erupt on its own for 6 to 8 months before moving it. The labial algorithm still holds.
