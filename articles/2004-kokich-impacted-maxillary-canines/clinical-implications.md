# Clinical implications — Kokich 2004

Kokich VG. Surgical and orthodontic management of impacted maxillary canines. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2004;126(3):278-83. This is an invited narrative paper from the 2004 AAO/AAOMS symposium. It has no sample or data of its own: five single-patient cases (Figs 1-5) illustrate a four-criterion algorithm for uncovering labial canines and an early, open technique for palatal canines. Every clinical message below is therefore expert opinion (Level 5), checked against the cited and later evidence where marked.

On this page, "the paper says" means printed in Kokich 2004, with the page. "The literature says" means another source checked in this run's research pass, marked VERIFIED (abstract read), VERIFIED-secondary (number confirmed only through papers citing it) or UNVERIFIED. "Proffit (notes)" means the user's Proffit study pages, not the printed book. "My read" is my interpretation.

## What this changes or confirms at the chair

1. **Name the uncovering technique in the referral; do not leave it to the surgeon.** The paper says: the orthodontist "might assume incorrectly" that the surgeon knows which procedure to use, and a poorly chosen technique leaves a long, difficult eruption and an unpredictable esthetic result (p 278). My read: the referral letter should state the side, labial or palatal position, the technique, how much bone to remove, the attachment, and how the flap is closed. That is the practical message of the whole paper.

2. **For a labial or intra-alveolar canine, run the four criteria in order before choosing.** The paper says (pp 278-279):
   - Labial crown: any technique. Crown mid-alveolus: excision and APF are "generally more difficult" because extensive labial bone might need to be removed.
   - Crown coronal to the MGJ: any technique. Crown apical to it: no excision. Crown significantly apical: no APF either.
   - Too little gingiva: APF is the only technique that adds it. Enough to leave **at least 2-3 mm** of attached gingiva after eruption: any technique.
   - Crown mesial over the lateral incisor root: APF; closed eruption and excision "generally" not recommended.

   Caveat: there is no cut-off for "significantly" apical, and no priority when criteria conflict (a high canine that is also mesial over the lateral root fits both "APF" and "not APF").

3. **A high labial canine goes to closed eruption, not an apically positioned flap.** The paper says: an APF on a high labial canine heals to the adjacent mucosa, the mucosa is pulled coronally during eruption and later pulls the crown back apically (reintrusion); APF crowns are also longer than the contralateral because the gingival margin migrates apically (Vermette, pp 279-281). Closed-eruption crowns matched the contralateral tooth. The literature says: Vermette's sample was 30 labially impacted maxillary anterior teeth, not canines only (APF 18, closed 12), and its abstract also reports worse probing bone levels than the contralateral tooth in both groups, at more sites with APF (VERIFIED). Later systematic reviews found APF comparable to untreated teeth (Incerti-Parenti 2016) and both APF and closed eruption similar to untreated teeth (Xenou 2025) (VERIFIED). My read: the esthetic and vertical-stability argument for closed eruption stands; the periodontal difference is small.

4. **Erupt a labial canine vertically into the centre of the ridge, never labially.** The paper says: pulling labially risks a bony dehiscence, accelerated gingival migration and labial recession; a ballista loop gives a simple vertical force vector (Fig 4E-G). For a crown mesial over the lateral root, elastomeric chain toward the centre of the edentulous ridge, then distally (Fig 2E) (p 279). Caveat: no force magnitude, wire or attachment is specified. Proffit (notes): create the space first, use a heavy stabilising wire (at least 18-mil round steel), and load within 2-3 weeks (Ch 15).

5. **Localise the crown before you refer.** The paper says: two adjacent periapicals at slightly different horizontal angles; the palatal crown moves with the beam, the buccal crown against it (SLOB) (p 281). The literature says: in a systematic review, CBCT localised impacted canines with 50-95 % accuracy against 39-85 % for conventional radiography, and is recommended for difficult cases (Eslami 2017, VERIFIED). Proffit (notes) lists impacted teeth among the justified indications for CBCT (Ch 6) and moves from the panoramic to small-field CBCT (Ch 11/15). My read: parallax first; CBCT when the films disagree, when the lateral root may be resorbing, or when the crown is mid-alveolus.

6. **For a palatally displaced canine in the mixed dentition, extracting the deciduous canine is worth doing, but it is not a guarantee.** The paper says: if the crown has not passed the mesial surface of the lateral incisor root, self-correction occurs "with high predictability" (Ericson & Kurol); well beyond it, it does not occur (pp 281-282). The literature says:
   - Ericson & Kurol's source threshold is the lateral-root **midline**: 91 % normalised when distal to it, 64 % when mesial (VERIFIED-secondary; same figures in Proffit (notes), Ch 11).
   - RCTs: spontaneous eruption 69 % with extraction vs 39 % without (Naoumova 2015) and 67 % vs 42 % (Bazargani 2014; VERIFIED).
   - The extraction side lost arch perimeter, and a palatal arch is recommended (Bazargani 2014; VERIFIED).

   My read: extract, hold the space, and reassess. About 1 in 3 canines did not erupt spontaneously even with extraction (derived from 69 %).

7. **A palatal canine that will not move is usually under-exposed, not ankylosed.** The paper says: once the follicle is removed, enamel has no cells to resorb bone, so resorption occurs slowly by pressure necrosis; the incidence of ankylosis is "low" (p 282). Proffit (notes) gives the same mechanism and advises removing enough bone; for partial ankylosis, luxate and load at once (Ch 15). My read: before calling it ankylosis, check for bone still covering the crown on CBCT or at re-exposure.

8. **Conventional dragging of a palatal canine across the incisor roots has a periodontal and root cost.** The paper says: in Woloshyn's 32 patients, bone was more apical on the distal of the lateral incisor and the mesial of the canine than on the contralateral side, and lateral and canine root resorption were typical (p 283). The literature says: the abstract reports shorter **lateral incisor and premolar** roots and identification of the treated side in about 75 % (VERIFIED). My read: whichever technique is used, move the crown away from the lateral root early and avoid traction against it.

9. **Early open exposure with autonomous eruption is a valid option, but the trials say equivalent, not superior.** The paper says: uncover before brackets, sometimes in the late mixed dentition; remove all bone to the CEJ; leave a hole in the flap; the canine reaches the occlusal plane in about 6-8 months; the result is shorter overall treatment and superior periodontal and esthetic outcomes (p 283). The literature says (all VERIFIED):
   - Success: Cochrane 2017 (3 RCTs, 146 participants) RR 0.99 (0.93-1.06).
   - Attachment level, open vs closed: Parkin 2013, MD 0.1 mm (95 % CI −0.2 to 0.5).
   - Total treatment time: Björksved 2021, MD −0.1 months (95 % CI −3.2 to 2.9, P = .93).
   - Esthetics: the published version of the cited thesis (Schmidt & Kokich 2007) reports the treated canine identified 78.9 % of the time and shorter lateral incisor roots.
   - Pain: closed exposure hurt more during traction (Björksved); open exposure scored more pain on a VAS in another trial (Færøvig 2025).

   My read: choose open or closed on the surgeon's experience, the patient's pain tolerance and the depth of the canine. Do not promise a shorter treatment.

10. **Active lateral incisor resorption changes the timing.** The paper does not address it. Proffit (notes) says that once resorption is active, expose the canine and move it away now: this stops the resorption but does not reverse it (Ch 11/12). My read: a 6-8-month autonomous-eruption wait is hard to justify when the lateral root is already resorbing.

## Practical protocol notes (as the paper describes them)

**Before referral**
1. Open space for the canine first; in Fig 1 the space was created and the tooth still did not erupt (Fig 1A), and in Fig 4 space was opened before the closed eruption (Fig 4B).
2. Localise the crown with the buccal object rule on two periapicals (SLOB) (p 281). The paper predates routine CBCT.
3. Record, for a labial canine: labial or mid-alveolus; crown coronal, apical or significantly apical to the MGJ; width of gingiva (will at least 2-3 mm remain attached after eruption?); crown over the lateral root or not.
4. Choose the technique from the four criteria and write it on the referral.

**Labial techniques**
- *Excisional uncovering (Fig 1):* only for a labial crown coronal to the MGJ with enough gingiva.
- *Apically positioned flap (Figs 2-3; Vanarsdall & Corn, ref 5):* full exposure; adds gingiva. In Fig 3B the mucosa was stained with Schiller's iodine before the flap (my read: to mark the mucogingival boundary; the paper does not say why). Avoid for crowns significantly apical to the MGJ.
- *Closed eruption (Fig 4; Kokich & Mathews 1993, ref 6):* labial flap elevated, enough bone removed around the crown for unimpeded eruption (Fig 4D). Use a vertical force into the centre of the ridge with a ballista loop (Fig 4E-G).
- Never pull the crown labially (p 279).

**Palatal: early technique (Fig 5; Kokich & Mathews 2001, ref 11)**
1. Timing: before orthodontic treatment, in some cases in the late mixed dentition (p 283).
2. Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap over the canine.
3. Remove all bone over the crown down to the CEJ (Fig 5C).
4. Reposition the flap and cut a hole through it over the crown (Fig 5D). If the canine is high in the palate, a dressing is occasionally placed over the window.
5. Let it erupt without orthodontic force; the canine reaches the occlusal plane in about 6-8 months (p 283).
6. Bond a bracket at the occlusal plane; move the root labially through bone as the crown translates into the arch (Fig 5G).

**Palatal: conventional route the paper argues against**
- Uncovering after 6-9 months of alignment, traction soon after surgery (p 282). If used, remove enough bone, because enamel cannot resorb bone (p 282), and keep the crown off the lateral incisor root (Woloshyn, p 283).

## What the paper does NOT tell you

- **Any data of its own.** There is no sample, no measurement and no statistic; the five cases are successes chosen to illustrate.
- **The threshold between "apical" and "significantly apical" to the MGJ,** or which criterion wins when two conflict.
- **Attachment, force, wire and activation details** for any technique: no grams, wire sizes, attachment type (button, bracket, eyelet, chain) or closed-flap management.
- **The success rate of deciduous canine extraction.** "High predictability" has no number; the source's 78 % overall, 91 % and 64 % are not printed (VERIFIED-secondary).
- **The age or root stage for early palatal uncovering,** beyond "in some cases" in the late mixed dentition, and what to do if autonomous eruption stalls.
- **Treatment-time data.** The conclusion that overall time is reduced has no cited source. In Fig 5E-F, brackets are already on the adjacent teeth while the canine erupts, so time in appliances for that case cannot be judged.
- **The ankylosis incidence** ("low", no number).
- **The details behind the Schmidt thesis:** no n, comparator or values. The 2007 publication reports shorter lateral roots, lower lateral crestal bone and 78.9 % identification (VERIFIED). See `conflicts.md`.
- **How Woloshyn's judges assessed the canines** (blinding and identification rate are not given). The printed reason clause says the tissue "compared favorably", which reads opposite to the judges being able to identify the treated canine; see `conflicts.md`.
- **Patients outside the illustrated range:** bilateral impactions, adults with complete roots, transpositions, dilacerated or ankylosed canines, mandibular canines, and active lateral incisor resorption.
- **3D localisation.** Diagnosis is by periapical tube shift only.

## One sentence for a patient or a junior

- **To a junior:** "Decide the uncovering yourself: a labial canine whose crown sits coronal to the mucogingival junction, with enough gum, can just be uncovered; one with too little gum or sitting over the lateral root gets an apically positioned flap; a high one gets closed eruption and a vertical pull. For a palatal canine, get the bone off down to the CEJ, because enamel can't eat through bone."
- **To a parent:** "Removing the baby canine now often lets the adult canine find its own way down, but not always; if it doesn't, a small operation will uncover it and the braces will guide it into place."
