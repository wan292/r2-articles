# Surgical and orthodontic management of impacted maxillary canines (Kokich 2004)

Kokich VG. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2004;126(3):278-83. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2004.06.009. Written up from a talk at the AAO/AAOMS symposium, February 2004; University of Washington, Seattle.

## Bottom line

The orthodontist, not the surgeon, should choose how an impacted canine is uncovered. For a labial canine, four criteria (labiolingual position, height relative to the mucogingival junction, width of gingiva, mesiodistal position) choose between excision, an apically positioned flap (APF) and closed eruption. For a palatal canine, Kokich recommends early open exposure with all bone removed to the CEJ, so the tooth erupts on its own before it is bracketed.

## What they did

A narrative expert review with no sample, measurements or statistics. Five single-patient cases (Figs 1-5: four labial or intra-alveolar, one palatal) illustrate rules drawn from 13 references.

## What they found

- Maxillary canines are the most commonly impacted teeth after third molars; about one third are labial or intra-alveolar, two thirds palatal (p 278).
- Excision only when the crown is coronal to the MGJ and enough gingiva remains for at least 2-3 mm attached after eruption; APF when gingiva is short or the crown lies mesially over the lateral incisor root; closed eruption when the crown is significantly apical to the MGJ (pp 278-279; Figs 1-4).
- APF on high labial canines: longer crowns and reintrusion; closed-eruption crowns similar to the contralateral tooth (Vermette, pp 279-281).
- Erupt vertically into the centre of the ridge (ballista loop) and avoid pulling labially (p 279).
- Localise with the buccal object rule on two periapicals: SLOB (p 281).
- Extracting the deciduous canine lets a palatal canine self-correct if its crown has not passed the mesial surface of the lateral incisor root (Ericson & Kurol, as cited, pp 281-282).
- A palatal canine that will not move is usually under-exposed, not ankylosed: enamel has no cells to resorb bone (p 282).
- Conventional uncovering after 6-9 months of alignment, then dragging: more apical bone and root resorption (Woloshyn, n = 32). Early exposure: erupts to the occlusal plane in about 6-8 months, then a bracket (p 283).

## Why it matters

It is the standard referral algorithm for labial canines and the source of the open-exposure, autonomous-eruption approach for palatal canines. Later trials found that approach equivalent, not superior, to closed exposure (Cochrane 2017 RR 0.99; Björksved 2021 treatment time MD −0.1 months; VERIFIED).

## One-line caveat

Level 5 expert opinion: shorter treatment and superior periodontal and esthetic results are concluded without data in the paper, partly on an unpublished thesis.

## Mechanism at a glance

| Technique | When the paper uses it | Why it works or fails | Figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excisional uncovering | Labial crown coronal to the MGJ, enough gingiva (at least 2-3 mm attached after eruption) | Simple; on a crown apical to the MGJ it leaves no labial gingiva | Fig 1 |
| Apically positioned flap | Too little gingiva, or crown mesial over the lateral incisor root | The only technique that adds gingiva; full exposure lets chain guide the crown; on a high canine the flap heals to mucosa, giving a long crown and reintrusion | Figs 2, 3 |
| Closed eruption | Crown significantly apical to the MGJ, or in the centre of the alveolus | Erupts through gingiva like normal eruption; ballista loop gives a vertical force into the ridge centre | Fig 4 |
| Early open palatal exposure | Palatal canine, before orthodontic treatment, sometimes late mixed dentition | All bone removed to the CEJ because enamel cannot resorb bone; hole in the flap; autonomous eruption about 6-8 months | Fig 5 |
| Deciduous canine extraction | Palatal canine not past the mesial surface of the lateral incisor root | Self-correction "with high predictability" (no number given) | ref 10 |
