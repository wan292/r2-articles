# Questions to ask about this paper

Nielsen IL. Maxillary superimposition: a comparison of three methods for cephalometric evaluation of growth and treatment change. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 1989;95(5):422-431. UCSF. Eighteen implanted children, two anatomic superimpositions tested against implants over four years.

This is a methods paper, so the good questions are not "what did they find" but "what does the finding do to a measurement I make every week". The abstract gives one number, 30 to 50 %, and one reassurance, that the structural method had no vertical bias. Tables I to IV give a landmark-by-landmark picture that is more useful and less tidy: the percentage runs from 28 % to 59 % depending on the landmark, the structural method carries a 0.5 mm horizontal offset the abstract never mentions, and the implant rows of Tables III and IV do not rebuild from Tables I and II. Every number below was checked against the printed tables (page images at 2×). Anything from outside the paper is labelled (literature), (Proffit), (registry) or my read, and is UNVERIFIED unless a source was seen.

How to ask: give the table number first, keep the question to one breath, and frame every methodological point as "typo or design?" rather than as a charge. The structural method is what the ABO requires today and several people in the room were trained on palatal best fit; the paper is in effect telling them their old superimpositions hid a third of the eruption. Ask so that the data do that, not you.

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "The abstract says best fit underestimates eruption by 30 to 50 %. From Table II I get 28 % at the molar cusp, 39 % at the molar apex, 59 % at the incisal edge and 40 % at the incisor apex. Should we teach it as a range by landmark rather than one factor, and why is the incisal edge the worst?"
- **Why it lands:** It shows you divided the Table II columns yourself (1 − best fit / implant: U6C 3.40/4.75, U6A 2.33/3.80, UIE 1.40/3.39, UIA 2.60/4.36; my arithmetic). The Discussion itself says "nearly 30 %" for the molars and "as much as 50 %" for the incisors, so the abstract rounds the two extremes. Point A is worse still: 0.47 mm on best fit against 2.33 mm on implants, 80 % missed. My read on the edge: it is the landmark farthest from the palate (about 34 mm anterior to the molar origin in Fig 6) and the best-fit error is an en masse rotation driven by anterior-dominant nasal-floor resorption, so the anterior end moves most.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that the direction is what matters, and a caution worth repeating: this is 4 years of pubertal growth (10 to 14 y). The masked quantity is nasal-floor resorption per unit time, not a fixed share of tooth movement, so the percentage is not a correction factor for other ages or intervals.
- **Follow-up:** "In an adult with no nasal-floor resorption the same best fit would be almost unbiased. Is the method wrong, or wrong for growing patients only?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did best fit underestimate eruption?" The abstract answers that.

### Q2. "The abstract says structural and implant did not differ vertically, but Tables III and IV show a horizontal difference of 0.53 to 0.66 mm that is significant at seven of eight landmarks. The Discussion calls it a posterior shift; Tables I and II put every structural landmark 0.5 to 0.75 mm less posterior than the implant value. Which way does the bias run, and what does it do to a headgear distalisation measured this way?"
- **Why it lands:** Three things in one question: the abstract's omission, the magnitude (implant minus structural X: PNS 0.53, ANS-Harvold 0.56, ANS 0.56, point A 0.41 unstarred; U6C 0.62, U6A 0.56, UIE 0.66, UIA 0.58 mm), and a direction the paper states both ways. Discussion p 429-430: the structural method showed a posterior displacement of all points averaging 0.5 mm, explained by sliding the second film down a distally sloping zygomatic surface with apposition set equal to resorption. Tables I and II (Fig 6 convention, +X anterior): structural PNS −1.81 vs implant −2.34, U6C +2.06 vs +1.31, UIE +0.15 vs −0.46; structural minus implant averages +0.51 mm at the eight landmarks (my arithmetic), i.e. less posterior. The Results text agrees with the tables ("overall less displacement" horizontally). Magnitude is solid; sign is not resolvable from the paper.
- **What you will probably hear:** Most will not have noticed. The honest answer: if the structural method places the molar 0.6 mm more anterior at T2 than implants, a headgear distalisation is under-read by about 0.6 mm and mesial drift over-read by the same; if the Discussion's direction is right, the reverse. Either way 0.5 mm is about the size of a single tracing error (Doppel 1994: duplicate tracings ± 0.25 mm, total 0.5 mm; registry).
- **Follow-up:** "The author blames the 1 : 1 ratio for the shift. Would the 3 : 2 ratio he cites from Björk and Skieller have removed it?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Was the structural method accurate?" The paper says valid and reliable; the question is in which plane.

### Q3. "On best fit, PNS, ANS and ANS-Harvold appear to move up 0.3 to 0.4 mm in four years and point A down only 0.5 mm, while the implants show them coming down 0.7, 1.6 and 2.3 mm. When a palatal superimposition tells us a growing maxilla has stopped descending or the incisors have not erupted, how much of that is the method?"
- **Why it lands:** Table I in one sentence, with the direction reversed, not just reduced: best fit PNS +0.36, ANS +0.28, ANS-Harvold +0.37, point A −0.47 against implant −0.70, −1.62, −1.56, −2.33. The Results text confirms the "slight upward displacement". Proffit (5th edition text, seen) says serial cephalograms are the most accurate way to know whether growth has stopped; this table says that is true only if the registration is not on the structure that is remodelling.
- **What you will probably hear:** The palate is registered on itself, so its own descent, nasal-floor resorption with oral-side apposition, is subtracted. Proffit (study pages, Ch 2 maxilla, Fig 2.30) makes the same point: the total downward movement of the palatal vault exceeds the displacement of the maxilla because of surface remodelling. They will say use the structural method, or the ABO version of it.
- **Follow-up:** "Harvold's 3-mm definition of ANS was meant to tame ANS. Table I says ANS and ANS-Harvold differ by 0.06 mm vertically on implants, so the problem is not where ANS is but what it is registered on. Agree?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does the palate remodel?"

### Q4. "Our Khaleel 2026 paper superimposed the maxilla on the palatal vault after Broadbent, which is Nielsen's Fig 1a, in 16- to 17-year-olds over 44 to 94 days, and reported 0.07 to 0.42 mm of upper posterior extrusion. Scaling Nielsen's masked descent gives well under 0.1 mm over those intervals. So is the method's bias the problem there, or its resolution?"
- **Why it lands:** It connects two papers in the hub (registry: Khaleel key-numbers, maxillary superimposition "palatal vault (Broadbent)", ages 15.82 / 16.00 / 17.35 y, intervals 44.42 / 55.72 / 94.15 d). The scaling is yours and labelled: Nielsen's implant-minus-best-fit vertical is 1.08 to 2.24 mm skeletal and 1.33 to 1.84 mm dental per 4 y, i.e. 0.27 to 0.56 and 0.33 to 0.46 mm per year, so 0.04 to 0.12 mm over 0.12 to 0.26 y with no age discount, and less at 16 to 17 y where remodelling is slower than at 10 to 14 y (UNVERIFIED for that age band). The resolution point is the sharper one: Nielsen's own landmark SDs are 0.53 to 2.90 mm (Tables I and II), Doppel's tracing error is 0.5 mm (registry), and Khaleel's between-arm differences of 0.2 to 0.35 mm sit below both.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that over 6 to 13 weeks the palatal bias is negligible and the direction is known (upper molar extrusion under-read), but that any sub-0.5 mm claim from a hand-traced 2D cephalogram is not measurable. Someone may add that Khaleel's ICC of 0.92 to 0.98 is repeatability of measurement, not validity of the superimposition.
- **Follow-up:** "Should a short-interval 2D study report sub-millimetre tooth movements at all, or only direction and significance?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did Khaleel use the wrong superimposition?" Frame it as bias versus resolution and let the numbers settle it.

## Tier 2. Mechanics and biology of remodeling

### Q5. "Implants showed ANS lowering 1.62 mm and PNS 0.70 mm in four years, Table I, which the paper reads as anterior-dominant nasal-floor resorption after Björk and Skieller. Baumrind's 1987 implant series found the opposite, PNS 2.50 mm against ANS 1.56 mm over a longer interval. Which pattern do you see, and does treatment change it?"
- **Why it lands:** Table I against the literature. Nielsen's Discussion says the extent of this remodelling has been quantified only in a few untreated subjects and that a treatment effect "cannot be ruled out"; his sample is mostly Class II and mixed treated/untreated. Baumrind, Korn, Ben-Bassat and West 1987 part 1 (AJODO 91:29-41; literature, abstract seen): 31 implant subjects, PNS remodelled downward 2.50 mm (SD 2.23) and ANS 1.56 mm (SD 2.92) over 8.5 to 15.5 y, with ANS-PNS rotating 1.1° forward on average but ranging from 11.3° forward to 6.7° backward. My read: part of the gap is definitional, because PNS moves back 2.34 mm in 4 y (Table I) and a "vertical" value at a point that is itself migrating posteriorly along a lengthening palate depends on which PNS you measure; and part is individual rotation, which Doppel 1994 (registry) puts at +0.92 ± 2.10° between palatal plane and implant line, range −5° to +8°.
- **What you will probably hear:** That the mean hides forward and backward rotators, and that Björk and Skieller's compensation between internal and external rotation (Proffit Ch 4, rotation of jaws, study pages; 6th ed p 88) is a population statement. A headgear-minded attending will say extraoral force tips the palatal plane, which is exactly why Nielsen could not separate treatment from growth.
- **Follow-up:** "If the direction of palatal rotation varies by patient, is any fixed ratio, 3 : 2 or 1 : 1, right for an individual, or only for the group mean?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does the nasal floor resorb?"

### Q6. "The paper cites Björk and Skieller's 3 : 2 for orbital-floor apposition to nasal-floor resorption, then runs the structural method at 1 : 1, and attributes its small vertical over-read of 0.04 to 0.33 mm to that choice. Doppel found 1.5 : 1 in 50 mostly treated cases and the ABO teaches three-fifths to two-fifths. Why not use 3 : 2, and does the ratio survive headgear or a functional appliance?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods p 424 ("quantitatively equal"), Discussion p 429, and Fig 2 (orbital apposition 6.4, nasal-floor resorption 4.6, which is 1.39 : 1 by my arithmetic, called "approximately 3 : 2" in the text). The over-read is largest at PNS (structural −1.02 vs implant −0.70, 0.32 mm) and under 0.25 mm elsewhere (Tables I and II). Doppel's 1.5 : 1 came from a sample treated with headgear and Class II/III elastics (registry), so the ratio looks robust to treatment, though Doppel prints no table for it. The ABO deck (seen) writes 3/5 apposition to 2/5 resorption.
- **What you will probably hear:** That the ratio is a group mean from nine boys aged 4 to 20 y and that over 4 years the difference between 1 : 1 and 3 : 2 is a fraction of a millimetre, which is what Nielsen's −0.04 to −0.33 mm shows. Practical version: register anteroposteriorly on the anterior zygomatic surface, then set the final palatal plane slightly below the initial one.
- **Follow-up:** "Nielsen attributes the 0.5 mm horizontal shift to the 1 : 1 choice acting through the slope of the zygomatic process. Doppel used 1.5 : 1 but reports no horizontal number for his method, so we do not know whether it went away. Does anyone have a feel for it?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is the ratio?"

### Q7. "Nielsen found no resorptive modeling of the anterior surface of the zygomatic process on implants, contrary to Enlow, and Björk and Skieller saw it stable in nine of nine boys. Doppel's 50 cases had a posterior change in 35 % and an anterior change in 27.5 %, with a mean maximum deviation of 0.95 ± 0.57 mm. Is the surface stable, or stable only on the films clean enough to use it?"
- **Why it lands:** Discussion p 430 against Doppel 1994 Table II (registry; 40 of 50 traceable). Nielsen selected films for clear zygomatic processes and matched double contours, warns that a short process "can create a rotational effect", and admits that unilateral implants left head-position error in the implant standard itself. Doppel's explanations for his spread are a larger and treated sample, an exact fit on three implants, transverse rotation of the two maxillae (Björk and Skieller 1977; Korn and Baumrind 1990 widening 0.43 mm per year; literature) and head rotation moving a zygomatic implant up to 3 mm (Julius thesis; literature).
- **What you will probably hear:** That on a well-positioned film it is the best anatomic structure the maxilla offers, and that Doppel's 0.95 mm is close to his 0.5 mm tracing error plus head-position noise. Nobody will claim it is stable to the tenth of a millimetre.
- **Follow-up:** "It is bilateral and you superimpose on a double contour. When the left and right processes do not coincide, do you average, pick a side, or reject the film?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Where is the zygomatic process?"

### Q8. "With implants PNS moved back 2.34 mm in four years; on best fit only 1.18 mm, a 50 % under-read of growth in length. That is why Moore's PTM registration and Riedel's infratemporal fossa fail. In a headgear case, how do you separate maxillary lengthening at the tuberosity from molar distalisation if the registration sits at the back of the maxilla?"
- **Why it lands:** Table I and the Results text (2.3 vs 1.2 mm), the Discussion's mechanism (sutural growth plus tuberosity apposition, citing Melsen and Melsen 1982 on autopsy material), and the two rejected methods with their reasons (maxillary lengthening at PNS; the posterior zygomatic surface is appositional). The molar row makes it concrete: U6C moved 1.31 mm anteriorly on implants and 3.02 mm on best fit (Table II). Doppel (registry) adds that PNS was visible in only 52 % of his films and PTM deviated 2.39 ± 1.69 mm vertically. Proffit (study pages, Ch 2 maxilla) has bone added at the tuberosity to make room for the molars.
- **What you will probably hear:** That PNS and PTM are the growth front of the maxilla and any posterior registration subtracts the growth you want to measure; molar "distalisation" read against PTM is partly tuberosity growth. The structural method reads the molar against a stable anterior region instead.
- **Follow-up:** "Second-molar germs obscure PNS at this age. Between 10 and 14 y, how often can you locate PNS well enough to register on it at all?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why not register at PTM?" The Discussion answers it.

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q9. "Only 18 of 35 implant subjects were usable, chosen for clear zygomatic processes, visible ANS and orbital floors, no excessive double contours and three to four stable zygomatic implants. Those are the structural method's own requirements. Are these error figures a best case for the structural method, and what would it do on an average clinic film?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods p 424 says selection was on film quality only, then lists criteria that are precisely what the structural method needs; the Discussion p 430 says the method is "greatly dependent" on film quality and bilateral double contours and that only 18 of 35 could be used for those reasons. Not all subjects had bilateral implants and the criterion for an "unstable" implant is never stated, so the reference standard was selected too.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, a lower bound, and the same is true of Doppel (50 of about 700 implant records, registry). Nobody has error figures for routine films. The ABO deck (seen) warns that heads rotate 3° and tip 5° in a cephalostat, which is the same worry.
- **Follow-up:** "A 3D voxel registration does not need two zygomatic processes to coincide on a double contour. Nguyen's two observers differed by 0.12 ± 0.11 mm on the mandible. Is film quality the one problem 3D really removes?"
- **Avoid saying:** "How many were excluded?"

### Q10. "Tables III and IV should be the differences of the Table I and II means, because they are the same 18 subjects. The structural-minus-best-fit rows rebuild to the hundredth; the implant rows do not, by up to 0.38 mm at point A vertically. Were the implant comparisons run on all 18, or on a different determination?"
- **Why it lands:** The arithmetic is yours and it is clean: point A Y, implant −2.33 minus best fit −0.47 is 1.86 mm, printed 2.24; point A X 0.92 vs printed 1.15; UIA X 1.09 vs 1.30; implant minus structural point A X 0.13 vs 0.41; all 16 structural-minus-best-fit cells match within 0.01 mm. For paired data the mean of differences must equal the difference of means, so either fewer subjects fed the implant rows (films with an unstable implant dropped per landmark?) or a different tracing did. While you are there: the test is never named, 64 comparisons run without correction, Table IV's footnote (** P < .001, *** P < .0001) contradicts Table III's (** P < .01, *** P < .001) and its own numbers (U6C X implant minus structural 0.62 ± 0.79 gives t ≈ 3.3, P ≈ .004 by paired t on 18, my arithmetic), and Fig 8 labels the apex value 1.71 ± 0.84 as UIE when Table IV gives UIE 1.84 ± 0.86.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Typesetting", then a pause. A statistically minded attending will agree that gaps of 0.2 to 0.4 mm with n = 18 mean the n differs. The headline survives regardless: the implant-vs-best-fit vertical effects give t of about 3.6 to 9.1 on the printed means and SDs and would survive any correction.
- **Follow-up:** "Double determinations were made for all three methods but no method error is reported and the tables do not say whether the first, the second or the mean went in. Which?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Are the statistics wrong?" Ask which n and which determination.

### Q11. "No significant vertical difference between structural and implant is a statement about the mean. The SDs of those differences are 0.68 to 1.19 mm, so by my arithmetic 95 % of individuals fall within about ± 1.3 to ± 2.3 mm of the implant answer, and two observers differed by 0.8 to 0.9 mm vertically at ANS and the incisor after brief instructions. For one patient's molar, what precision should we claim from a structural superimposition?"
- **Why it lands:** Tables III and IV SDs, turned into limits of agreement (mean ± 1.96 SD, assuming normality; derived): point A −2.4 to +2.3 mm, U6C −1.8 to +1.2, UIE −1.6 to +1.2. The observer row: ANS 0.88 ± 1.29 (P < .01), ANS-Harvold 0.87 ± 1.30 (P < .01), point A 0.77 ± 1.42 (P < .05), UIE 0.86 ± 1.28 (P < .05), UIA 0.52 ± 0.75 (P < .01); the second observer worked "after brief instructions". The paper attributes the variability to landmark location (Baumrind and Frantz 1971) and says it was equal across methods. There is no ICC, no Dahlberg error and no limits of agreement anywhere in the paper.
- **What you will probably hear:** About 1 mm for a single tooth in 2D, which is fine for group studies and marginal for an individual claim. Someone will quote Nguyen's 0.12 mm (registry) and someone else will point out that it is reproducibility between observers, not validity against a gold standard.
- **Follow-up:** "Is the reliability problem in the superimposition or in finding ANS? Horizontal observer differences were not significant, which points at the landmark."
- **Avoid saying:** "Was it reliable?" The abstract says so.

## Tier 4. Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "Proffit describes three superimpositions, cranial base, maxillary and mandibular, and in the records chapter draws the maxillary one on the maxilla's own contour. The ABO specifies the anterior and posterior zygomatic surfaces, the orbital floors and the palatal floor with a three-fifths to two-fifths rule, and cites Nielsen and Doppel as its validation. For a board case, which structures do you actually put the tracing on?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit (study pages, Ch 6, Figs 6.58 to 6.60; the maxillary legend is transcribed there as "maximum contour of the maxilla", wording to be checked in print) against the ABO Cephalometric Review deck (seen): maxilla registered anteroposteriorly on the anterior zygomatic surface, final palatal plane slightly below the initial, apposition 3/5 to resorption 2/5, with Buschang 1986, Doppel 1994 and Nielsen 1989 as references 5 to 7. Nielsen's structural method is the ABO method minus the ratio. Against both sits Graf 2022 (Eur J Orthod 44:477-490; literature, abstract seen): 27 superimposition studies, 25 at high risk of bias, no cephalometric method proved accurate.
- **What you will probably hear:** The ABO structural method from anyone who has sat the board recently; a palatal best fit from some who trained earlier. The room will demonstrate the paper's point for you.
- **Follow-up:** "Is Proffit's maxillary figure on the palate or on the zygomatic structures? If it is the palate, his figure and Nielsen's Table I disagree." Ask it as a check, not a claim; the legend wording is UNVERIFIED.
- **Avoid saying:** "What does the ABO require?" Read the deck before the session.

### Q13. "Proffit says implant radiography has been largely superseded by 3D imaging, and he is an author on the voxel-based CBCT method that Nguyen used to get 0.12 ± 0.11 mm between observers. Does that make Nielsen's 2D structural method obsolete, or is it still what we do for every patient without a CBCT?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit (study pages, Ch 2, implant radiography) against the paper's whole premise. Nguyen 2018 (registry): interobserver 0.12 ± 0.11 mm, ICC 0.998, below the 0.3 mm voxel, on 25 growing patients. Proffit's CBCT section (study pages, Ch 6) lists four evidence-supported indications, none of them growth monitoring, and doses of 0.005 mSv for a cephalogram against 0.05 to 0.15 mSv for CBCT. Ponce-Garcia 2018 (Angle Orthod 88:233-245; literature, abstract seen): no 3D cranial-base superimposition study had a gold standard.
- **What you will probably hear:** 2D structural remains the standard for board cases and routine records; 3D for surgery and research. The sharper attendings will separate reproducibility (Nguyen's 0.12 mm) from validity (Nielsen's implants) and note that Nguyen's validity arm rests on bone plates checked clinically, not on tantalum implants.
- **Follow-up:** "Fan 2023 (literature, abstract seen) found Björk's lateral structural superimposition gave the same first-molar and incisor movement as a mini-screw-referenced 3D registration in adolescents. If that holds, is 2D structural good enough for tooth movement and 3D needed only for surfaces?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is 3D better?"

### Q14. "Proffit's growth chapter says the palatal vault's total downward movement exceeds the maxilla's displacement, because the nasal floor resorbs and the oral side apposes, and that maxillary internal rotation is masked by external rotation. If an exam case gives me a growing Class II treated with headgear and asks for the maxillary dentoalveolar change, how should I state which superimposition I used and what it can and cannot show?"
- **Why it lands:** You are putting Proffit's biology (study pages, Ch 2 Fig 2.30; Ch 4 rotation of jaws, 6th ed p 88) next to the paper that quantified its consequence for measurement, which is what an examiner wants. The exam-ready version: structural method on the anterior zygomatic surface with the orbital-floor/nasal-floor rule; palatal best fit under-reads descent and eruption by about a third at the molars and up to half at the incisal edge over 4 years of pubertal growth and under-reads PNS lengthening by half (Tables I to IV); the structural method itself carries a 0.5 mm horizontal offset and about ± 1.5 to 2 mm individual precision (derived).
- **What you will probably hear:** The three-level logic the ABO deck spells out (seen): cranial-base superimposition for total change, regional superimposition for tooth movement within each jaw, the difference is growth or orthopaedic displacement. They will want the method named and the bias direction stated, not the percentage recited.
- **Follow-up:** "Would you quote 30 to 50 % in the exam? I would say about a third at the molars and up to half at the incisal edge over four years, Nielsen 1989, and then give the mechanism."
- **Avoid saying:** "Which superimposition is in Proffit?" Read the chapter.

## Do not ask these

1. "Which method was most accurate?" The abstract says structural matched implants vertically and best fit under-read eruption by 30 to 50 %.
2. "How many patients, and how old?" Eighteen of 35, 6 boys and 12 girls, 10 ± 0.37 y at T1, 4 ± 0.56 y interval (Methods, p 424).
3. "Did they separate treated from untreated, or boys from girls?" No, and the Methods and Discussion both say why: limited sample size.
4. "What is the structural method?" Defined in the Introduction (Fig 3) and the Methods: anterior surface of the zygomatic process, left and right, with orbital-floor apposition set equal to nasal-floor resorption.
5. "Were the tracers blinded to the implants?" The Methods say the best-fit and structural tracings omitted the implants.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Describe the three methods in one breath each."**
Best fit: optimal fit of the hard palate with the nasal floors aligned, registered at ANS and the ANS to point A region. Implant: tantalum implants in the zygomatic process traced on both films and the inter-implant distance bisected, unstable implants excluded. Structural: both zygomatic processes, orbital floors and palatal floor traced, superimposed on the anterior surface of the zygomatic process with the second film oriented so nasal-floor resorption equals orbital-floor apposition, after Björk and Skieller, who actually give the ratio as about 3 : 2 (Fig 2).

**"What were the headline numbers?"**
Over four years best fit under-read vertical displacement at every landmark: PNS by 1.08 mm, ANS by 1.88, point A by 2.24, the molar cusp by 1.33 and the incisal edge by 1.84 mm (Tables III and IV, P < .001). As a share of implant-measured movement that is 28 % at the molar cusp, 39 % at the molar apex, 40 % at the incisor apex and 59 % at the incisal edge (Table II, my arithmetic); the abstract rounds it to 30 to 50 %. Best fit also showed ANS and PNS moving slightly upward and under-read the posterior movement of PNS by half (1.18 vs 2.34 mm).

**"Was the structural method biased at all?"**
Vertically no: implant minus structural was −0.33 to −0.04 mm skeletal and −0.29 to −0.20 mm dental, all non-significant, the structural method reading slightly more descent because apposition and resorption were held at 1 : 1 instead of 3 : 2. Horizontally yes: 0.53 to 0.66 mm, significant at seven of eight landmarks (Tables III and IV). The Discussion calls that shift posterior; the Table I and II means place structural landmarks less posterior than implant ones, so I quote the size and say the direction is not resolvable from the paper.

**"How reliable was it between observers, and what does that mean?"**
A second observer repeated the structural superimposition twice after brief instructions. Differences were significant only vertically, at ANS (0.88 ± 1.29 mm), ANS-Harvold (0.87 ± 1.30), point A (0.77 ± 1.42), UIE (0.86 ± 1.28) and UIA (0.52 ± 0.75); nothing horizontal reached significance. The author attributes the variability to locating the landmarks rather than to the superimposition, and says it was equal across all three methods. No ICC or method error is reported, so "reliable" means no large systematic observer bias, not sub-millimetre agreement.

**"What is the biology behind the best-fit error?"**
Björk's implants showed the nasal floor resorbing downward, more anteriorly than posteriorly, with apposition at the orbital floor at about 3 : 2 (Fig 2, nine boys 4 to 20 y) and the maxilla lengthening at PNS by sutural growth and tuberosity apposition. A palatal registration rides down with the remodelling palate and subtracts it, so ANS appears to rise 0.28 mm when it fell 1.62 mm (Table I); the anterior-dominant resorption rotates the whole maxilla in the frame, giving an average 1.2 mm horizontal difference at every landmark; and a posterior registration at PTM subtracts the 2.3 mm of lengthening. Proffit's growth chapter says the same thing qualitatively (study pages).

**"What level of evidence is this, and would it change what you do?"**
A retrospective method-comparison against an implant reference standard in a quality-selected convenience sample of 18, with the test unnamed and no individual-level agreement statistics: diagnostic level 3 to 4 on the Oxford scale by my mapping (literature, UNVERIFIED). Its value is the implant standard, which cannot be ethically regenerated, and the direction and size of the effect are large and biologically predicted. It would not change what I do, because the ABO already requires the structural method and cites this paper for it; it changes how I read any growing-patient study that superimposed on the palate, including the one we discussed last session.

## Ten-second summary

"Eighteen children with maxillary implants, four years of growth. Superimposing on the palate at ANS hid about a third of molar eruption and up to half of incisor eruption, because the palate itself descends and lengthens. Björk's structural method on the anterior zygomatic surface matched the implants vertically, with a half-millimetre horizontal offset the abstract does not mention and about a millimetre of individual noise. Small, selected sample, but it is the evidence behind the ABO maxillary superimposition we are all expected to use."
