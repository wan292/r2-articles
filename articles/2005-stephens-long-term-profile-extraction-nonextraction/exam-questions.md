# Exam questions: Stephens 2005, long-term profile change after extraction and nonextraction treatment

Source: Stephens CK, Boley JC, Behrents RG, Alexander RG, Buschang PH. Long-term profile changes in extraction and nonextraction patients. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2005;128(4):450-7. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2004.04.034. Baylor College of Dentistry and Saint Louis University.

Design: a retrospective matched cohort from one private practice. There were 20 four-premolar extraction patients and 20 nonextraction patients, matched at the end of treatment and followed for about 15 years. Profile photos were judged by 105 orthodontists and 225 laypeople.

Twelve questions: Q1 to Q7 are single-best-answer MCQs, Q8 to Q11 short answer, and Q12 a case-based oral defence. Tags: R = recall, A = application, N = analysis (4 R, 3 A, 5 N).

Conventions used in the answers:
- Table numbers refer to the paper. Every table value was read from the page images, because the text layer drops minus signs.
- Where the text and a table disagree, the table value is used and the discrepancy is flagged.
- Arithmetic done for this file is labelled derived; the scripts are in `checks.py`.
- Material from outside the paper is labelled (literature) or (Proffit), and marked VERIFIED if seen in a source this run, otherwise UNVERIFIED. Proffit items come from the user's study pages (wan292/proffit-study-pages): VERIFIED in those notes, not against the printed book.

## Reference card

**Sample and design (Methods, pp 451-452; Table I)**
- Patients:
  - 40 white Class I and Class II patients, all treated by the same orthodontist in one private practice.
  - 20 four-premolar extraction and 20 nonextraction patients, 6 males and 14 females in each group.
  - Not reported: the Class I/II split per group, which premolars, treatment time, retention, and any pretreatment value.
- Matching:
  - Matched for age and sex, and on three posttreatment measures: U1-SN, L1-NB and Holdaway's H-line.
  - U1-SN and L1-NB were chosen from a discriminant analysis (ref 11). The H-line was chosen to judge lip protrusion without the nose.
  - The extraction group was selected first, with the three measures "in the normal range"; nonextraction patients were then matched to it.
- Cephalometrics:
  - Digitised with Dentofacial Planner: 15 landmarks (Fig 1) on an x–y grid from posttreatment sella along S-N −7°. Horizontal change (h) is parallel to that line and vertical change (v) perpendicular to it.
  - Nine measurements (Table II).
  - Method error from 15 replicated patients: no systematic error. Table II gives 0.28 to 1.45; the text says "0.3 to 1.4 mm", although three of the nine measures are in degrees.
- Survey:
  - Two versions, each with the posttreatment and long-term profile photos of 20 patients, four pairs per page, left/right order random.
  - Raters: 105 orthodontists, 115 laywomen and 110 laymen. The mailings had a 60 % reply rate.
  - Each rater gave a preference (A, B or neither) and marked the amount of change on a 106-mm visual analogue scale running from "same" to "very different". Perceived change = distance ÷ 106.
- Statistics:
  - t tests between groups; paired t tests for change; a single-sample t test for perceived change; Pearson correlations between perceived and cephalometric change; chi-square for preferences. SPSS, P < .05.
  - No sample-size calculation and no multiplicity correction.

### Q1 (R). Sample and matching

Which statement correctly describes the sample of Stephens et al 2005?

A. Forty white Class I and Class II patients treated by one orthodontist: 20 four-premolar extraction and 20 nonextraction, 6 males and 14 females in each group. They were matched for age and sex, and for U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line on the posttreatment cephalogram, with the extraction cases selected first and their three measures in the normal range.
B. Sixty-three borderline Class II patients, 33 extraction and 30 nonextraction, selected on pretreatment records so that the two groups were morphologically similar before treatment.
C. Forty patients randomly allocated to extraction or nonextraction treatment and followed for 2 years.
D. Sixty-three Class II division 1 patients with stable results, 23 extraction and 40 nonextraction, who differed before treatment.
E. Twenty extraction patients compared with 20 untreated controls from a growth study, matched on pretreatment ANB.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- Methods, pp 451-452, and Table I.
- B is Paquette 1992 and D is Zierhut 2000 (literature, VERIFIED abstracts). The Discussion contrasts both with this study.
- There was no randomisation and no untreated control. The paper takes the untreated patterns from refs 15 and 17.

**Tests:** Recall of the design and the three matching measures; separating this study from the long-term studies it cites.

### Q2 (R). Extraction vs nonextraction over about 15 years

Which statement best summarises Table III?

A. Extraction patients' upper lips moved 2 to 4 mm further behind the E-line than nonextraction patients' lips over the follow-up.
B. In both groups the lips moved significantly further behind the E-line and S-line, and N'-Sn-Pg' opened by 4.0° (extraction) and 2.9° (nonextraction). Upper lip to E-line changed −1.5 and −1.6 mm. No change differed significantly between the groups (P .21 to .82).
C. Nonextraction patients' lips moved forward relative to the E-line, while extraction patients' lips moved back.
D. The H-line measurement decreased significantly in the extraction group, showing loss of lip fullness.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**

- A misuses the Introduction's figure (p 450): extraction patients are 2 to 4 mm flatter at the end of treatment in unmatched samples (refs 3 to 7). That is not a posttreatment change.
- D is wrong: the H-line changed 0.1 and −0.4 mm, NS.

**Tests:** Recall of the main result with its numbers.

### Q3 (N). Direction of the convexity change

Between posttreatment and long-term follow-up, N'-Sn-Pg' rose from 163.6° to 167.6° in the extraction group and from 163.5° to 166.4° in the nonextraction group (Table III). Which interpretation is correct?

A. The soft-tissue profile became more convex, because the convexity angle increased.
B. The soft-tissue profile became less convex, or straighter: the angle opened toward 180°. The Discussion describes the same change as convexity that "decreased considerably", and the abstract calls the male profiles flatter.
C. There was no real change, because the change was within the method error of N'-Sn-Pg'.
D. Extraction profiles straightened significantly more than nonextraction profiles.
E. Only the total convexity, N'-Pn-Pg', changed significantly.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- Table III changes: +4.0° and +2.9°, both P < .05 within group, P = .33 between groups. Males +5.5° and females +2.6° (Table IV, P = .02).
- The wording differs between sections. The Results (p 453) call this an "increase in facial convexity". The Discussion (pp 455-456) says convexity decreased and male profiles were "significantly straighter". The angle is what decides it.
- C is wrong: the method error is 1.07° (Table II), against changes of 2.9 to 4.0°.
- D is wrong: P = .33.
- E is wrong: N'-Pn-Pg' changed 0.9° and −0.2°, NS.

**Tests:** Reading the direction of an angular measure; spotting a wording inconsistency.

### Q4 (A). Predicting a boy's profile

A 15-year-old boy is debonded with his upper lip 5.8 mm behind Ricketts' E-line, the male posttreatment mean in Table IV. According to Stephens' data, the best prediction at about 30 years is:

A. About 6.7 mm behind the E-line, about 0.9 mm further back, as in the females.
B. About 9 mm behind the E-line, about 3 mm further back, mainly because the nose (6.7 mm) and soft-tissue chin (7.9 mm) come forward more than the upper lip (4.1 mm).
C. About 9 mm behind the E-line, because the upper lip moves about 3 mm backward in space.
D. About 3 mm in front of his posttreatment value, because male lips thicken after adolescence.
E. It depends mainly on whether premolars were extracted.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- Table IV: male LS-E went from −5.8 to −9.0 mm, a change of −3.2 ± 2.1 mm, against −0.9 ± 1.5 mm in females (P < .01).
- Table V gives the horizontal changes: Pn-h 6.7, Pg'-h 7.9 and Ls-h 4.1 mm.
- C is wrong: the lip moved forward 4.1 mm. It fell behind a frame that moved forward faster.
- E is wrong: Table III shows no difference by treatment (P .82).
- Proffit (Ch 2, VERIFIED in the notes) gives the same direction.

**Tests:** Applying Tables IV and V to a patient; separating relative from absolute lip retrusion.

### Q5 (N). Evidence behind Conclusion 2

Conclusion 2 attributes the long-term lip retrusion relative to the E-line and S-line mainly to growth of the nose and chin. Which finding in the paper directly shows that the nose and chin came forward more than the lips?

A. The H-line measurement, the perpendicular distance from the H-line to the superior labial sulcus, did not change significantly.
B. In Table V the soft-tissue nose (Pn-h: males 6.7, females 3.1 mm) and chin (Pg'-h: 7.9, 4.0 mm) moved forward more than the upper lip (Ls-h: 4.1, 1.8 mm) and lower lip (Li-h: 5.0, 2.5 mm).
C. Total facial convexity, N'-Pn-Pg', did not change significantly.
D. Orthodontists' perceived change correlated with the change in N'-Pn-Pg' (r = 0.34).
E. U1-SN and L1-NB did not change significantly in either treatment group.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- Table V compares the landmarks directly in one frame (derived: in males the nose gained 2.6 mm on the upper lip and the chin 2.9 mm on the lower lip).
- A is the reason the authors give. It shows unchanged lip curvature, not where the nose and chin went. The H-line also runs to the soft-tissue chin (Holdaway's construction, UNVERIFIED this run), so it is not chin-independent.
- C, D and E say nothing about how far the nose and chin moved relative to the lips. E does argue against an incisor cause.

**Tests:** Telling direct evidence from the authors' stated reasoning.

### Q7 (N). What the design can and cannot answer

You want to answer the question "Does premolar extraction flatten the soft-tissue profile?" What is the most important limitation of Stephens 2005 for that question?

A. The follow-up, about 15 years, is too short to show late soft-tissue change.
B. The groups were matched on posttreatment U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line. Any profile difference produced during treatment was therefore removed by design, so the study tests whether the groups changed differently after treatment, not whether extraction flattens the profile.
C. The method error (0.28 to 1.45) is larger than the changes observed.
D. Too few raters took part in the survey.
E. Treatment by a single orthodontist means the extraction protocol varied between patients.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- Methods, pp 451-452; Discussion, p 454 (the goal was groups similar at posttreatment); abstract, p 450 (the purpose names patients "treated to the same incisor position and lip line").
- Paquette 1992 started with groups similar before treatment and found nonextraction faces about 2 mm fuller (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- A is wrong: 15 years is long.
- C is wrong: LS-E changed 1.5 mm against a method error of 0.34 mm, and N'-Sn-Pg' changed 4.0° against 1.07°.
- D is wrong: there were 330 raters (derived).
- E has it backwards: one operator makes the protocol more uniform.

**Tests:** Linking the allocation method to the question the study can answer.

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q8 (R). Methods

Describe (a) the sample and how the groups were matched, (b) the cephalometric method and measurements, (c) the perception survey, and (d) the statistics.

**Model answer:**
(a) **Sample and matching (Methods, pp 451-452; Table I)**
- 40 white Class I and Class II patients, all treated by the same orthodontist in one private practice.
- 20 four-premolar extraction and 20 nonextraction patients, each group 6 males and 14 females.
- Matched for age and sex, and on three posttreatment measures:
  - U1-SN and L1-NB, the dental protrusion measures picked out by a discriminant analysis (Luppanapornlarp and Johnston, ref 11);
  - Holdaway's H-line, for lip protrusion without the nose.
- The extraction group was chosen first with these measures in the normal range, then nonextraction patients were matched to it.
- Mean ages: about 15 years at posttreatment (group means 14.8 to 15.6) and 28.9 to 30.3 years at long term; interval 14.1 to 15.0 years by group.

(b) **Cephalometrics (Methods, p 452; Fig 1; Table II)**
- Posttreatment and long-term cephalograms were traced and digitised in Dentofacial Planner.
- 15 landmarks (Riolo; Daskalogiannakis) on an x–y grid from posttreatment sella along S-N −7°. Horizontal change was measured parallel to that line and vertical change perpendicular to it (for example Pn-h, Pn-v).
- Nine measurements:
  - total facial convexity N'-Pn-Pg' and facial convexity N'-Sn-Pg';
  - upper and lower lip to Ricketts' E-line and to Steiner's S-line;
  - H-line to the superior labial sulcus;
  - U1-SN and L1-NB.
- Method error from 15 replicated patients: no systematic error; 0.28 to 1.45 (Table II).

(c) **Survey (Methods, p 452; Fig 2)**
- A pilot study set the instructions and the number of patients.
- Two versions, each with posttreatment and long-term photos of 20 patients, balanced for treatment, sex and Class. Four pairs per page, left/right order random.
- 105 orthodontists (meetings, random ADA mailing lists, Baylor alumni; 60 % reply rate to the mailings), 115 laywomen and 110 laymen (waiting rooms, the dental school, family and friends).
- Each rater gave a preference (A, B or neither) and marked the difference on a 106-mm scale from "same" to "very different". Perceived change = distance ÷ 106.

(d) **Statistics**
- Independent t tests between groups; paired t tests for change; a single-sample t test for perceived change; Pearson correlations between perceived and cephalometric change; chi-square for preferences. SPSS, P < .05.
- No sample-size calculation and no correction for multiple testing. Whether Student's or Welch's t was used is not stated.

**Marking points (6):**
1. 20 + 20, four premolars, one orthodontist, white Class I/II, 6 M + 14 F per group.
2. Matched at posttreatment on age, sex, U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line, extraction cases chosen first.
3. About 15 years of follow-up, from about 15 to about 30 years of age.
4. Digitised landmarks on an S-N −7° frame and the nine measures.
5. Survey: 105 orthodontists and 225 laypeople, preference plus a 106-mm scale, 20 patients per version.
6. The tests named.

### Q9 (A). Long-term soft-tissue change and the sex difference

Using Tables III to V, describe the soft-tissue profile changes between about 15 and 30 years of age, explain the difference between males and females, and state how you would use this when finishing a 15-year-old boy and a 15-year-old girl.

**Model answer:**

**What changed (Table III).** Both treatment groups changed the same way.
- Lips moved further behind the E-line: upper lip −1.5 and −1.6 mm, lower lip −1.3 and −1.6 mm.
- Lips moved further behind the S-line by 0.9 to 1.2 mm.
- N'-Sn-Pg' opened by 4.0° and 2.9°, a straighter and less convex soft-tissue profile.
- No change: total convexity N'-Pn-Pg' (0.9°, −0.2°), H-line (0.1, −0.4 mm), U1-SN and L1-NB.
- None of the changes differed between extraction and nonextraction (P .21 to .82).

**Sex difference (Tables IV and V).** Males changed far more.
- Upper lip to E-line −3.2 vs −0.9 mm and lower lip −3.0 vs −0.8 mm (both P < .01); N'-Sn-Pg' +5.5 vs +2.6° (P .02).
- At long term, male lips sat further back: upper lip −9.0 vs −6.4 mm to the E-line.
- The landmark changes (Table V) show why:
  - Nose tip forward 6.7 vs 3.1 mm and down 2.7 vs 0.4 mm.
  - Soft-tissue pogonion forward 7.9 vs 4.0 mm and down 5.6 vs 1.8 mm.
  - Upper lip forward only 4.1 vs 1.8 mm, lower lip 5.0 vs 2.5 mm.
- So the lips did not move backward. The nose and chin outgrew them, by 2.6 and 2.9 mm in males against 1.3 and 1.5 mm in females (derived).
- The Discussion (p 456) explains this by timing: males at about 15 years are only about 1 year past peak adolescent velocity and have more growth to come.
- This agrees with Formby 1994, where untreated males kept straightening after 18 and females did not (literature, VERIFIED abstract), and with Proffit (Ch 2 and Ch 4, VERIFIED in the notes): the nose and chin become more prominent after adolescence, the lips do not, and male nasal growth continues to 16 to 18 years and beyond.

**Use at the chair**
- Plan lip support at debond for everyone, expecting about 1.5 mm of relative lip retrusion over 15 years whatever the treatment.
- In a boy, expect about 3 mm. Avoid finishing him at the flat end of normal, especially with a prominent nose or chin. Where extraction is chosen, control the amount of incisor retraction.
- In a girl, expect about 1 mm; a normal finish is fine.

**Caveats**
- White patients only, finished to normal values.
- The incisors also moved after debond in males: U1-SN +2.6° and L1-NB 2.3 vs 4.0 mm at long term (Table IV). Retention was not reported.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Both groups changed similarly, with no treatment effect, and the lip numbers given.
2. N'-Sn-Pg' opened, meaning straighter, not more convex.
3. H-line and incisors unchanged.
4. Male vs female E-line numbers.
5. Nose and chin outgrow the lips, using Table V numbers, with the timing explanation.
6. A sex-specific finishing plan with a caveat.

### Q10 (N). Appraising Conclusion 1

**Model answer:**

**What supports it**
- Two groups from one operator and one practice, balanced for sex (6/6 and 14/14) and age (Table I).
- The matching worked: posttreatment P .15 to .95 (Table III).
- A long follow-up of about 15 years.
- The same measurements at both time points, with method error reported (Table II).
- Small point estimates for the group difference in change: LS-E 0.1 mm (d 0.05), N'-Sn-Pg' 1.1° (d 0.31); all |d| 0.05 to 0.48 (derived).
- Agreement with other long-term work (literature, VERIFIED abstracts): Zierhut 2000, Erdinc 2007, Rathod 2015 against untreated controls, and Bravo Vallejo 2024 at about 35 years.

**What limits it**
1. *What it can answer.* Matching at posttreatment removes the treatment effect by design. The study tests whether the groups age differently, not whether extraction changes the face. Paquette 1992, with groups similar before treatment, found nonextraction faces about 2 mm fuller (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
2. *Selection.*
   - Extraction cases were chosen with normal values, so over-retracted extraction and protrusive nonextraction faces were left out; this biases toward no difference.
   - The number screened, the Class I/II split per group, pretreatment data, treatment time and retention are not reported.
   - Only patients with complete long-term records were selected, and the number eligible or recalled is not given, so attrition and its direction are unknown.
3. *Power.*
   - No sample-size calculation was done.
   - With 20 per group the minimum detectable effect is d 0.91: 1.5 to 1.9 mm for lip change and 3.3° for N'-Sn-Pg'. Power for d 0.5 is 0.34 (derived).
   - The 95 % CI of the LS-E change difference is −1.22 to 1.42 mm (derived).
   - So "demonstrating" overstates it: a difference was not detected, and equivalence was not tested.
4. *Measurement.*
   - The arm difference in N'-Sn-Pg' change (1.1°) is about the size of its method error (1.07°).
   - The registration of the long-term film is not described, and tracer blinding is not stated.
5. *Generalisability.* White patients, about 15 years at debond, Class I/II, four premolars, one practice. The result does not cover adults, other ethnic groups, bimaxillary protrusion or Class III.
6. *No untreated control.* The normal growth pattern is taken from the literature (refs 15 and 17).

**Level of evidence.** Oxford CEBM 2011 level 3 (2b on the 2009 table); GRADE-style certainty low to very low (appraisal mapping, derived).

**What survives.** When incisors and lips finish alike, extraction does not change how the profile ages by more than about 1.5 mm over 15 years (derived, from the 95 % CIs of the lip-change differences). "Not due to treatment" should be read as "no difference detected".

**Marking points (6):**
1. Strengths with the posttreatment P values.
2. Matching at posttreatment and what it means, with the contrast to Paquette.
3. Selection on normal values and the bias direction.
4. Power or CI with a number.
5. External validity.
6. Level of evidence and a correctly worded conclusion.

### Q11 (N). Appraising the perception arm and the reporting

Appraise the survey: what it found, what it can and cannot show, and the reporting points an examiner might raise.

**Model answer:**

**Findings (Tables VI and VII; Results, pp 453-454)**
- Orthodontists and laypeople perceived 33.9 to 39.2 % change, all different from zero (P < .01).
- Treatment, sex or rater type made no significant difference to perceived change (P .45 to .95), and the two rater types agreed (r 0.89).
- Only 1 of 18 correlations with the cephalometric changes was significant: N'-Pn-Pg' for orthodontists, r 0.34, P .03.
- Preferences split about evenly overall. Individual patients drew strong preferences: chi-square significant for 87.5 % of patients; about 90 % of orthodontists preferred the long-term photo for one case and 80 % the posttreatment photo for another.
- The authors conclude that what we measure cephalometrically may not be what people look at, and that no one can predict who will age better (Discussion, p 456).

**Strengths**
- A large rater pool (330, derived) with both professional and lay raters.
- Random left/right order and a pilot-tested instrument.
- A continuous visual analogue scale as well as a forced preference.

**Limits**
1. *No blinding to time point.* The photos showed the same person at about 15 and about 30 years, so ageing cues were visible. Photo standardisation over 15 years is not described.
2. *Multiplicity.* With 18 correlations, 0.9 false positives are expected and P(at least one) is 0.60. The critical |r| for n = 40 is 0.312, so r 0.34 is compatible with chance (derived).
3. *Unit of analysis.* Each rater saw only 20 patients (20 pairs, so 40 profile photos; the abstract's "40 profiles" fits that reading, not 40 patients). The comparisons in Table VI reproduce with the patient as the unit (derived), so n is 20 per group, not 330.
4. *Wording against the tables.*
   - Preferences of "approximately 30%" each: Table VII cells run from 26.5 to 42.3 % and each row sums to about 100 %; three times 30 is 90 (derived).
   - Variation "7.3% to 14.9%" in the text: the Table VI SDs run from 6.9 to 14.1.
   - The 87.5 % chi-square sentence speaks of differences between orthodontists and laypeople. A later sentence in the same paragraph says their preferences for individual patients were similar, and Conclusion 5 calls the 87 % clear preferences. The likely meaning is a non-uniform choice for each patient.
   - Conclusion 5 rounds 87.5 % to 87 %.
5. *Silhouettes.* Silhouettes would have removed skin, hair and age texture (compare Bishara and Jakobsen 1997, literature, VERIFIED abstract).

**Other reporting points**
- *Convexity wording.* The Results call the N'-Sn-Pg' change an increase in convexity, while the Discussion and abstract call it a decrease, or flatter. The angle opened by 2.6 to 5.5° (Tables III and IV), a straighter profile.
- *Table V.*
  - The Li-h/Li-v and UIA-h/UIA-v P values recompute as transposed (Li-h .029 printed NS, Li-v .38 printed .03, UIA-h .34 printed .01, UIA-v .007 printed NS).
  - Its title says soft tissue but it lists dental landmarks and B point, and no sign convention is printed.
- *Table IV.* The L1-NB change P of .08 matches Welch's test (.088); Student's test gives .036 (derived).
- *Labels.*
  - Tables III and IV print "N'-Pr-Pg'" for N'-Pn-Pg'.
  - The abstract writes "no significant (P < .05)" where NS is meant.

**What survives.** People notice that faces change over 15 years. Cephalometric lip lines do not predict what they notice. Neither treatment produced a consistently preferred long-term profile.

**Marking points (6):**
1. The perceived-change numbers and r 0.89.
2. 1 of 18 correlations, with the multiplicity point.
3. The preference findings, including 87.5 % and the inconsistency across cases.
4. Raters not blind to age and photo standardisation not reported.
5. At least two text-versus-table points, stated neutrally.
6. A conclusion on what the survey can support.

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q12 (A). "Will taking out four teeth make my son's face sink in when he grows up?"

**Case.** A 13-year-old boy has a Class I molar relationship and about 6 mm of maxillary and 7 mm of mandibular crowding. His upper incisors are upright, his upper lip is about 2 mm behind the E-line, his nose is prominent and his chin is average. Growth is ongoing. You propose four-premolar extraction as one option. His mother says her sister had four premolars removed as a teenager and "looks sunken now". She asks whether extraction will spoil his face in the long run.

**Examiner question 1. What does Stephens 2005 tell you that is relevant to her question?**
Model answer:
- Forty white Class I and II patients from one orthodontist were treated with and without four-premolar extraction, matched at debond on incisor position (U1-SN, L1-NB) and lip line (H-line), and followed for about 15 years.
- The two groups changed identically. Upper lip to E-line −1.5 vs −1.6 mm (P .82), and N'-Sn-Pg' opened 4.0 vs 2.9° (P .33). At about 30 years no measure differed (P .10 to .99) (Table III).
- Raters saw changes but showed no consistent preference, and the changes were not related to treatment type (Tables VI and VII).
- So if the incisors and lips are finished in the same place, extraction does not make the face age worse.

**Examiner question 2. What can that paper not tell you?**
Model answer:
- Whether extraction changes the face during treatment. The groups were matched after treatment, so any treatment difference was designed out.
- Paquette 1992 started with groups similar before treatment and found nonextraction faces about 2 mm fuller (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- The Konstantonis 2018 meta-analysis puts the treatment-period difference at about 1.3 mm for the upper lip and 2.0 mm for the lower lip, very low certainty (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Stephens also chose extraction cases already finished to normal values, so it says nothing about the over-retracted case, which is probably what the aunt's face reflects.

**Examiner question 3. What will happen to his profile between 15 and 30 whatever you do?**
Model answer:
- In Stephens' males, the upper lip moved 3.2 mm further behind the E-line and the lower lip 3.0 mm, against 0.9 and 0.8 mm in females. N'-Sn-Pg' straightened by 5.5° against 2.6° (Table IV).
- This is because the nose came forward 6.7 mm and the chin 7.9 mm while the upper lip came forward only 4.1 mm (Table V). Males at 15 are only about 1 year past peak velocity (Discussion, p 456).
- With a prominent nose already, I would expect at least this much.
- Proffit (Ch 2 and Ch 4, VERIFIED in the notes) says the same: nose and chin growth continues after adolescence, longer in males, while the lips do not grow forward. So lip support is planned at the end of treatment.

**Examiner question 4. How do you decide between extraction and nonextraction here?**
Model answer:
- 6 to 7 mm of crowding sits in Proffit's borderline zone of about 5 to 9 mm. There the decision rests on the hard and soft tissues and the final incisor position (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes; check the thresholds in print).
- Points against heavy retraction:
  - upright incisors;
  - lips already 2 mm behind the E-line;
  - a prominent nose;
  - a male who will lose about 3 mm of relative lip support over 15 years.
- Proffit adds that a large nose or chin argues for keeping the incisors forward and that the lower lip should be at least as prominent as the chin (VERIFIED in the notes).
- My lean would be nonextraction with arch development, or interproximal reduction if the periodontium allows, or extraction with anchorage set to close space mainly from behind. Either way the incisors should not be retracted.
- Stephens' message: decide the target incisor and lip position first; extraction versus nonextraction is the means, not the outcome.

**Examiner question 5. If you do extract, how do you avoid the sunken look?**
Model answer:
- Control incisor retraction through anchorage, so that space closes largely by mesial movement of the posterior teeth.
- Finish the incisors and lips within the normal range, not at the flat end.
- Re-evaluate the profile before space closure is complete.
- Proffit's notes describe the lips following incisor retraction only partly, until lip competence is reached (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes, ratio to be checked in print).
- The amount of retraction, not the extraction, is what changes the lips. Konstantonis 2018 lists the extraction protocol and the amount of incisor retraction as modifiers (literature, VERIFIED abstract).

**Examiner question 6. Will people even notice the difference?**
Model answer:
- In Stephens, orthodontists and laypeople perceived about a third of the scale as change over 15 years (33.9 to 39.2 %). They did not perceive significantly more change in extraction patients (Table VI, P .63 to .95).
- Their perceptions did not track the cephalometric numbers (1 of 18 correlations significant).
- Boley 1998 found that professionals identified extraction cases only 54 % of the time, close to chance (literature, VERIFIED abstract; Stephens quotes about 50 %).
- So a well-finished extraction case is not recognisable as one.

**Examiner question 7. How strong is this evidence?**
Model answer:
- A retrospective matched cohort of 20 per group, with no sample-size calculation and no untreated control.
- With these numbers it could miss a difference in lip change of up to about 1.5 to 1.9 mm (derived). Rater age cues and multiple testing weaken the survey.
- Oxford level 3; low to very low certainty on a GRADE-style judgement.
- Its message is consistent with Zierhut 2000, Erdinc 2007, Rathod 2015 and Bravo Vallejo 2024 (about 35-year follow-up), all VERIFIED abstracts. That consistency, not this sample alone, is why I trust it.

**Examiner question 8. Tell the mother in one or two sentences.**
Model answer: "Long-term studies show that faces treated with and without extractions age the same way when the teeth and lips are finished in the same place. All faces flatten a little as the nose and chin grow, boys more than girls, and we plan for that by not pulling his front teeth back too far."
