# Questions to ask about this paper

Stephens CK, Boley JC, Behrents RG, Alexander RG, Buschang PH. Long-term profile changes in extraction and nonextraction patients. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2005;128(4):450-7. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2004.04.034. Baylor College of Dentistry and Saint Louis University. Patients came from one private practice. Forty white Class I and Class II patients, 20 four-premolar extraction and 20 nonextraction, were matched at the end of treatment and followed for about 15 years. A profile-photo survey of 330 raters was added (derived: 105 + 115 + 110).

The headline is easy: extraction does not dish the face in the long term. So a good question shows that you know what the matching did to that headline, and what Tables IV to VII add to it.

The groups were made alike at debond. Extraction cases were chosen first, with U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line "in the normal range", and nonextraction cases were matched to them (Methods, pp 451-452). The paper therefore tests whether the two groups age differently. It does not test whether extraction changes the face. The richer material is elsewhere: the sex difference (Tables IV and V), and the survey (Tables VI and VII), where people saw a change but not the change the cephalometrics measured.

Every table value below was read from the page images, because the text layer drops minus signs. Material from outside the paper is labelled (literature), (Proffit) or (registry), and marked VERIFIED if it was seen in a source this run, otherwise UNVERIFIED. Proffit items come from the user's study pages (wan292/proffit-study-pages): they are VERIFIED in those notes, not against the printed book. Arithmetic done for this file is labelled derived; the scripts are in `checks.py`.

How to ask. The session is a casual discussion and mixing Arabic with English is normal, so ask the way you talk. Name the table first and keep the question to one breath. For example: "بالـ Table III الـ N'-Sn-Pg' زاد من 163.6 إلى 167.6°، يعني الـ profile صار straighter مو more convex، صح؟" Frame any wording or table issue as "typo or wording?", never as an error. Boley and Behrents are co-authors and their own work is cited here (Boley 1998, ref 27; Behrents 1985, ref 18). Several people in the room will have extracted in borderline cases. Let the tables make the point, not you.

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "In Table III, N'-Sn-Pg' goes from 163.6 to 167.6° in the extraction group and from 163.5 to 166.4° in the nonextraction group. The Results call that an increase in facial convexity. The Discussion says convexity decreased considerably, and the abstract says the male profiles became flatter. The angle opened toward 180°, so I read it as a straighter, less convex profile. Is that how we should say it?"
- **Why it lands:** You read the angle, not the sentence.
  - Changes in Table III: +4.0° (Ext) and +2.9° (Nonext), both significant within the group, P = .33 between groups. Males +5.5° and females +2.6° (Table IV, P = .02).
  - Wording: the Results (p 453) say "increase in facial convexity", and "greater increases" for males. The Discussion (pp 455-456) says convexity "decreased considerably" and male profiles were "significantly straighter". The abstract says male profiles "became flatter". All of these describe the same angle opening.
  - Literature: Zierhut 2000 found that both groups flattened progressively, and Formby 1994 found that the adult male profile straightened (literature, VERIFIED abstracts).
  - The Results wording is the one most easily copied into a summary, so it is worth settling in the room.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement. N'-Sn-Pg' is the soft-tissue convexity angle without the nose, and a larger angle means a straighter face. Someone may say the Results meant "the angle of convexity increased". That is fine; the point is the direction.
- **Follow-up:** "Total convexity with the nose, N'-Pn-Pg', did not change: 0.9° and −0.2°, NS. So with the nose included the face kept its shape, and it straightened only when the nose is left out. Does that fit what you see in patients in their late twenties?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did the face get more convex?" or "the paper contradicts itself". Ask which word to use.

### Q2. "They matched the groups at the end of treatment on U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line. Paquette started with borderline cases that were similar before treatment, found the nonextraction faces about 2 mm fuller, and the difference was still there at recall. So is the fair reading of Conclusion 1 that extraction does not change how the face ages, rather than that extraction does not change the face?"
- **Why it lands:** This is the most important point about the design, and the paper states it.
  - Methods (pp 451-452) describe the matching. The Discussion (p 454) says the goal was a sample similar at posttreatment.
  - Table III confirms the matching worked: every posttreatment P is .15 or more.
  - The Discussion itself contrasts two designs. Zierhut: groups different before treatment, similar after it. Paquette: similar before treatment, different after it, about 2 mm.
  - Paquette, Beattie and Johnston 1992 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): 33 extraction and 30 nonextraction borderline Class II patients, 14.5 years after treatment, nonextraction about 2 mm fuller.
  - Proffit (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes): comparable extraction and nonextraction cases differ by about 2 mm of lip fullness.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes. The study answers "if I finish both at the same incisor and lip position, will extraction make the face age worse?", and the answer is no. The lip change during treatment is a separate question, where the effect is small but real: Konstantonis 2018 meta-analysis, extraction vs nonextraction, lower lip 1.96 mm and upper lip 1.26 mm more retraction, very low certainty (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- **Follow-up:** "The extraction cases were chosen with incisors and H-line in the normal range. That leaves out the over-retracted extraction case, which is the one the dished-in worry is about. Does that make the long-term answer optimistic for the cases we actually worry about?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Were the groups similar before treatment?" No pretreatment data are given, and the question sounds as if you missed that they were matched at the end.

### Q3. "Table IV: from about 15 to 30 years the male upper lip went from −5.8 to −9.0 mm behind the E-line, a change of −3.2 mm, against −0.9 mm in females, P < .01. Table V says the male nose came forward 6.7 mm and the chin 7.9 mm, but the upper lip only 4.1 mm. If I am debonding a 15-year-old boy, should I finish his lips a little fuller than a girl's?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns Tables IV and V into a finishing decision.
  - Derived from Table V: in males the nose gained 2.6 mm on the upper lip and the chin gained 2.9 mm on the lower lip; in females 1.3 and 1.5 mm.
  - The Discussion (p 456) places males at 15 years only about 1 year past peak adolescent velocity, with more growth to come than females.
  - Proffit (Ch 2 "Growth of the nose", VERIFIED in the notes): the nose and chin become more prominent after adolescence and the lips do not, so lip support should be planned at the end of treatment. Ch 4 (7th-edition box, VERIFIED in the notes): male nasal growth peaks at about 13 to 14 years and continues to 16 to 18 years and beyond.
  - Nanda 1990 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): the male nose was not at adult size at 18 years.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, within limits; that is Proffit's position. Someone may point out that the E-line measures the frame, not the lip. The male upper lip itself moved 4.1 mm forward and 3.5 mm down (Ls-h, Ls-v, Table V); it just moved less than the nose and chin.
- **Follow-up:** "Females' upper lips still lost 0.9 mm to the E-line, which was significant, and their chin came forward 4.0 mm. Formby found that adult female lips did not retrude after 18. Is the female change here just the tail of adolescence?" (Formby 1994, 18 to 42 years; literature, VERIFIED abstract.)
- **Avoid saying:** "Do boys grow more than girls?"

### Q4. "Raters perceived 33.9 to 39.2 % change, and the Results describe the preferences as roughly 30, 30 and 30. But in Table VII, for male patients, on average 42.3 % of orthodontists picked the posttreatment photo against 29.0 % for the long-term one. For female patients, 38.3 % of laypeople picked the long-term photo against 30.3 % for the posttreatment one. Is a pattern hiding inside the 30, 30, 30?"
- **Why it lands:** You read Table VII rather than the summary sentence.
  - The cells run from 26.5 to 42.3 % and each row sums to about 100 %. Three times 30 is 90 (derived).
  - The Results (p 454) say orthodontists leaned toward the posttreatment photo in every group, and laypeople toward the long-term photo in every group except males. None of these differences was significant.
  - Individual cases split hard: about 90 % of orthodontists preferred the long-term photo for one case, and 80 % preferred the posttreatment photo for another.
  - The Discussion (p 456) cites Lines et al: straighter profiles are preferred for males and more convex ones for females.
- **What you will probably hear:** Not a real pattern. The SDs in Table VII run from 12.4 to 26.5 points, the unit is the patient, and individual faces drive the choice. Someone may cite Bishara and Jakobsen 1997: right after treatment, laypeople rated the extraction profile changes more favourably, but at about 2 years no group was preferred (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- **Follow-up:** "Mendes 2019 rated a similar 15-year recall on a 0 to 10 scale, and mean attractiveness fell in every group: 4.76 to 4.32 for nonextraction and 4.53 to 4.33 for four-premolar extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Is 'no clear preference' the same as 'no change in attractiveness'?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did people prefer the extraction faces?" The abstract answers that: there were no significant differences.

## Tier 2. Growth of the nose, chin and lips

### Q5. "Conclusion 2 says the E-line and S-line change must come from nose and chin growth, because the H-line, which is independent of the nose, did not change. But the H-line runs from the upper lip to the soft-tissue chin, so it includes the chin, and Table II defines the measurement as the depth of the superior labial sulcus. Isn't Table V the better evidence, with the nose and chin coming forward more than the lips?"
- **Why it lands:** It separates the right conclusion from the weaker reason given for it.
  - Table II defines the H-line value as the perpendicular distance from Holdaway's H-line to the superior labial sulcus. The Methods (pp 451-452) say the H-line was chosen because it judges the lips without the nose.
  - The H-line did not change: 0.1 / −0.4 mm by treatment (Table III) and −0.1 / −0.2 mm by sex (Table IV), all NS.
  - The H-line construction, tangent to the upper lip and soft-tissue pogonion, is Holdaway's standard definition and is not spelled out in the text (UNVERIFIED this run).
  - Table V gives the direct comparison. Males: Pn-h 6.7 and Pg'-h 7.9 mm against Ls-h 4.1 and Li-h 5.0 mm. Females: 3.1 and 4.0 mm against 1.8 and 2.5 mm.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement. Sulcus depth reflects lip curvature and strain, not lip position against the nose. Someone may add that lip thickness was not measured, and that Proffit's adult-growth section (Ch 4, Behrents data, VERIFIED in the notes) says the lips flatten and thin with age.
- **Follow-up:** "If the lips thin with age, could part of the E-line change be thinning that a sulcus-depth measure would not show?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is the H-line?"

### Q6. "The matched incisor values separated by sex. Male U1-SN rose 2.6°, a significant change, to 109.6° against 103.0° in females at long term, P = .01. L1-NB ended at 2.3 against 4.0 mm, P = .02. The Discussion does not mention this. Is it late mandibular growth moving the NB line, something about retention, or real incisor movement?"
- **Why it lands:** Few people read the incisor rows of Table IV.
  - At debond the males already had 3.2° more U1-SN (107.0 vs 103.8°, P .15). By long term the gap was 6.6° (derived).
  - Table V hints at the mechanism. In males B point came forward 5.1 mm and the lower incisal edge 3.9 mm, so the NB line gained about 1.2 mm on the incisor (derived), which shrinks L1-NB even if the incisor does not move within bone.
  - For the upper incisor: the edge moved forward more than the apex (4.7 vs 4.2 mm) and the apex moved down more than the edge (4.6 vs 3.1 mm). That is consistent with some proclination (derived; my read). The P values of the UIA row look transposed (see Q11), but the means are what matter here.
  - Retention protocol and duration are not reported.
  - Sex is balanced across arms (6 and 6 males, 14 and 14 females, Table I), so this does not bias the extraction comparison.
- **What you will probably hear:** Late forward mandibular growth in males, with the lower incisors uprighting against a basal bone that keeps coming forward (UNVERIFIED this run). Nobody can say more without the retention history.
- **Follow-up:** "If the dentition kept moving differently by sex after debond, does 'treated to the same incisor position' still describe these patients at 30?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did the incisors relapse?" The paper gives no data on that.

### Q7. "Bishara 1997 found that posttreatment trends were similar in males and females. Here, males changed more than three times as much on the E-line. Bishara followed patients for about 2 years and Stephens for about 15. Is the sex difference simply a matter of when you look?"
- **Why it lands:** It reconciles an apparent conflict with numbers instead of picking a side.
  - Bishara, Cummins and Zaher 1997 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): 45 extraction, 46 nonextraction and 35 untreated controls, about 2 years after treatment, similar trends in both sexes.
  - Stephens: LS-E −3.2 vs −0.9 mm (Table IV); about 3.6 times (derived).
  - The Discussion (p 456) says 15-year-old males are about 1 year past peak velocity.
  - Formby 1994: males kept straightening between 18 and 42 years, while the female profile did not (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
  - Behrents, a co-author here, wrote the adult-growth monograph cited as ref 18.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes. Male soft-tissue maturation runs past 18 years, and a 2-year window catches little of it.
- **Follow-up:** "Table I gives the interval as 14.1 to 15.0 years by group, but the SDs reach 4.9 years. With a window that wide, should the changes have been adjusted for interval or age?"
- **Avoid saying:** "When does the nose stop growing?"

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q8. "Landmarks were placed on an x–y grid from posttreatment sella along S-N −7°. The paper does not say how the 30-year film was registered on the 15-year film. If S-N rotates or nasion moves with growth, could part of each h and v change in Table V be the frame rather than the face?"
- **Why it lands:** It links this paper to the superimposition topic already in the hub.
  - Methods (p 452) and Fig 1 describe the frame. The registration of the second film is not described.
  - The topic-1 papers in the hub (Nielsen 1989, Doppel 1994, Nguyen 2018; registry) deal with regional superimposition. There is no conflict, but here the method is unstated.
  - Every landmark in Table V moved forward and down, as whole-face growth looks in a cranial-base frame.
- **What you will probably hear:** The usual approach is S-N registered at sella, which is acceptable for group comparisons. Nasion can move forward with frontal growth (UNVERIFIED this run).
- **Follow-up:** "The conclusions rest on differences, nose against lip and group against group, measured in the same frame. So does the registration matter much for the main result?" This invites agreement that the main result is robust.
- **Avoid saying:** "What is S-N −7°?"

### Q9. "Conclusion 1 says the results demonstrate that the long-term changes were not due to treatment. With 20 per group, the smallest difference in lip change the study could reliably detect is about 1.5 to 1.9 mm. The 95 % confidence interval for the difference in upper-lip-to-E-line change runs from −1.2 to +1.4 mm. Should we say 'no difference found' or 'no difference'?"
- **Why it lands:** It is the statistical point, made with numbers and without an accusation.
  - The paper gives no sample-size calculation.
  - Derived (`checks.py`): at 80 % power with 20 per group, the minimum detectable effect is d = 0.91. In units: 1.5 to 1.9 mm for the lip lines, 3.3° for N'-Sn-Pg' and 0.96 mm for H-line. Power to detect d = 0.5 is 0.34.
  - Observed differences in change were small: LS-E 0.1 mm (d 0.05) and N'-Sn-Pg' 1.1° (d 0.31) (derived).
  - Derived 95 % CIs of the change difference: LS-E −1.22 to 1.42 mm; N'-Sn-Pg' −1.21 to 3.41°.
  - The arm difference in N'-Sn-Pg' change (1.1°) is about the size of its method error (1.07°, Table II).
- **What you will probably hear:** A 1 mm difference over 15 years is below what anyone would see, and the point estimates are tiny. The other long-term studies agree: Zierhut 2000, Erdinc 2007, and Bravo Vallejo 2024 at about 35 years (literature, VERIFIED abstracts). The statistically minded will accept "absence of evidence" as the correct phrase.
- **Follow-up:** "So the strength of the conclusion comes from the literature agreeing, not from this sample alone?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Was it powered?"

### Q10. "Extraction cases were picked first with the three matching measures in the normal range, and nonextraction cases were matched to them, all from one orthodontist. We are not told how many records were screened, the Class I and II split per group, treatment time, retention, or any pretreatment value. Who does this result not apply to?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns a list of gaps into a question about external validity.
  - Methods (pp 451-452). Table I gives only ages. The survey section says each version had about equal numbers of Class I and Class II patients, but no split per group is given.
  - Bias direction (derived reasoning): leaving out over-retracted extraction faces and protrusive nonextraction faces narrows both groups toward the middle and pushes any group difference toward the null.
  - The result covers white patients about 15 years old at debond, Class I or II, four premolars removed, finished to normal values. It does not cover adults, other ethnic soft-tissue norms, bimaxillary protrusion, Class III, or extraction done to retract a protrusive profile.
  - Proffit (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes): in Black patients, extraction retruded the lips more.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that this is a best-case, well-finished sample. The useful message is about the finish, not the extraction.
- **Follow-up:** "Would you use this paper to reassure the parent of a bimaxillary-protrusion patient?"
- **Avoid saying:** "How many patients were there?"

### Q11. "In Table V, two pairs of P values look swapped. Li-h is printed NS and Li-v .03, but from the printed means and SDs I get about .03 for Li-h and .38 for Li-v. UIA-h is printed .01 and UIA-v NS, but I get .34 and .007. Is that a typo, or a test I am not reproducing?"
- **Why it lands:** The arithmetic is clean and the question is neutral.
  - Derived (`checks.py`, Student t, n 12 / 28):
    - Li-h: 5.0 ± 3.2 vs 2.5 ± 3.2 mm, P .029 (printed NS).
    - Li-v: 2.6 ± 3.5 vs 1.6 ± 3.2 mm, P .38 (printed .03).
    - UIA-h: 4.2 ± 2.9 vs 3.3 ± 2.6 mm, P .34 (printed .01).
    - UIA-v: 4.6 ± 3.3 vs 1.9 ± 2.5 mm, P .007 (printed NS).
  - Welch's test gives the same pattern.
  - A related point: the sex difference in L1-NB change is printed P = .08. Student's t gives .036 and Welch's .088 (derived), and the paper does not say which was used.
  - Two smaller points about Table V: its title says "soft-tissue", but it includes UIE, UIA, LIE, LIA and B point; and the sign convention is not printed.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Probably a transposition." It does not change the story, because the nose and chin rows (Pn-h, Pg'-h, Pg'-v) recompute cleanly.
- **Follow-up:** "Then the lower-lip difference by sex is horizontal, which fits the E-line result in Table IV?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The table is wrong."

### Q12. "The raters compared a 15-year-old's photo with the same person at 30. That age difference is visible, so they could not have been blind to which photo was which. Only 1 of 18 correlations with the cephalometric changes was significant: N'-Pn-Pg' for orthodontists, r = 0.34. With 18 tests, about one false positive is expected. Does the survey show that cephalometric lines miss what people see, or that people mainly saw ageing?"
- **Why it lands:** It appraises the paper's most original part.
  - Methods (p 452): two survey versions of 20 patients each, four pairs per page, left and right order randomised, a 106-mm visual analogue scale.
  - Each rater saw 20 patients, that is 20 pairs or 40 profile photos. The abstract's "among the 40 profiles" fits that reading; it does not mean 40 patients per rater.
  - The paper does not describe how the photos were standardised over 15 years.
  - Derived: 18 × 0.05 = 0.9 false positives expected, and P(at least one) = 0.60. The critical |r| for n = 40 is 0.312, so r = 0.34 barely clears it.
  - The Discussion (p 456) asks whether what we measure on cephalograms is what people actually look at.
- **What you will probably hear:** Both readings. The authors' point is fair, but the survey cannot separate ageing cues from profile change.
- **Follow-up:** "Would cropped silhouettes, like those Bishara and Jakobsen used, have been a fairer test?" (Bishara and Jakobsen 1997 had 39 laypeople rate silhouettes; literature, VERIFIED abstract.)
- **Avoid saying:** "Did the raters see a change?" Table VI and the Results (p 453) answer that: P < .01.

## Tier 4. Proffit and the exam

### Q13. "Proffit's growth chapter says the nose and chin become more prominent after adolescence while the lips do not, so lip support should be planned at the end of treatment. This paper seems to put numbers on that: about 1.5 mm of relative lip retrusion to the E-line over 15 years in both groups, and about 3 mm in males. Is that the figure you would quote?"
- **Why it lands:** It pairs the textbook statement with the data that quantify it, which is what an examiner wants.
  - Proffit (Ch 2 "Growth of the nose", VERIFIED in the notes).
  - Proffit Ch 4, adult growth from the Behrents data (VERIFIED in the notes): the nose lengthens, the lips flatten and thin, and the soft-tissue chin grows.
  - Stephens: LS-E −1.5 / −1.6 mm (Table III); −3.2 mm in males and −0.9 mm in females (Table IV). Behrents is a co-author.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, with two caveats: these are white patients from about 15 to 30 years, and the E-line moves with the nose.
- **Follow-up:** "Proffit adds that the lips sag with age, so less of the upper incisor shows. Stephens did not measure incisor display. Would you add that to long-term counselling?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does the nose grow in adults?"

### Q14. "In Proffit's extraction guidelines, about 5 to 9 mm of crowding is the borderline zone, decided by the hard and soft tissues and the final incisor position, and a large nose or chin argues for keeping the incisors forward. If Stephens says the long-term course is the same when incisors and lips finish alike, is the exam answer that the decision is about the target incisor and lip position, not extraction as such?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns the paper into a decision rule.
  - Proffit (Ch 7 "Contemporary extraction guidelines", VERIFIED in the notes; check the exact thresholds in print): under about 4 mm of crowding, extraction is rarely needed; at 5 to 9 mm, either approach; at 10 mm or more, almost always extraction. The lower lip should be at least as prominent as the chin.
  - Stephens Conclusion 1, and p 456: orthodontists cannot predict who will age better.
  - Konstantonis 2018 (literature, VERIFIED abstract) quantifies the effect during treatment.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, with the male caveat and a reminder about anchorage, which decides how far the incisors go back.
- **Follow-up:** "In a 15-year-old borderline boy with a large nose and chin, does Table IV push you toward nonextraction, or toward extraction with less incisor retraction?"
- **Avoid saying:** "So should we extract or not?"

## Do not ask these

1. "How long was the follow-up, and how old were they?" About 15 years. Group means were 14.8 to 15.6 years at posttreatment and 28.9 to 30.3 years at long term (Table I).
2. "Were the groups similar at debond?" They were matched; posttreatment P values run from .15 to .95 (Table III).
3. "Did the extraction faces end up flatter at 30?" No: the long-term P values run from .10 to .99 (Table III), and the abstract says so.
4. "Who did the ratings?" 105 orthodontists, 115 laywomen and 110 laymen, with a 60 % reply rate to the mailings (Methods, p 452).
5. "Did people prefer the extraction or the nonextraction profiles?" The abstract reports no significant differences between treatment groups, between sexes, or between orthodontists and laypeople.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Summarise the design in one breath."**
A retrospective matched comparison from one private practice and one orthodontist. Forty white Class I and Class II patients: 20 treated with four premolar extractions and 20 without, each group 6 males and 14 females. They were matched at the end of treatment on age, sex, U1-SN, L1-NB and H-line, with the extraction cases chosen first. Cephalograms were taken at about 15 years of age and again about 15 years later, digitised with Dentofacial Planner, with 15 landmarks and nine profile measures. Profile photos from both time points went to 105 orthodontists and 225 laypeople, who marked a preference and the amount of change on a 106-mm scale.

**"What were the headline numbers?"**
- Both groups changed the same way: upper lip to E-line −1.5 and −1.6 mm (P .82), lower lip −1.3 and −1.6 mm, and S-line −0.9 to −1.2 mm. N'-Sn-Pg' opened by 4.0° and 2.9° (P .33). H-line, U1-SN and L1-NB did not change (Table III).
- Males changed more. Upper lip to E-line −3.2 vs −0.9 mm and N'-Sn-Pg' +5.5 vs +2.6° (Table IV). Nose 6.7 vs 3.1 mm forward and chin 7.9 vs 4.0 mm (Table V).
- Raters perceived about 36 % change and agreed with each other (r 0.89). Their perceptions did not track the cephalometric change (1 of 18 correlations significant), and they showed no consistent preference.

**"Why did the lips become more retrusive if Table V says they moved forward?"**
The E-line and S-line are drawn from the nose and the soft-tissue chin, and both grew forward more than the lips. In males the nose moved 6.7 mm and the chin 7.9 mm against 4.1 mm for the upper lip (Table V). So the lips fell behind a frame that was moving forward; they did not move backward in space. The H-line sulcus depth did not change, which the authors read as unchanged lip fullness (Conclusion 2).

**"What do the E-line, S-line and H-line measure here?"**
Table II gives the distance in mm from labrale superius and labrale inferius to Ricketts' E-line and to Steiner's S-line, and for the H-line the perpendicular distance from Holdaway's H-line to the superior labial sulcus. Negative values mean the lip is behind the line. The standard constructions, which the paper does not spell out (UNVERIFIED this run):
- E-line: nose tip to soft-tissue pogonion, so it is the most nose-dependent.
- S-line: from the middle of the S-curve of the nose to soft-tissue pogonion, so it is less nose-dependent.
- H-line: tangent to the upper lip and soft-tissue chin, so it leaves out the nose but includes the chin.

**"What did the perception survey show?"**
- Both orthodontists and laypeople saw a small-to-moderate change: 33.9 to 39.2 % of the scale, all P < .01 (Table VI).
- There were no significant differences in perceived change by treatment, sex or rater type, and the two rater types agreed (r 0.89).
- Perceived change did not track the cephalometric change. Only N'-Pn-Pg' correlated, for orthodontists, at r 0.34; with 18 correlations that is about what chance gives (derived).
- Preferences split roughly evenly overall, but sharply for individual patients (87.5 % significant by chi-square), with no consistency across cases.
- I would add that the raters could see the 15-year age difference.

**"What level of evidence is this, and does it change what you do?"**
It is a retrospective matched cohort of 20 per group, with no sample-size calculation and no untreated control: Oxford CEBM 2011 level 3 (2b on the 2009 table), and low to very low certainty on a GRADE-style judgement (appraisal mapping, derived). It does not change the extraction decision. It changes the counselling and the finish:
- extraction itself does not make the face age worse;
- everyone's lips fall back about 1.5 mm against the nose and chin over 15 years, and males about 3 mm;
- so I plan lip support at the end of treatment, especially in boys.

The wider long-term literature agrees: Zierhut 2000, Erdinc 2007, Rathod 2015, Bravo Vallejo 2024 (literature, VERIFIED abstracts).

## Ten-second summary

"Forty patients from one practice, 20 extraction and 20 nonextraction, matched at debond on incisor position and lip line and followed for 15 years. Both groups changed the same way. The lips dropped about 1.5 mm behind the E-line, not because the lips went back but because the nose and chin grew forward, about twice as much in males. Raters saw the change but had no consistent preference. Bottom line: extraction does not change how the profile ages if you finish the incisors and lips in the same place."
