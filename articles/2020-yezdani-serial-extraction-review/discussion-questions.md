# Questions to ask about this paper

Arif Yezdani A, Nithya Nandhini P, Padmavati R. Serial extraction in orthodontics – review article. Eur J Mol Clin Med 2020;7(2):6432-41. Sree Balaji Dental College and Hospital, Bharath Institute of Higher Education and Research, Chennai. There are exactly three authors, so the citation takes no "et al". No DOI and no received or accepted dates are printed.

The paper is a short narrative review. It has no methods section, no patients, no tables and no statistics. It is built from definitions, lists and three textbook extraction sequences, supported by 16 references (7 of them textbooks; newest book 2008, newest journal item 2004; counts derived). The journal is a multidisciplinary title whose Scopus coverage was discontinued after 2020 (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).

Good questions about it are therefore not "what did they find". They are "where does this rule come from, and does it still hold". Its value for you is as the exam vocabulary sheet for serial extraction: the definitions, indications, contraindications, the Dewel, Tweed and Nance codes, and the two phases. You get your edge in the room in three ways:
- reading it against Proffit Ch 12 (pp 442-446), which you can quote;
- knowing the outcome studies it does not cite (Little 1990, Wilson 1999, O'Shaughnessy 2011, Lopes Filho 2015);
- noticing where its own text, captions and lists disagree.

Every page reference below is a journal page (6432-6441), checked on the page images. Anything from outside the paper carries a label:
- (Proffit 7th, p N): read in the 7th-edition PDF text in this run (VERIFIED).
- (Proffit notes, Ch N): VERIFIED in the user's Proffit study pages (6th-edition summary), not checked against the printed book.
- (literature): VERIFIED means the abstract or full text was read in this run; VERIFIED-secondary means the number was seen only in a full-text copy on a secondary site or in a later summary; UNVERIFIED means not checked.
- (past exam): a recalled promotion question, from the promotion folder or the past-year SBO recall on the Proffit study pages. "Keyed" is the answer the recall gives.
- (my read): my own interpretation.

How to ask: the session is a casual discussion, so keep each question to one breath and open with the page or the figure ("On p 6438 ..."). Many consultants learnt serial extraction from the same textbook tradition this paper summarises (Graber, Tandon, Rani, the CD4 and D4C codes), and some will teach it exactly as printed. Frame any wording problem as "which version do you teach?" or "did I read it right?". Keep the discussion on the rule, never on the journal or the authors.

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "On p 6438 Tweed and Nance are both coded D4C. The Tweed bullets take the first premolar out together with the primary canine, but the Fig 3b caption takes the premolar first and the primary canine after it, which is the Nance sequence in Fig 3c. Which version do you teach for Tweed? And clinically, why leave the primary canine in once the D is out?"
- **Why it lands:** As printed, two of the "three methods" share a code (p 6438), and their caption sequences are identical (Figs 3b and 3c, p 6439). The more common teaching gives Tweed as the D first, then the 4 and the C at the same visit, and Nance as D, then 4, then C (literature, VERIFIED-secondary; teaching sources disagree). That matches the Tweed text, not the Fig 3b caption. Proffit names none of the three methods; he describes one C-first sequence (Proffit 7th, pp 443-445).
- **What you will probably hear:** "Tweed takes the 4 and the C together; Nance staggers them." Some will say they never use either and follow the C-first order. A likely reason for keeping the C: it holds the canine position and the incisors while the premolar comes through (my read).
- **Follow-up:** "If the lower canine is already ahead of the premolar on the OPG, is a D-first method safer than Dewel's C-first?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is Dewel's method?" It is on p 6438 with a figure.

### Q2. "The first indication is an arch deficiency of 8 to 10 mm or more. Proffit puts it above 10 mm per arch, and his Ch 7 treats 5 to 9 mm as the zone where either plan is possible. Is the paper's figure per arch? And with 8 mm in a Class I child with a good profile, would you start serial extraction or hold the leeway space?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6433 gives no arch, no total and no measurement method, and the contraindication "mild to moderate crowding" (p 6435) has no number. That leaves the band between moderate and 8 mm undefined. Proffit's figures:
  - serial extraction is best with no skeletal problem and more than 10 mm per arch; with smaller discrepancies expect more residual space (Proffit 7th, p 443);
  - Class I extraction guidelines: under 4 mm rarely, 5 to 9 mm either way depending on hard and soft tissues, 10 mm or more almost always extraction (Proffit 7th, pp 221-222).

  O'Shaughnessy 2011 entered patients with more than 8 mm of crowding, judged visually (literature, VERIFIED-secondary). Both past-year stems on this used 10 mm or more (past exam, Ch 12, 2020 and 2024).
- **What you will probably hear:** "10 mm per arch, from a mixed dentition analysis. Below that, a lingual arch and wait."
- **Follow-up:** "Which mixed dentition analysis do you trust here? The paper names Carey's for the lower arch and arch perimeter for the upper, and uses the OPG for a radiographic analysis." Nance's analysis is the radiographic one (past exam, Ch 11, 2020, keyed).
- **Avoid saying:** "What are the indications?" They take up pp 6433-6435.

### Q3. "Dewel's method is timed by age: the primary canine at 8.5 years and the primary first molar a year later. Proffit times the lower D by the first premolar root, at half to two thirds formed. When the calendar and the OPG disagree, which wins? And if the lower canine is clearly going to beat the premolar, do you enucleate?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6438 and the Fig 3a caption give ages only. The paper lists knowledge of calcification as a requirement (p 6435) and the OPG for root development (p 6437), but never names a root stage or mentions enucleation. Proffit 7th (pp 444-445):
  - in the lower arch the canine usually erupts before the first premolar and is displaced facially;
  - extract the lower D at half to two thirds of first-premolar root formation to speed the premolar;
  - if the premolar still lags, it can impact and need surgical removal;
  - it can be enucleated with the D, but this is best avoided, because an erupting premolar brings bone with it and early enucleation can leave a lasting defect.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The radiograph wins. Enucleate only when the premolar is obviously going to be late."
- **Follow-up:** "Is this only a lower-arch problem? Proffit says the upper first premolar usually erupts before the canine (p 444)."
- **Avoid saying:** "When do you extract the D?"

### Q4. "One indication is unilateral loss of a primary canine with a shift to that side (Fig 2c). Proffit's 7th edition says early loss of an upper primary canine almost never needs immediate treatment. For the lower, he cites growth-study data in which the midline shift after losing one primary canine was no bigger than with normal eruption, 1.3 vs 1.0 mm. Do we still do a balancing extraction here, or wait for the space analysis?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6434 (Fig 2c caption: with incisor crowding, the loss leads to a midline shift and lost canine space) against Proffit 7th, pp 396-397. Proffit treats early canine loss as a signal for a space analysis, with the incisor position and profile deciding what follows. The past-year recalls take the conservative line (past exam, Ch 11, 2024, keyed):
  - an 8-year-old with early loss of an upper primary canine: no treatment;
  - a 9-year-old with bilateral loss of the upper primary canines: no treatment, wait for comprehensive care.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Upper: leave it. Lower: a passive lingual arch, and balance only if the midline is really moving."
- **Follow-up:** "And if that space analysis shows 10 mm, is that the moment to turn it into a serial extraction?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What does early loss of a primary canine mean?"

## Tier 2. Mechanics and biology

### Q5. "The paper lists deepening of the bite and residual space between the canine and second premolar as disadvantages, and deep bite as a contraindication. Proffit says that as the teeth drift without appliances, the lower incisors tip lingually and the overbite often increases, and the spaces close mostly by distal eruption of the canines. In a child with 11 mm of crowding and an overbite already at the upper limit, is serial extraction off the table?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6435 (both lists) against Proffit 7th (pp 444-445; the Fig 12.37 caption on p 446 describes the residual space and nonparallel roots between the canine and second premolar). It asks whether the deep bite is permanent or a stage. Persson 1989 (literature, VERIFIED abstract):
  - 44 Class I crowded patients had all four first premolars removed at a mean of 10½ years as the only treatment;
  - 42 of them were re-examined at a mean of 30 years 4 months, with no harmful effect on overjet or overbite;
  - on average, crowding developed to about the level of a nonextraction normal-occlusion sample.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Plan the fixed phase from the start; a lower lingual arch stops the incisors collapsing." Someone may point out that a lingual arch also holds the molars and limits the drift that closes the space (my read).
- **Follow-up:** "So does the lingual arch fight the space closure we want from drift?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does serial extraction deepen the bite?" p 6435 says so.

### Q6. "The only complication listed is anterior crossbite, in three forms: dentoalveolar, skeletal and functional (pp 6439-6440). How does a serial extraction produce one? Do the upper incisors tip lingually after the primary canines go? Or is the section really about crossbites to screen for before starting?"
- **Why it lands:** The section heading, the figure and the caption describe three different things (pp 6439-6440, checked on the page image):
  - Fig 4 is a slide of crossbite photographs;
  - its caption describes a flat face, a prominent chin, an aged look and lingual incisor inclination, which reads like over-retraction;
  - the cited source (ref 16) is a two-page 1966 item.

  Proffit lists lingual tipping of the lower incisors and a deeper bite, not anterior crossbite (Proffit 7th, p 444). Ask it as a reading question.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Correct any anterior crossbite first, usually with a 2×4. A functional (pseudo Class III) crossbite has to be ruled out before you take teeth." A 2×4 is the keyed answer for an 8-year-old with edge-to-edge, spaced upper incisors and no posterior crossbite (past exam, Ch 11, 2020), and a single central incisor in crossbite was keyed to a lingually placed tooth bud (past exam, Ch 11, 2022).
- **Follow-up:** "Is that why the paper calls Class III 'not suitable'? Lingual tipping of the upper incisors in an edge-to-edge child would push them into crossbite (my read)."
- **Avoid saying:** "What are the types of anterior crossbite?" They are listed on p 6440.

### Q7. "The paper allows serial extraction in the upper arch only for a Class II maxillary alveodental protrusion, but calls Class III 'not suitable'. Proffit allows the mirror image, lower teeth only, for a minor to moderate Class III, and warns that the residual space then has to close in one direction and needs real biomechanics. Has anyone here done a lower-only sequence?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6436 against Proffit 7th, pp 443-444. The paper's own lists also sit uneasily together: p 6433 makes Class I with no skeletal disproportion an indication, and severe Class II or III is a contraindication (p 6435), yet p 6436 accepts a Class II upper-only sequence.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Rarely. Class III growth is unpredictable; we would rather wait." For Class II: "Upper 4s only, with headgear or a TAD to hold the molars."
- **Follow-up:** "If only the upper 4s go early in a Class II, how do you stop the upper molars drifting forward into the space?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Can you do serial extraction in Class II?"

### Q8. "Kouvelis 2018, paper 4 of this topic, found that four-premolar extraction has no specific effect on the skeletal vertical dimension. In growing children, Quinzi 2023 found that the mandibular and occlusal plane angles fell after serial extraction, compared with untreated controls. The paper calls the backward-rotating Class II retrognathic child with an open bite 'not a good case' (p 6437). In a high-angle 8-year-old with severe crowding, does serial extraction help the vertical, hurt it, or do nothing?"
- **Why it lands:** It links paper 3 to paper 4 of the topic.
  - Kouvelis 2018 (`2018-kouvelis-extraction-vertical-dimension`): 14 retrospective studies, none pooled; no specific skeletal vertical effect (literature, VERIFIED).
  - Quinzi 2023 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): retrospective, 78 children aged 8.5 ± 1.4 years, 52 treated with serial extraction with or without expansion against 26 untreated controls. The mandibular and occlusal plane angles fell in the treated groups, and the upper gonial angle changed by −0.44 ± 0.6 a year with serial extraction alone and −0.34 ± 0.5 with expansion plus extraction, against −0.04 ± 0.6 in the controls (three-group P = .036; no unit in the abstract, degrees presumed). It is retrospective and small.
  - The paper itself lists open bite as a contraindication (p 6435).
- **What you will probably hear:** "The vertical is growth, not extraction," which fits Kouvelis. Some will add that extraction helps close an anterior open bite dentally.
- **Follow-up:** "Then why does Proffit's Ch 7 list a severe vertical discrepancy as one of the few reasons to extract with under 4 mm of crowding (Proffit 7th, p 221)?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does extraction close the bite?"

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q9. "The paper lists 'more stable results' and a shorter retention period as advantages, with one citation, Dewel 1959, for the whole list. Little followed 30 serial-extraction patients at least ten years out of retention: 22 had unsatisfactory lower alignment, no better than matched late-extraction cases. What would we base a stability claim on today?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6435 against the outcome literature:
  - Little, Riedel & Engst 1990 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): 22 of 30 (73 %) unsatisfactory; intercanine width and arch length fell in 29 of 30; no difference from a matched late-extraction sample.
  - Little 1990, *Br J Orthod*, which is topic 19, paper 2 (literature, VERIFIED, full text): serial-extraction cases were no better aligned than late-extraction cases, with success under 30 %; 14 second-premolar serial extractions showed no difference from first-premolar ones.
  - Lopes Filho 2015 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): early extraction showed less relapse, but the six included studies were all retrospective and of low to moderate quality, and the authors judged the evidence insufficient.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Retain everyone: a bonded lower retainer." Someone may cite Lopes Filho.
- **Follow-up:** "Little did find that alignment usually improved during the drift stage before treatment (topic 19, paper 2). Is that the real benefit: easier treatment rather than a more stable result?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is serial extraction stable?"

### Q10. "The paper says serial extraction shortens multibanded treatment, and a few lines lower, under disadvantages, that it means 2 to 3 years of multiple visits. O'Shaughnessy compared 51 serial with 49 late-extraction patients: a shorter fixed phase, but longer total time and more appointments. For a family who lives far away, which is the exact stem of the 2023 and 2024 promotion questions, what do we tell them?"
- **Why it lands:** Both sides are on p 6435 and never reconciled. O'Shaughnessy 2011 (literature, VERIFIED abstract):
  - serial-extraction patients started fixed appliances with a lower PAR (P < .001) and finished with a similar PAR (P = .27);
  - their active treatment was shorter (P < .001), but total time and total appointments were greater (P < .001).

  From a full-text copy (literature, VERIFIED-secondary):
  - PAR at the start of fixed appliances was 15.18 vs 31.27, and at the end 2.61 vs 3.08;
  - total chair time did not differ (P = .07);
  - the serial group was 7.95 years at T0 and 13.11 years at the start of fixed appliances, about 5.2 years apart (derived), well beyond the paper's "2-3 years".

  Wagner & Berg 2000, the paper's own ref 6, point the same way (literature, VERIFIED abstract). The past-year recalls (past exam, 2023 item 14 and 2024 item 36) test exactly this split between active and total time.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Shorter in brackets, longer under supervision. For a family far away, late extraction is simpler."
- **Follow-up:** "The serial group started fixed treatment with half the PAR score. Is the shorter active phase a benefit of serial extraction, or just what drift does to a case before we start?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is serial extraction faster?"

### Q11. "The reference list stops at 2008 for books and 2004 for journals, and leaves out the outcome studies: Little, Wilson, O'Shaughnessy, Lopes Filho. If a junior resident wanted one source on serial versus late extraction, would you send them to Lopes Filho's 2015 review, or are six retrospective studies with no meta-analysis still too thin?"
- **Why it lands:** The reference counts are derived from pp 6440-6441:
  - 16 references, 7 of them textbooks;
  - 10 of the 15 dated references come before 1990, with a median year of 1982;
  - ref 12 (Proffit, 2nd ed) has no year.

  Lopes Filho 2015 (literature, VERIFIED abstract) found similar crowding correction, less relapse and a shorter active phase with early extraction, but called the evidence insufficient; the six studies held 219 early and 199 late patients (literature, VERIFIED-secondary). The question is about the evidence, not the journal.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Proffit Ch 12 plus Lopes Filho. There is no RCT."
- **Follow-up:** "Could we ever randomise 8-year-olds to serial extraction or waiting? What would the outcome even be?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is this a good journal?"

## Tier 4. Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "Proffit says serial extraction by itself almost never gives ideal tooth position or space closure, calls it an adjunct to comprehensive treatment rather than a substitute, and says it was overused 25 to 30 years ago and is perhaps underused now. The paper's conclusion presents it as interceptive treatment and never mentions the fixed phase. In this department, who still gets a serial extraction?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6440 against Proffit 7th (pp 443 and 445). Proffit's Fig 12.37 (p 446) shows a serial extraction without fixed appliances with an excellent result; its caption adds that properly timed serial extraction usually leaves incomplete space closure and nonparallel roots between the canine and second premolar. Studies of serial extraction without appliances (literature, VERIFIED abstracts) show it does real work, short of a finished result:
  - Yoshihara 1999 and 2000: irregularity fell, and canine movement and tipping helped correct the anterior crowding.
  - Persson 1989: spontaneous alignment and space closure into adult life.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Severe crowding, no skeletal problem, a reliable family, and a plan for fixed appliances afterwards."
- **Follow-up:** "So for the exam: more than 10 mm per arch, no skeletal problem, and fixed appliances to finish. Is that the line you want to hear?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Do we still do serial extraction?"

### Q13. "For the borderline case, Proffit's advice is to keep the options open. Take the primary canines early if the incisors need room, which also lowers the risk of canine impaction, but put in a lower lingual arch to hold the leeway space. Where is your line between that and starting a serial extraction: a number, or the lips and chin?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit 7th, p 446. It ties two papers in this topic together:
  - Dewel's first step is the same primary canine extraction that Kokich 2004 (paper 1, `2004-kokich-impacted-maxillary-canines`) recommends for a palatally displaced maxillary canine. The present paper never mentions this benefit. The past-year recalls key primary canine extraction for an ectopically erupting upper canine (past exam, Ch 11, 2019) and "extract both primary canines" on Proffit's Fig 11.43 (past exam, Ch 11, 2020 and 2023).
  - "Congenital absence of teeth providing space" is a contraindication (p 6435). That is the situation of Kokich & Kokich 2006 (paper 2, `2006-kokich-missing-mandibular-second-premolars`).

  The past-year recalls give soft tissue as the deciding factor in moderate crowding (past exam, Ch 7, 2020, 2022 and 2024, keyed "chin and lip").
- **What you will probably hear:** "The lips and the incisor inclination decide, not the millimetres alone."
- **Follow-up:** "If we take the primary canines early for a displaced maxillary canine, as Kokich 2004 advises, have we started a serial extraction without meaning to?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is leeway space?"

### Q14. "The paper says extraction in a Class I alveodental retrusion gives a dished-in face, and the Fig 4 caption describes a flat face, prominent chin and aged look. Wilson 1999 found no soft-tissue profile difference between serial extraction with or without treatment and late extraction. Stephens 2005, paper 5 of this topic, found no long-term profile difference when the incisors were finished in the same place. So is the dished face a risk of extraction, or of extracting for the wrong face?"
- **Why it lands:** p 6436 and p 6440 against:
  - Wilson 1999 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): serial extraction without treatment 28, serial extraction with treatment 30, late extraction 30; no significant soft-tissue profile differences.
  - Stephens 2005 (`2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`): no difference on 9 profile measures at about 15 years, in groups matched on incisor position and the H-line.

  Proffit's Ch 7 notes put the average extraction-nonextraction difference in lip fullness at about 2 mm (Proffit notes, Ch 7). A bimaxillary four-premolar case was recalled with an expected upper-lip retraction of 2-3.2 mm (past exam, 2023 item 11, keyed).
- **What you will probably hear:** "Extracting for the wrong face. The profile decides, not the extraction itself."
- **Follow-up:** "We make the decision at 8, before the nose and chin grow. Stephens showed the lips falling behind the E-line mainly because of nose and chin growth. Does that make the profile call at 8 harder?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does extraction flatten the face?"

## Do not ask these

Items 1 to 5 are answered in the paper itself, so asking them shows it was not read.

1. "Who coined the term serial extraction?" Kjellgren, 1929 (p 6433).
2. "What are the indications and contraindications?" They are listed on pp 6433-6435.
3. "What is Dewel's sequence?" C, then D, then 4 (p 6438, Fig 3a).
4. "What records do we need?" Photographs, IOPA, lateral ceph, OPG and study models (p 6437).
5. "What is the reversible phase?" Extraction of anterior primary teeth in the first transitory period (p 6435).
6. "Is this a predatory journal?" or "Why is an undergraduate an author?" Both are about people, not content, and nobody in the room can answer them. Q9 to Q11 make the evidence point properly.
7. "Why is this not a systematic review?" It calls itself a review article and does not claim to be one. Ask about the evidence behind a specific claim instead.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Define serial extraction and give its principles."**
The paper quotes Proffit: the timed extraction of primary and ultimately permanent teeth to relieve severe crowding (p 6433). Proffit 7th (p 443) has the same wording. Hotz called it "guidance of eruption" (p 6433); Hotz 1970 is Proffit's own reference for the section (Proffit 7th, ref 40, p 447). It rests on two principles (Graber 1971, ref 5): a discrepancy between arch length and tooth material, and physiologic tooth movement, which means the teeth drift and erupt into the spaces (p 6433). Proffit's modern framing is that it is an adjunct to later comprehensive treatment, not a substitute (p 443).

**"Who is the ideal candidate?"**
The ideal candidate, as the paper describes it (p 6433):
- a Class I malocclusion with no skeletal disproportion;
- a normal overbite and a good profile;
- severe crowding of 8 to 10 mm or more, with no physiologic spacing.

Warning signs that add to the case include lateral incisors erupting lingually or palatally, early or asymmetric loss or resorption of the primary canines with a midline shift, and labial recession of a lower incisor (pp 6433-6435). Contraindications (p 6435) are mild to moderate crowding, congenitally missing teeth, deep or open bite, severe Class II or III, cleft lip and palate, spacing or diastema, and dilacerations. For the exam, give Proffit's version: more than 10 mm per arch with no skeletal problem (Proffit 7th, p 443).

**"Walk me through the sequence."**
Dewel's C-D-4 (p 6438) is the same order Proffit describes (Proffit 7th, pp 444-445):
1. Primary incisors if needed, then the primary canines. In the paper this is at about 8.5 years. The incisors align, and crowding is usually controlled for 1 to 2 years.
2. The primary first molars, a year later in the paper. In Proffit, the lower D comes out when the first premolar root is half to two thirds formed, so that the premolar erupts before the canine.
3. The first premolars, as they erupt.
4. The canines erupt distally into the premolar spaces. The spaces close partly by mesial drift of the posterior teeth, but mostly by distal eruption of the canines.

Then fixed appliances, because drift alone usually leaves residual space, nonparallel roots and a deeper bite (Proffit 7th, p 445; Fig 12.37, p 446).

**"What is the difference between Dewel, Tweed and Nance?"**
- Dewel (CD4, 1954): the primary canine first, at 8.5 years, then the D, then the 4.
- Tweed and Nance are both D-first, at 8 years.
  - Tweed: the 4 and the C come out together once the 4 erupts (p 6438 text).
  - Nance: the 4 first, then the C (p 6439).
- The paper's Fig 3b caption gives Tweed the Nance order, so if asked, I would quote the text version and say the caption differs.
- Proffit uses only the C-first order (pp 443-445).

**"What can go wrong?"**
- From the paper (p 6435): a long programme with many visits, reliance on cooperation, possible tongue thrust, a deeper bite, and residual space between the canine and the second premolar. The only complication it names is anterior crossbite (p 6439).
- From Proffit (pp 444-445):
  - the lower incisors tip lingually;
  - rotations resolve worse than labiolingual displacements;
  - an early D extraction can still leave the 4 behind the canine and impacted;
  - enucleation can leave a bone defect;
  - without fixed appliances, the alignment, root positions, overbite and space closure are usually not ideal.

**"Is serial extraction more efficient than late extraction?"**
The fixed phase is shorter, but the total time and the number of visits are greater. In O'Shaughnessy 2011 the active phase was shorter (P < .001), but total time and total appointments were greater (P < .001), and the final PAR was similar (P = .27) (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Wagner & Berg 2000 agree (literature, VERIFIED abstract). The paper says both things on p 6435. This is the answer to the 2023 and 2024 recalls.

**"Is it more stable?"**
There is no evidence that it is. Little 1990 found 73 % unsatisfactory lower alignment at least 10 years out of retention, no better than late extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstract). The paper's "more stable, less retention" claim has no data behind it (p 6435). Everyone needs the same retention.

**"If it is no more stable and takes longer overall, why do it at all?"**
- It reduces crowding and irregularity during the transition and lets the teeth erupt over the alveolus and through keratinised tissue instead of being displaced buccally or lingually (Proffit 7th, pp 442-443).
- It makes the later comprehensive treatment easier and often quicker (Proffit 7th, p 443).
- Little 1990, *Br J Orthod* (topic 19, paper 2; literature, VERIFIED full text) reaches the same view: it still makes sense to reduce the severity of crowding, speed the following treatment and keep erupting teeth within the attached gingiva. The paper's own indication of labial stripping or recession of a lower incisor (Fig 2d, p 6434) is the same gingival argument.
- Early primary canine extraction lowers the chance of canine impaction (Proffit 7th, p 446).

**"What records do you need, and why the OPG?"**
The paper lists (p 6437):
- photographs before, during and after treatment;
- an IOPA and a lateral ceph;
- an OPG, to find missing or supernumerary teeth, carry out a radiographic and mixed dentition analysis, judge dental age, root development and the likely eruption pattern, and exclude pathology;
- study models, with Carey's analysis for the lower arch and arch perimeter analysis for the upper.

Root development on the OPG is what times the D (Proffit 7th, p 444).

**"Early loss of a primary canine in an 8-year-old: what do you do?"**
- Upper: almost never needs immediate treatment (Proffit 7th, p 396). That is the keyed past-year answer (past exam, Ch 11, 2024).
- Lower: treat it as a sign of crowding and do a space analysis. A passive lingual arch is an option. A balancing extraction is no longer considered necessary, because the midline shift was no greater than with normal eruption, 1.3 vs 1.0 mm (Proffit 7th, p 397).
- The paper's indication covers unilateral loss with a midline shift and crowding (p 6434). If the analysis shows severe crowding, that is where serial extraction starts.

**"What level of evidence is this paper?"**
Oxford CEBM Level 5. It is a narrative review, with no search strategy, data or appraisal, and 16 references, 7 of them textbooks. Treat it as a summary of textbook teaching. The evidence on serial extraction itself is retrospective: Little 1990, Wilson 1999 and O'Shaughnessy 2011, summarised by Lopes Filho 2015, which found the evidence insufficient to choose between early and late extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstracts).

## Ten-second summary

"Yezdani 2020 is a short narrative review, not a study. It defines serial extraction as timed extraction of primary teeth and then the first premolars for severe crowding: 8 to 10 mm or more in a Class I child with no skeletal problem, a normal overbite and a good profile. It gives three sequences: Dewel's C-D-4, starting with the primary canines at 8.5, and Tweed's and Nance's D-first methods at 8. Read it with Proffit: more than 10 mm per arch, time the D by premolar root formation, and finish with fixed appliances. The outcome studies show a shorter fixed phase but longer total time and more visits, and no better long-term stability than late extraction."
