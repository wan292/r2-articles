### PE1. Severe crowding in the mixed dentition (Ch 12; 2020 and 2024 recalls)

**2020 recall** (Proffit study pages, Ch 12; the same stem is item 100 in the crash-course section of the promotion folder's 2021 recall file). A 9-year-old girl has 10 mm of crowding. The OPG shows the primary canines and primary first molars already extracted and the permanent canines erupting. What is the best treatment?
A. Serial extraction
B. Expansion

**2024 recall** (Proffit study pages, Ch 12). A panoramic radiograph is shown; the patient has severe crowding of more than 10 mm. What is the best intervention?
A. Serial extraction
B. Wait for comprehensive treatment

**Recalled answer:** A in both.
**Answer per the paper:** A. The first indication is severe crowding with an arch deficiency of 8-10 mm or more (p 6433). The OPG is listed for mixed dentition analysis and for judging root development (p 6437).
**Explanation:**
- Proffit agrees: serial extraction is best with no skeletal problem and more than 10 mm per arch (Proffit 7th, p 443). His Class I extraction guideline says 10 mm or more almost always needs extraction (Proffit 7th, p 222).
- Mixed-dentition expansion does badly long term. In Little's sample of 26 patients whose arch length was actively increased in the mixed dentition, 20 ended with a net loss of arch length and 89 % had unsatisfactory alignment after retention (Little 1990, Br J Orthod; topic 19, paper 2; literature, VERIFIED full text).
- In the 2020 stem the C and D are already out and the canines are erupting. The goal of the sequence is to get the first premolar out before the canine erupts (Proffit 7th, p 444), so the next step is the first premolars (my read).

**Flag:** None. The recall agrees with the paper and with Proffit.

### PE2. Serial versus late premolar extraction for a family who lives far away (2023 and 2024 recalls)

**2023 recall** (R2 promotion exam 2023, item 14, promotion folder). The parents live far from the clinic and are unsure whether to choose serial extraction or late extraction. Which statement is correct? Only the recalled answer survives: serial extraction has a shorter active phase with the orthodontic appliance.

**2024 recall** (R2 promotion exam 2024, item 36, promotion folder). The patient lives far from the clinic and is worried about the number of visits. What is the difference between late premolar extraction and early serial extraction?
A. Serial extraction: longer treatment time
B. Late premolar extraction: longer treatment time

**Recalled answer:** 2023: shorter active appliance phase with serial extraction. 2024: no answer recorded.
**Answer per the paper:** p 6435 says both things:
- Advantage: serial extraction reduces the duration of multibanded treatment. That is the 2023 answer.
- Disadvantages: "prolonged treatment time with multiple visits (2-3 years)" and multiple extraction visits. That makes A the 2024 answer.

**Explanation:**
- O'Shaughnessy 2011 compared 51 serial-extraction with 49 late-extraction patients (literature, VERIFIED abstract):
  - the active (fixed) phase was shorter with serial extraction (P < .001);
  - total time and total appointments were greater (P < .001);
  - the final PAR was similar (P = .27).
- Wagner & Berg 2000, the paper's own ref 6, found the same: a shorter fixed phase, but more appointments and a longer total duration (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- For a family who lives far away, serial extraction means more visits over more years.

**Flag:**
- The 2024 recall has no recorded key; A is supplied here.
- The two recalls do not conflict: one asks about the active phase, the other about total time and visits.

### PE3. Early loss of a primary canine (Ch 11; 2024 recall)

**2024 recall** (Proffit study pages, Ch 11). An 8-year-old has lost a maxillary primary canine early. What is the best management?
A. Space maintainer
B. No treatment is required
C. Extraction of the contralateral primary canine (balancing)

**Recalled answer:** B.
**Answer per the paper:** The paper lists unilateral loss of a primary canine with a shift to the same side as an **indication for serial extraction** (p 6434). The Fig 2c caption adds incisor crowding, a midline shift and lost space for the permanent canine. It does not address an isolated upper loss without crowding.
**Explanation:**
- Proffit 7th (p 396): early loss of one or both upper primary canines almost never needs immediate treatment.
- For a lower primary canine (p 397), the older teaching was to extract the other one and fit a lingual arch. Growth-study data then showed the midline shift was no greater than with normal eruption (1.3 vs 1.0 mm), so a balancing extraction is no longer considered necessary. A passive lingual arch remains an option.
- In both arches, early canine loss is a reason to do a space analysis, including incisor position and profile (p 396). Serial extraction follows only if that analysis shows severe crowding.

**Flag:** Partial disagreement. The paper's indication is broader and older than Proffit's current advice. For the exam, choose B; give serial extraction only when the stem adds severe crowding.

## Reference card

**Identity and history (pp 6432-6433)**
- Abstract: serial extraction, or "guidance of eruption", is planned, sequential extraction of certain primary teeth and then specific permanent teeth in the early mixed dentition, to encourage spontaneous correction. It is part of interceptive orthodontics.
- History:
  - Bunon (1743) first explained it (UNVERIFIED outside the paper).
  - Kjellgren (1929) coined the term. Kjellgren himself dates the name to 1929 (literature, VERIFIED: Kjellgren 1948), although the paper's ref 14 is a 1947 item.
  - Nance (1940) is called the "Father of serial extraction"; the label is usually given for the United States (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).
  - Pioneers: Hotz, Tweed, Dewel and Jack Dale. Hotz renamed it "guidance of eruption" (Hotz 1970, Am J Orthod 58:1-20, carries that title, and it is Proffit's reference for the section; literature, VERIFIED; Proffit 7th, ref 40, p 447).
- Definitions:
  - Proffit: timed extraction of primary and ultimately permanent teeth to relieve severe crowding (matches Proffit 7th, p 443).
  - Dewel: early removal of selected primary and permanent teeth in a predetermined sequence.
  - Tandon: timed removal in mixed-dentition cases with dentoalveolar disproportion.
- Two principles (ref 5, Graber 1971): an arch length-tooth material discrepancy, and physiologic tooth movement.

**Indications (pp 6433-6435; 12 bullets, count derived; ref 6 on the last bullet)**
1. Severe crowding with an arch deficiency of 8-10 mm or more.
2. Class I with no skeletal disproportion, skeletal-muscular harmony, normal overbite and a good skeletal profile.
3. Tooth size-jaw size discrepancy.
4. No physiologic spacing (Fig 2a).
5. Lateral incisors erupting lingually (Fig 2b).
6. Unilateral primary canine loss with a shift to the same side (Fig 2c).
7. Malposed or impacted lateral incisors erupting palatally.
8. Abnormal or asymmetric primary canine root resorption.
9. Labial stripping or gingival recession, usually of a lower incisor (Fig 2d).
10. Mesial eruption of the canines over the laterals.
11. Abnormal eruption direction or sequence.
12. "Ankylosis, etc."

**Contraindications (p 6435; 9, numbered in the paper; ref 6)**
1. Congenital absence of teeth providing space.
2. Mild to moderate crowding.
3. Deep or open bites.
4. Severe Class II or III, dental or skeletal.
5. Cleft lip and palate.
6. Spaced dentition.
7. Anodontia or oligodontia.
8. Midline diastema.
9. Dilacerations.

**Advantages and disadvantages (p 6435; 7 each, counts derived)**

| Advantages (ref 7) | Disadvantages (refs 7, 8) |
|---|---|
| Psychological trauma avoided | Long-term procedure needing knowledge of growth, eruption sequence and calcification |
| Shorter multibanded treatment | Prolonged treatment with multiple visits (2-3 years) |
| "Physiological" treatment, using physiological forces | Patient cooperation essential |
| Better oral hygiene | Possible tongue thrust |
| Lower cost | Tendency to deepen the bite |
| More stable results | Residual spaces between the canine and the second premolar |
| Shorter retention | Multiple extraction visits |

**Technique and stages (pp 6435-6436; ref 9)**
- Reversible phase: first transitory period; extraction of anterior primary teeth; the permanent incisors align.
- Irreversible phase: second transitory period; extraction of permanent teeth; the posterior crowding is corrected.

**Diagnosis by facial pattern (pp 6436-6437)**

| Pattern | Paper's verdict |
|---|---|
| Class I bimaxillary alveodental protrusion (normal face, dentition forward) | "Responds well" (ref 11) |
| Class II maxillary alveodental protrusion | Serial extraction in the maxilla only |
| Class III (alveodental protrusion heading) | "Not suitable" |
| Class I bimaxillary alveodental retrusion | No extraction; extraction gives a "dished in face" (ref 12) |
| Class II mandibular alveodental retrusion | Not indicated |
| Class I bimaxillary prognathism | "Indicated if" severely crowded, then extraction "usually not indicated" (ref 13) |
| Class II maxillary prognathism | Difficult to treat with serial extraction |
| Class I bimaxillary retrognathism | Extractions contraindicated |
| Class II mandibular retrognathism (backward rotation, open bite) | "Not a good case" (ref 14) |

**Records (p 6437; ref 15)**
- Photographs before, during and after treatment.
- IOPA, lateral ceph and OPG. The OPG shows missing and supernumerary teeth, allows a radiographic and mixed dentition analysis, gives dental age, root development and the likely eruption pattern, and screens for pathology.
- Study models: Carey's analysis in the lower arch, arch perimeter analysis in the upper.

**Three methods (pp 6438-6439)**

| Method | Code, year | Sequence as printed |
|---|---|---|
| Dewel | CD4, 1954 | Primary canines at 8.5 years; primary first molars (Fig 3a: a year later); first premolars as they erupt; the permanent canines erupt. "3 step" (Fig 3a) |
| Tweed | D4C, 1966 | All primary first molars at 8 years; when the first premolar erupts it is extracted **together with** the primary canine. Fig 3b caption: first premolar, **then** primary canines |
| Nance | D4C, no year | All primary first molars at 8 years; first premolars; then primary canines (Fig 3c) |

**Complication (pp 6439-6440)**
- "Anterior crossbites" (ref 16): dentoalveolar, skeletal or functional (pseudo Class III).
- Fig 4 is a slide of crossbite photographs. Its caption describes a flat face, a prominent chin, an aged look and lingual inclination of the incisors.

**Conclusion (p 6440):** an interceptive procedure in the early mixed dentition that relieves crowding and guides the remaining teeth. The fixed-appliance phase is not mentioned.

**Not given in the paper:** whether 8-10 mm is per arch, or how it is measured; any root-development trigger; space maintenance or a lingual arch; enucleation; the fixed phase; any outcome, time or stability data.

**Proffit Ch 12 to keep beside these (Proffit 7th, pp 442-446)**
- A planned sequence of extractions reduces crowding and irregularity during the transition and lets the teeth erupt over the alveolus and through keratinised tissue.
- Serial extraction is an adjunct to later comprehensive treatment, not a substitute. It makes that treatment easier and often quicker, but by itself almost never gives ideal positions or space closure.
- It is best with no skeletal problem and a discrepancy of more than 10 mm per arch; smaller discrepancies leave more residual space.
- A minor to moderate Class II can be treated with upper teeth only, and a Class III with lower teeth only, but this is much more complex and the space closure has to be directional.
- The sequence:
  1. Primary incisors if needed, then the primary canines. Crowding is usually controlled for 1 to 2 years. The lower incisors tip lingually and the overbite often increases; labiolingual displacements resolve better than rotations.
  2. The lower primary first molar at half to two thirds of first-premolar root formation, because the lower canine usually erupts first. The upper first premolar usually erupts before the canine.
  3. The first premolars, before the canines erupt.
- If the premolar still lags, it can impact. Enucleation is possible but best avoided, because early enucleation can leave a bone defect.
- The spaces close partly by mesial drift but mostly by distal eruption of the canines.
- Without appliances, residual space and nonparallel roots usually remain between the canine and the second premolar (Fig 12.37).
- It was overused 25 to 30 years ago and is perhaps underused now; far from a panacea.
- Borderline crowding: keep the options open. Early primary canine extraction helps incisor alignment and lowers the risk of canine impaction, with a lower lingual arch to hold the leeway space.

**Literature to keep beside these**
- O'Shaughnessy 2011, 51 serial-extraction vs 49 late-extraction patients:
  - active time shorter (P < .001); total time and appointments greater (P < .001); final PAR similar (P = .27) (literature, VERIFIED abstract);
  - PAR at the start of fixed appliances 15.18 vs 31.27, at the end 2.61 vs 3.08; serial group 7.95 years at T0 and 13.11 years at the start of fixed appliances, about 5.2 years apart (derived) (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).
- Little, Riedel & Engst 1990: 22 of 30 (73 %) unsatisfactory lower alignment at 10 years or more after retention; no different from a matched late-extraction sample (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Little 1990, Br J Orthod (topic 19, paper 2): alignment usually improved during the drift stage; serial cases no better aligned than late cases after retention; success under 30 % (literature, VERIFIED full text).
- Wilson 1999 (28 / 30 / 30): no soft-tissue profile difference between serial extraction alone, serial extraction plus treatment, and late extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Lopes Filho 2015 systematic review: six retrospective studies; similar crowding correction; less relapse and a shorter active phase with early extraction; evidence insufficient (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Proffit Table 8.4 (Kennedy 1983): slightly less root shortening with serial extraction plus treatment than with late extraction, for example the upper central incisor −1.5 vs −2.0 (Proffit 7th, p 292).

## Part 1. Multiple-choice questions (single best answer)

### Q1 (R). History and names

According to the paper's History section, which statement is correct?

A. Kjellgren coined the term "serial extraction" in 1929, Nance is called its father, and Hotz renamed the technique "guidance of eruption".
B. Dewel coined the term in 1954, and Tweed renamed it "guidance of eruption" in 1966.
C. Bunon coined the term in 1929, and Kjellgren is called the father of serial extraction.
D. Nance coined the term in 1940, and Hotz is called the father of serial extraction.
E. Hotz first explained the procedure in 1743, and Jack Dale renamed it "planned extraction".

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A is p 6433.
- B: the paper gives 1954 as the date of Dewel's **method** (p 6438), not of the term. "Guidance of eruption" is Hotz's name.
- C: Bunon is dated 1743 and described as the first to explain the procedure; the paper does not credit him with the term.
- D swaps the roles.
- E: Jack Dale is listed only as a pioneer.
- Outside the paper: Kjellgren himself dates the name to 1929 (literature, VERIFIED: Kjellgren 1948), although the paper's reference for him (ref 14) is a 1947 item. Hotz's 1970 paper is titled "Guidance of eruption versus serial extraction" (literature, VERIFIED). Nance's "father" title is usually given for the United States (literature, VERIFIED-secondary). The paper gives no references for the 1743, 1929 or 1940 dates.

**Tests:** Recall of the names an MCQ is most likely to swap.

### Q2 (A). Choosing the candidate

Which 9-year-old best fits the paper's indications for serial extraction?

A. Class I, no skeletal disproportion, normal overbite and a good profile, with no spacing in the primary dentition and an arch deficiency of about 10 mm.
B. Class I with 4 mm of crowding and a midline diastema.
C. Class II with severe skeletal mandibular retrognathism, a backward-rotating mandible and 9 mm of crowding.
D. Class I with 10 mm of crowding and congenitally missing lower second premolars.
E. Class I with 10 mm of crowding and an anterior open bite.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A combines the first two indications with absent physiologic spacing (p 6433).
- Each distractor hits a contraindication (p 6435) or the diagnosis section:
  - B: mild crowding (no 2) and midline diastema (no 8);
  - C: severe skeletal Class II (no 4); Class II mandibular retrognathism with backward rotation is "not a good case" (p 6437);
  - D: congenital absence of teeth providing space (no 1);
  - E: open bite (no 3).
- Proffit's version of the ideal case is the same: no skeletal problem and more than 10 mm per arch (Proffit 7th, p 443).

**Tests:** Applying the indication and contraindication lists together.

### Q3 (A). Dewel's method

An 8.5-year-old with a Class I skeletal pattern, a normal overbite and severe crowding in both arches is to have serial extraction by Dewel's method as the paper describes it. What is the sequence?

A. Primary canines now; primary first molars about a year later; first premolars as they erupt; the permanent canines then erupt into the premolar spaces.
B. Primary first molars now; then the first premolars together with the primary canines once the premolars erupt.
C. Primary first molars now; then the first premolars; then the primary canines.
D. Primary canines and primary first molars together now; second premolars when they erupt.
E. Primary first molars now with enucleation of the first premolars; primary canines a year later.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A is p 6438 and the Fig 3a caption ("3 step", primary first molars "a year later").
- B is the Tweed text (p 6438).
- C is the Nance sequence (p 6439) and the Tweed caption.
- D is not in the paper.
- E: the paper does not mention enucleation. Proffit allows it but advises avoiding it, because early enucleation can leave a bone defect (Proffit 7th, p 445).
- Proffit uses the same C-D-4 order but times the lower D by the first premolar root, at half to two thirds formed, not by a fixed interval (Proffit 7th, p 444).

**Tests:** Applying the sequence and the age.

### Q4 (N). Tweed against Nance as printed

Which statement best describes how the paper presents the Tweed and Nance methods?

A. Both begin with extraction of all primary first molars at 8 years and both are coded D4C. The Tweed text extracts the first premolar together with the primary canine, but the Fig 3b caption gives the premolar first and then the primary canine, the same order as Nance.
B. Tweed begins with the primary canines at 8.5 years and Nance with the primary first molars at 8 years.
C. Tweed's method is coded CD4 and dated 1954; Nance's is coded D4C and dated 1966.
D. Both methods extract the first premolars before the primary first molars.
E. Both methods are the "reversible phase", because they begin with anterior primary teeth.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- pp 6438-6439, read text against captions.
- The commoner teaching is Tweed = D, then 4 and C together; Nance = D, 4, then C (literature, VERIFIED-secondary; teaching sources disagree). That fits the Tweed text, not the Fig 3b caption.
- B describes Dewel's start for Tweed.
- C swaps Dewel's code and date (CD4, 1954) and gives Tweed's year (1966) to Nance, for whom no year is printed.
- D is backwards.
- E: the reversible phase is defined as extraction of **anterior** primary teeth (p 6435). Both D-first methods start with molars, so the two-phase description fits Dewel's C-first sequence only (my read).

**Tests:** Analysis: reading the text against the figure captions and spotting that two methods are indistinguishable as printed.

### Q5 (A). The moderate case

A 9-year-old has a Class I skeletal pattern, a good profile, a normal overbite and 5 mm of crowding in the lower arch, with no spacing. The parents ask for serial extraction. Based on the paper, what is the best response?

A. Serial extraction is not indicated: mild to moderate crowding is a contraindication, and the indication needs an arch deficiency of 8-10 mm or more.
B. Serial extraction is indicated, because there is no physiologic spacing.
C. Serial extraction is indicated, because the child is Class I with a normal overbite and a good profile.
D. Serial extraction of the lower arch only, because the crowding is confined to it.
E. Tweed's method, because the D-first sequence suits milder crowding.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- Contraindication 2 (p 6435) and indication 1 (p 6433).
- B and C name real indications (p 6433), but neither outweighs a crowding contraindication; the paper's ideal case has severe crowding.
- D: the paper allows a single-arch sequence only for Class II maxillary alveodental protrusion, upper arch only (p 6436).
- E: the paper ties no method to the severity of crowding.
- Proffit agrees and goes further (Proffit 7th, pp 222 and 446). 5 to 9 mm can go either way; the wise course is to keep the options open and hold the leeway space with a lower lingual arch.

**Tests:** Applying the threshold against the softer indications.

### Q6 (R). Disadvantages as listed

Which of the following does the paper list among the disadvantages of serial extraction?

A. A tendency to deepen the bite, and residual spaces between the canine and the second premolar.
B. Reduction of the overbite leading to an anterior open bite.
C. More root resorption of the incisors than with late extraction.
D. Impaction of the first premolar requiring enucleation.
E. A shorter total treatment time but more cost.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A is p 6435.
- B is the opposite direction.
- C: the paper does not discuss root resorption. Proffit's Table 8.4 (Kennedy 1983) shows slightly **less** root shortening with serial extraction plus treatment than with late extraction, for example the upper central incisor −1.5 vs −2.0 (Proffit 7th, p 292).
- D: Proffit describes premolar impaction needing later surgical removal, and enucleation with the D as a way to avoid it (Proffit 7th, p 445), but the paper lists neither.
- E reverses the paper: it lists prolonged treatment over 2-3 years as a disadvantage and lower cost as an advantage (p 6435).

### Q7 (A). Facial pattern

A 9-year-old has a normal facial pattern, but both dentitions sit forward on their bases, with severe crowding and a Class I molar relationship. Using the paper's diagnostic classification, which statement is correct?

A. This is Class I maxillary-mandibular alveodental protrusion, a pattern the paper says responds well to serial extraction.
B. This is Class I alveodental retrusion; extraction would give a dished-in face.
C. This is Class I bimaxillary retrognathism; extractions are contraindicated.
D. This is Class II mandibular retrognathism; not a good case for serial extraction.
E. This is a Class III pattern; serial extraction is not suitable.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:**
- A is p 6436 (ref 11).
- B: retrusion means the dentition sits back, and the paper advises against extraction there because of a "dished in face" (p 6436, ref 12).
- C (p 6437): retrognathism is about the jaws, not the dentition.
- D: the molar relationship is Class I (p 6437).
- E: the molar relationship is Class I (p 6436).
- On the profile: Wilson 1999 found no soft-tissue profile difference between serial extraction with or without treatment and late extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Stephens 2005, paper 5 of this topic (`2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`), found no long-term profile difference when the incisors were finished in the same position. The dished face is a risk of extracting in the wrong face, not of extraction as such (my read).

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q8 (R). Definition, principles, indications, contraindications and records

(a) Give Proffit's definition of serial extraction as the paper quotes it, and the two principles it rests on. (b) List five indications and five contraindications from the paper. (c) List the diagnostic records the paper requires and what the OPG adds.

**Model answer:**

(a) **Definition and principles (p 6433)**
- Proffit's definition: the timed extraction of primary and ultimately permanent teeth to relieve severe crowding (also Proffit 7th, p 443).
- Two principles (Graber 1971, ref 5): an arch length-tooth material discrepancy, and physiologic tooth movement.

(b) **Indications (pp 6433-6435), any five:**
- severe crowding with an arch deficiency of 8-10 mm or more;
- Class I with no skeletal disproportion, normal overbite and a good profile;
- tooth size-jaw size discrepancy;
- no physiologic spacing;
- lateral incisors erupting lingually or palatally;
- unilateral primary canine loss with a midline shift;
- abnormal primary canine root resorption;
- labial recession of a lower incisor;
- canines erupting mesially over the laterals;
- an abnormal eruption path or sequence.

**Contraindications (p 6435), any five:**
- congenital absence of teeth providing space;
- mild to moderate crowding;
- deep or open bites;
- severe Class II or III;
- cleft lip and palate;
- spaced dentition;
- anodontia or oligodontia;
- midline diastema;
- dilacerations.

(c) **Records (p 6437)**
- Photographs before, during and after treatment, intra- and extraoral.
- Radiographs: IOPA, lateral cephalogram and OPG. The OPG detects missing and supernumerary teeth, allows a radiographic and mixed dentition analysis, gives dental age, root development and the likely eruption pattern, and screens for bony pathology.
- Study models: tooth morphology, arch form and occlusion, with Carey's analysis in the lower arch and arch perimeter analysis in the upper.

**Marking points (6):**
1. The definition, with "timed" and "severe crowding".
2. Both principles.
3. At least five correct indications, including the 8-10 mm threshold and Class I with no skeletal problem.
4. At least five correct contraindications.
5. All three radiographs, and the two model analyses with their arches.
6. At least two OPG uses, including root development.

### Q9 (A). The three sequences and how to time them

(a) Describe the Dewel, Tweed and Nance methods as given in the paper, with ages. (b) Explain the paper's reversible and irreversible phases. (c) Explain how Proffit times the extraction of the lower primary first molar, and why.

**Model answer:**

(a) **Methods (pp 6438-6439)**
- **Dewel (CD4, 1954):**
  1. Primary canines at 8.5 years.
  2. Primary first molars about a year later (Fig 3a).
  3. First premolars as they erupt.
  4. The permanent canines erupt into the spaces.
- **Tweed (D4C, 1966):** all primary first molars at 8 years; once the first premolars erupt, they are extracted together with the primary canines; then the permanent canines erupt. The Fig 3b caption gives the premolar first and then the primary canine.
- **Nance (D4C, no year):** all primary first molars at 8 years; first premolars; then primary canines; then the permanent canines erupt (Fig 3c).

As printed, Tweed's caption and Nance's sequence are identical and share a code. The text version for Tweed (4 and C together) matches the commoner teaching (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).

(b) **Phases (pp 6435-6436; ref 9)**
- Reversible phase: in the first transitory period, extraction of anterior primary teeth lets the permanent incisors align. It is reversible because no permanent tooth has been lost (my read).
- Irreversible phase: in the second transitory period, extraction of permanent teeth corrects the posterior crowding.
- This fits Dewel's C-first sequence. The D-first methods start with molars (my read).

(c) **Proffit's timing (Proffit 7th, pp 444-445)**
- In the lower arch the canine usually erupts before the first premolar, which displaces the canine facially.
- Extracting the lower primary first molar when the first-premolar root is half to two thirds formed speeds the premolar, so it erupts first and can be extracted before the canine erupts.
- If the premolar still lags, it can impact. It can be enucleated with the D, but this is best avoided, because the erupting premolar brings bone and early enucleation can leave a bone defect.
- In the upper arch the first premolar usually erupts before the canine, so the sequence is rarely a problem.
- The timing is radiographic (root formation), not by age. The paper's 8 and 8.5 years are approximations, and it lists knowledge of calcification as a requirement (p 6435) without giving a root stage.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Dewel's sequence with 8.5 years and the one-year interval.
2. Tweed and Nance both D-first at 8 years, with their order.
3. Recognising that the Tweed caption and the Nance sequence coincide as printed.
4. Both phases correctly defined.
5. Proffit's half-to-two-thirds root rule and the lower-canine reason.
6. The enucleation trade-off, or the upper/lower difference.

### Q10 (N). Appraise the advantages the paper claims

The paper lists a shorter multibanded phase, lower cost, more stable results and a shorter retention period as advantages (p 6435). Appraise each claim against the evidence.

**Model answer:**

**The paper's support.** All seven advantages are cited to one source, Dewel 1959 (ref 7), and no data are given. In the same section the paper lists prolonged treatment over 2-3 years and multiple extraction visits as disadvantages (p 6435), without reconciling the two.

**Shorter multibanded phase: supported, with a caveat.**
- O'Shaughnessy 2011, 51 serial-extraction vs 49 late-extraction patients (literature, VERIFIED abstract):
  - the active phase was shorter (P < .001);
  - total time and appointments were greater (P < .001);
  - the final PAR was similar (P = .27).
- Wagner & Berg 2000, the paper's ref 6, found the same (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Selection or drift is built in. The serial group started fixed appliances with a PAR of 15.18 against 31.27 (literature, VERIFIED-secondary), so a shorter active phase is partly a consequence of starting with a milder malocclusion.
- The serial group was 7.95 years at T0 and 13.11 years at the start of fixed appliances, about 5.2 years apart (derived; literature, VERIFIED-secondary), much longer than the paper's "2-3 years".

**Lower cost: unsupported.** No cost data are given. Total chair time did not differ in O'Shaughnessy (P = .07; literature, VERIFIED-secondary), and the number of visits was higher.

**More stable, shorter retention: contradicted.**
- Little, Riedel & Engst 1990 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): 22 of 30 serial-extraction cases (73 %) had unsatisfactory lower alignment at least 10 years out of retention. Arch length and intercanine width fell in 29 of 30. There was no difference from matched late-extraction cases.
- Little 1990, Br J Orthod (topic 19, paper 2; literature, VERIFIED full text): success was under 30 %, and serial cases were no better aligned than late ones.
- Lopes Filho 2015 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): less relapse with early extraction, but from six retrospective studies of low to moderate quality, and insufficient evidence overall.

**What does hold.** Serial extraction alone does reduce crowding:
- Yoshihara 1999 and 2000 (literature, VERIFIED abstracts): irregularity fell, and canine movement and tipping helped correct the anterior crowding.
- Persson 1989 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): marked spontaneous alignment and residual space closure into adult life in selected Class I cases.

Proffit's summary fits this (Proffit 7th, pp 443 and 445): an adjunct that makes comprehensive treatment easier and often quicker, not a substitute, and far from a panacea.

**Marking points (6):**
1. No data or primary source behind the advantages, and the internal tension with the 2-3 year disadvantage.
2. Active time shorter but total time and visits greater, with a source.
3. The PAR selection point, or total time longer than "2-3 years".
4. Cost unsupported.
5. Stability contradicted by Little, with numbers.
6. A balanced conclusion: an adjunct, not a substitute.

### Q11 (N). Appraise the paper as evidence

Appraise this review: design and level of evidence, sources, internal consistency, omissions, and how a resident should use it.

**Model answer:**

**Design.** A narrative review with no methods section, no search strategy, no eligibility criteria, no appraisal of sources and no synthesis method. Oxford CEBM Level 5. The abstract is definitional and reports no findings.

**Sources (pp 6440-6441; counts derived)**
- 16 references, 7 of them textbooks.
- Newest book 2008; newest journal item 2004.
- 10 of the 15 dated references come before 1990 (median year 1982), and ref 12 (Proffit, 2nd ed) has no year.
- The outcome literature is absent: Little 1990, Wilson 1999, O'Shaughnessy 2011 and Lopes Filho 2015.
- The journal is a multidisciplinary title whose Scopus coverage was discontinued after 2020 (literature, VERIFIED-secondary).

**Citation accuracy**
- The facial analysis is attributed to "Grabber (1971)" but cited to ref 10, Moorrees 1963 (p 6436). Graber 1971 is ref 5.
- Wagner & Berg 2000 (ref 6), a comparison of treatment duration and outcome, is cited for the indication and contraindication lists (p 6435).
- Kjellgren 1947 (ref 14) is cited for Class II mandibular retrognathism (p 6437).
- The dates for Bunon, Kjellgren 1929 and Nance 1940 have no references (p 6433).

**Internal consistency (phrase neutrally)**
1. Tweed and Nance share the code D4C. The Tweed text and its caption give different orders, and the caption matches Nance (pp 6438-6439).
2. The reversible phase (anterior primary teeth) fits only Dewel's C-first method (pp 6435, 6438).
3. Class I with no skeletal disproportion is the indication (p 6433), yet a Class II upper-only sequence is accepted (p 6436). Class I bimaxillary prognathism is "indicated if" severely crowded, followed by "usually not indicated" (p 6436).
4. Shorter multibanded treatment, but 2-3 years of visits (p 6435).
5. The complication section is headed anterior crossbite. Fig 4 shows crossbite photographs, but its caption describes a flat face with lingual incisor inclination (pp 6439-6440).
6. The introduction promises "adjuncts" that never appear (p 6432).

**Omissions against Proffit (Proffit 7th, pp 443-446)**
- The fixed-appliance phase.
- Timing by root formation.
- The lower canine erupting before the premolar.
- Enucleation and its bone defect.
- The lower lingual arch in borderline cases.
- Lower-only sequences in Class III.
- The lower risk of canine impaction after early primary canine extraction.

**Strengths.** A clear, teachable structure. Proffit's definition is quoted accurately. The deeper bite and the canine-to-second-premolar residual space are correctly named as sequelae. The OPG uses are well listed.

**Use.** As an exam vocabulary sheet, cross-checked against Proffit Ch 12; not as evidence for any outcome claim.

**Marking points (6):**
1. Design and Level 5.
2. The reference profile (old, textbook-heavy, outcome literature missing).
3. At least one citation-content mismatch.
4. At least two internal inconsistencies, neutrally phrased.
5. At least two omissions against Proffit.
6. A balanced statement of use.

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q12 (A). "The crowded 8-year-old"

**Case.** An 8½-year-old girl from a town two hours from the clinic. Class I skeletal pattern, straight profile with competent lips, normal overbite. The permanent incisors are erupted. The lower laterals came in lingually, and the lower right primary canine was lost early, with the lower midline 1.5 mm to the right. A mixed dentition analysis shows about 11 mm of crowding in the lower arch and 10 mm in the upper. On the OPG all premolars and canines are present, and the lower first-premolar roots are about one third formed.

**Examiner question 1. Is she a candidate for serial extraction?**

Model answer:
- Yes, on the paper's criteria:
  - Class I with no skeletal disproportion, a normal overbite and a good profile (p 6433);
  - severe crowding above 8-10 mm;
  - lateral incisors erupting lingually, and unilateral primary canine loss with a midline shift (pp 6433-6434).
- None of the contraindications applies: no missing teeth, no deep or open bite, no severe Class II or III, no spacing (p 6435).
- On Proffit's criterion, more than 10 mm per arch with no skeletal problem (Proffit 7th, p 443), the upper arch is borderline at 10 mm and the lower clearly qualifies.
- The profile matters too. With a straight profile and competent lips, the paper's warning about a dished-in face applies only to alveodental retrusion (p 6436). Proffit's guideline for 10 mm or more is extraction almost always, with little or no effect on lip support (Proffit 7th, p 222).

**Examiner question 2. What records do you need?**

Model answer:
- Photographs before, during and after treatment.
- An OPG for missing or supernumerary teeth, root development and dental age.
- A lateral ceph for the skeletal pattern and incisor position.
- Periapicals where needed.
- Study models: Carey's analysis for the lower arch and arch perimeter for the upper (p 6437).
- I would repeat the OPG during the programme, because root formation, not age, times the next step (Proffit 7th, p 444).

**Examiner question 3. Give me the sequence and the timing.**

Model answer:
1. Dewel's C-D-4 (p 6438), as Proffit describes it (Proffit 7th, pp 444-445). Extract the primary canines now, so that the incisors align. Crowding is usually controlled for 1 to 2 years.
2. Extract the lower primary first molars when the first-premolar root is half to two thirds formed. Hers is about one third, so not yet; I would review her with a radiograph rather than wait a fixed year.
3. Extract the first premolars as they erupt, before the canines.
4. Let the canines erupt distally into the spaces.

**Examiner question 4. At review the lower canine is clearly ahead of the first premolar. What now?**

Model answer:
- This is the lower-arch problem Proffit warns about: the canine erupts first and is displaced facially, and the premolar can impact (Proffit 7th, pp 444-445).
- Options:
  - extract the D now to speed the premolar;
  - enucleate the premolar with the D if it is clearly going to be late.
- Enucleation should be avoided where possible, because the erupting premolar brings alveolar bone and early enucleation can leave a lasting defect.
- The paper does not discuss this. A D-first sequence (Tweed or Nance, pp 6438-6439) starts with the molars, which would address it (my read).

**Examiner question 5. The family asks how long this takes and how many visits.**

Model answer:
- The paper says 2-3 years with multiple visits (p 6435). The evidence suggests longer.
- In O'Shaughnessy 2011:
  - total time and appointments were greater with serial extraction (P < .001), though the fixed phase was shorter (P < .001) (literature, VERIFIED abstract);
  - the serial group was about 8 years old at T0 and 13 at the start of fixed appliances, about 5.2 years apart (derived; literature, VERIFIED-secondary).
- For a family two hours away, I would offer late premolar extraction as an equal alternative. The final occlusion is similar (P = .27). This is the 2023 and 2024 promotion question (PE2).

**Examiner question 6. After the premolars are out, her lower incisors have tipped lingually, the overbite has deepened and there are spaces distal to the canines. Has it failed?**

Model answer:
- No. This is the expected course.
- The paper lists bite deepening and residual canine-to-second-premolar spaces as disadvantages (p 6435). Proffit describes lingual tipping of the lower incisors and an increased overbite during the drift stage (Proffit 7th, p 444). Without appliances, residual space and nonparallel roots usually remain between the canine and second premolar (Fig 12.37, p 446).
- Serial extraction is an adjunct (Proffit 7th, p 443). The plan always included a fixed-appliance phase to close space, level the bite and parallel the roots.

**Examiner question 7. Her parents were told this gives a more stable result with less retention. Is that true?**

Model answer:
- Not on the evidence. The paper states it without data (p 6435).
- Little followed 30 serial-extraction cases at least 10 years out of retention (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Twenty-two (73 %) had unsatisfactory lower alignment, and there was no difference from late-extraction cases.
- Lopes Filho 2015 found less relapse with early extraction, but from six retrospective, low to moderate quality studies (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- She needs the same retention as any extraction case.

**Examiner question 8. Will extraction flatten her face?**

Model answer:
- Unlikely, with this profile. The paper's dished-in face warning is for Class I alveodental retrusion (p 6436).
- Wilson 1999 found no soft-tissue profile difference between serial extraction, with or without treatment, and late extraction (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- Stephens 2005 (`2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`) found no long-term profile difference when the incisors were finished in the same place. It also showed the lips falling behind the nose and chin with growth in both groups.
- The deciding factors are the lips and the final incisor position (Proffit notes, Ch 7; past exam, Ch 7, keyed "chin and lip").

**Examiner question 9. What does this paper not tell you?**

Model answer:
- Whether 8-10 mm is per arch, or how it is measured.
- Any root-development trigger.
- The lower-canine problem and enucleation.
- Space maintenance and the lingual arch in borderline cases.
- The fixed-appliance phase.
- Any data on time, cost, stability or the face.
- That the Tweed and Nance sequences, as printed, cannot be told apart.
- That early primary canine extraction lowers the risk of canine impaction (Proffit 7th, p 446). That point links to Kokich 2004, paper 1 of this topic (`2004-kokich-impacted-maxillary-canines`).
