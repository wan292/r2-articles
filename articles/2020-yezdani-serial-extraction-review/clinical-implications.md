# Clinical implications — Yezdani 2020

Yezdani A, Nandhini P, Padmavati R. Serial extraction in orthodontics – review article. Eur J Mol Clin Med 2020;7(2):6432-6441. This is a 10-page narrative review in a low-tier multidisciplinary journal. It has no methods, no sample and no data: it lists definitions, indications, contraindications, advantages, a facial-pattern classification and three textbook extraction sequences (Dewel, Tweed, Nance), supported by 16 references of which 10 of the 15 dated ones predate 1990 (derived). Every clinical message below is therefore expert opinion (Level 5). Its best use is as an exam vocabulary sheet, read beside Proffit Ch 12 (pp 442-446), which is where the clinical rules below are checked.

On this page, "the paper says" means printed in Yezdani 2020, with the page. "Proffit" means the 7th-edition PDF on disk (book pages), VERIFIED this run; the resident's 6th-edition notes agree on the Ch 12 points unless marked. "The literature says" means another source checked in this run's research pass, marked VERIFIED (abstract or primary source read), VERIFIED-secondary (full-text copy or a colleague's summary) or UNVERIFIED. "My read" is my interpretation.

## What this changes or confirms at the chair

1. **Serial extraction is for severe crowding only, and the threshold is about 10 mm per arch.**
   - The paper says: indicated for severe crowding with an arch deficiency of 8-10 mm or more; contraindicated in mild to moderate crowding (pp 6433, 6435).
   - Proffit: best with no skeletal problem and a discrepancy greater than 10 mm per arch; with less, more residual space must be expected (p 443). In his general extraction guidelines for Class I crowding, 5-9 mm is borderline and 10 mm or more almost always means extraction (Ch 7, p 222).
   - Caveat: the paper does not say whether its 8-10 mm is per arch or how it is measured; it only names Carey's analysis (lower) and arch perimeter analysis (upper) (p 6437).
   - My read: use 10 mm per arch, measured on a mixed-dentition space analysis, as the answer in an exam and as the bar in clinic. Promotion recall 2021: 9-year-old with 10 mm crowding → serial extraction.

2. **Select the face first: Class I, normal overbite, good profile, incisors not retrusive.**
   - The paper says: ideal in Class I with no skeletal disproportion and a good profile (p 6433). Bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion responds well; dentoalveolar retrusion should be treated without extraction because extraction dishes in the face (p 6436). Deep bites, open bites and severe Class II or III are contraindications (p 6435).
   - The literature says: in Wilson 1999 (serial extraction alone n = 28, with treatment n = 30, late extraction n = 30) the soft-tissue profiles did not differ (VERIFIED). In the hub, Stephens 2005 (`2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`) found no long-term profile difference once incisor position was matched at debond.
   - Proffit: lips follow the incisors (Ch 7, VERIFIED notes), so a face that is already flat is the one at risk.
   - My read: "dished-in face" is a real risk only when the incisors and lips are already retrusive. In a Class I face with normal or protrusive incisors, the profile evidence does not argue against serial extraction.

3. **Class II and Class III: possible only in one arch, and not a general-practice procedure.**
   - The paper says: Class II maxillary dentoalveolar protrusion can be treated with serial extraction in the maxilla only; Class III is "not suitable"; Class II mandibular retrognathism and maxillary prognathism are poor or difficult cases (pp 6436-6437).
   - Proffit: mild to moderate Class II (upper teeth only) or Class III (lower teeth only) can be compensated this way, but it is much more complex, needs directional space closure and better biomechanics than most nonspecialists have (pp 443-444).
   - My read: for the exam, "Class I with severe crowding" is the classic indication. Proffit allows a lower-only sequence in mild Class III; the paper does not.

4. **Early warning signs tell you to do a space analysis, not to start extracting.**
   - The paper says: indications include no physiologic spacing (Fig 2a), lateral incisors erupting lingually (Fig 2b), unilateral primary canine loss with a midline shift (Fig 2c), abnormal or asymmetric primary canine root resorption, labial recession of a lower incisor (Fig 2d) and canines erupting mesially over the lateral incisors (pp 6433-6435).
   - Proffit agrees that little spacing in the primary dentition, severe incisor irregularity and early loss of primary canines are the signs of severe crowding (p 439).
   - Proffit adds two cautions:
     - Lower incisors erupting lingual to retained primary incisors is common, and removing the retained primary teeth allows some spontaneous alignment (Fig 11.37, p 396).
     - After one lower primary canine is lost, the midline shift is usually small. Growth-study data put it at 1.3 mm against 1.0 mm with normal eruption, so extracting the other primary canine is no longer considered necessary; a passive lingual arch is optional (pp 396-397, 7th ed; the 6th-ed wording is UNVERIFIED).
   - My read: these signs earn a space analysis, a profile assessment and an OPG, then the decision.

5. **Use Dewel's C-D-4 order, and time the D by premolar root formation, not by age.**
   - The paper says: Dewel extracts the deciduous canines at 8.5 years, the deciduous first molars a year later (about 9.5 y, derived) and the first premolars as they erupt (p 6438, Fig 3a).
   - Proffit: primary incisors if needed, then primary canines (crowding stays under control for 1-2 years), then the lower primary first molar when **½ to ⅔ of the first-premolar root** has formed, so that the 4 erupts before the 3; then the 4s (p 444, Fig 12.36).
   - Upper arch: the first premolar usually erupts before the canine, so the sequence rarely fails there. Lower arch: the canine usually comes first and is displaced facially unless the D is taken in time (p 444).
   - My read: an oral examiner who asks "when do you take the D?" wants the root-formation answer.

6. **The D-first (Tweed and Nance) sequences, and the premolar that will not come.**
   - The paper says: Tweed and Nance both extract all deciduous first molars at 8 years, then the first premolars and the deciduous canines (pp 6438-6439). As printed, the two are hard to tell apart: Tweed's text extracts the 4 with the C, its caption extracts the 4 then the C, and both are coded D4C (see `conflicts.md`). Teaching sources usually give Tweed as D, then 4 and C together (VERIFIED-secondary).
   - Proffit: if the D goes early and the 4 still lags behind the canine, the premolar can impact. Removing the 4 with the D (enucleation) is possible but should be avoided, because the erupting premolar brings bone with it and an early enucleation can leave a lasting defect (p 445).
   - The paper does not mention premolar impaction or enucleation.

7. **Tell the family: fewer months in braces, but more years and more visits overall.**
   - The paper says both: shorter multibanded treatment and lower cost (advantages), and prolonged treatment of 2-3 years with multiple visits and multiple extraction appointments (disadvantages) (p 6435). It gives no data for either.
   - The literature says: in O'Shaughnessy 2011 (51 serial vs 49 late extraction), active treatment was shorter with serial extraction, but total time and appointments were greater (all P < .001), and the final PAR did not differ significantly (P = .27) (VERIFIED abstract). The serial group was observed for about 5.2 years before fixed appliances (derived from mean ages 7.95 and 13.11 y; VERIFIED-secondary), not 2-3. Wagner & Berg 2000, the paper's own ref 6, reached the same conclusion (VERIFIED abstract).
   - Caveat: the serial group started fixed appliances with half the PAR score (15.18 vs 31.27, VERIFIED-secondary), so the shorter active phase is partly built in.
   - Promotion recalls 2023 (shorter active phase) and 2024 (longer total time, more visits) both test this.

8. **Plan the fixed-appliance phase from the start; serial extraction is an adjunct.**
   - The paper says: the overbite tends to deepen and residual spaces can remain between the canine and second premolar (p 6435); it also claims "more stable results" and a shorter retention period, without data (p 6435).
   - Proffit: now an adjunct to comprehensive treatment, not a substitute (p 443). Without appliances, the lower incisors tip lingually and the overbite increases (p 444); ideal alignment, root parallelism, correct overbite and space closure are usually not achieved (p 445). Residual space and nonparallel roots between canine and second premolar are typical (Fig 12.37, p 446).
   - The literature says: Little 1990 found 22 of 30 (73 %) serial-extraction cases with unsatisfactory lower anterior alignment at least 10 years postretention, no different from late extraction (VERIFIED). Persson 1989 found marked spontaneous alignment and space closure without appliances in selected Class I cases (first premolars out at a mean 10½ years, re-examined at a mean age of 30 years 4 months) (VERIFIED).
   - My read: retain a serial-extraction case like any other extraction case. Do not promise better stability.

9. **Get the OPG before the first extraction: missing premolars change everything.**
   - The paper says: congenital absence of teeth, anodontia and oligodontia are contraindications; the OPG detects missing and supernumerary teeth, dental age and root development (pp 6435, 6437).
   - Proffit (notes): good premolars seldom form after age 8 (Ch 12, VERIFIED notes).
   - My read: a missing lower 5 turns a serial-extraction plan into a different problem; Kokich & Kokich 2006 (topic 5, paper 2) covers those options.

10. **Early primary canine extraction also helps an ectopic maxillary canine.**
    - The paper does not mention it.
    - Proffit: early extraction of primary canines aids incisor alignment and lowers the chance of canine impaction, but a lower lingual arch is needed if the nonextraction option is to stay open (p 446; also Ch 11, p 396).
    - In the hub, Kokich 2004 (`2004-kokich-impacted-maxillary-canines`) uses the same extraction to let a palatally displaced canine self-correct. Dewel's first step therefore does two jobs.

11. **Check for an anterior crossbite before you start.**
    - The paper says: anterior crossbite (dentoalveolar, skeletal or functional) is the only complication listed; its Fig 4 caption describes a flat face, prominent chin and lingually inclined incisors (pp 6439-6440).
    - Caveat: the figure shows crossbite photographs, not documented results of serial extraction, and no mechanism is given. Proffit lists lingual tipping and deeper overbite as the usual side effects (p 444).
    - My read: treat a functional crossbite or pseudo Class III first, and do not extract in a child whose lower incisors are already retroclined.

## Practical protocol notes (as the paper describes them, with Proffit's additions)

**Diagnosis**
1. Examination and consultation (p 6437).
2. Records: pre-, mid- and post-treatment photographs; periapicals, lateral ceph and OPG; study models with Carey's analysis (lower) and arch perimeter analysis (upper) (p 6437).
3. Classify the face into one of five patterns: standard, alveodental protrusion, alveodental retrusion, prognathism, retrognathism (pp 6436-6437).
4. Proffit addition: confirm the discrepancy is more than 10 mm per arch and there is no skeletal problem (p 443).

**Sequence (Dewel, CD4; p 6438)**
1. Extract the deciduous canines at about 8.5 years. Proffit: primary incisors first if needed (p 444).
2. Extract the deciduous first molars about a year later. Proffit: lower D when ½ to ⅔ of the first-premolar root has formed (p 444).
3. Extract the first premolars as they erupt.
4. The permanent canines erupt and drift distally into the premolar spaces. Proffit: the spaces close mainly by distal canine eruption, partly by mesial drift of the 5s and 6s (p 445).
5. Proffit addition: fixed appliances to finish (root parallelism, overbite, residual space) (p 445).

**Alternative sequences (pp 6438-6439)**
- Tweed (D4C): deciduous first molars at 8 years; then the first premolars with the deciduous canines once the premolars erupt.
- Nance (D4C): deciduous first molars at 8 years; then the first premolars; then the deciduous canines.
- Both aim to bring the 4 in before the 3. Proffit warns that the 4 can still impact (p 445).

## What the paper does NOT tell you

- **Any data of its own.** There is no sample, no outcome and no statistic; the advantages "more stable", "lower cost" and "shorter retention" have no source data.
- **How the 8-10 mm is measured,** whether it is per arch, and what to do between 4 and 8 mm.
- **Timing by dental development.** Only chronological ages are given (8 and 8.5 years); there is no root-formation trigger, although calcification knowledge is listed as needed (p 6435).
- **What to do when the lower canine leads,** including premolar impaction and enucleation (Proffit pp 444-445).
- **Space maintenance.** No lingual arch, no leeway-space management, no borderline-case strategy.
- **The fixed-appliance phase and retention,** which Proffit treats as essential (pp 443-445).
- **The comparative evidence:** stability (Little 1990), total and active time (O'Shaughnessy 2011, Wagner & Berg 2000), profile (Wilson 1999), root resorption (Kennedy 1983 in Proffit Table 8.4, p 292: slightly less shortening with serial extraction, e.g. upper central −1.5 vs −2.0), and the 2015 systematic review (Lopes Filho).
- **The canine-impaction benefit** of early primary canine extraction (Proffit p 446).
- **The vertical effect** of serial extraction on the mandibular plane (Quinzi 2023; Kouvelis 2018 for premolar extraction in general).
- **The "various adjuncts"** promised in the introduction (p 6432).
- **Who did what in the figures:** no figure carries a source credit, and Fig 4 shows crossbites rather than documented serial-extraction outcomes.

## One sentence for a patient or a junior

- **To a junior:** "Serial extraction is for a Class I child with good lips and more than about 10 mm of crowding per arch: canines out first, the lower D when half to two-thirds of the premolar root has formed, the 4s as they erupt, then fixed appliances to finish, because on its own it leaves deep bites and tipped roots."
- **To a parent:** "Taking a few baby teeth and then four adult teeth at the right times lets the front teeth straighten as they come in, so the braces later are shorter, but it means more visits over several years and braces are still needed at the end."
