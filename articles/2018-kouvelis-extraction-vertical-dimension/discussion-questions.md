# Questions to ask about this paper

Kouvelis G, Dritsas K, Doulis I, Kloukos D, Gkantidis N. Effect of orthodontic treatment with 4 premolar extractions compared with nonextraction treatment on the vertical dimension of the face: a systematic review. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop 2018;154(2):175-87. doi:10.1016/j.ajodo.2018.03.007. Authors from the 251 Hellenic Air Force Hospital (Athens), the University of Bern and Aristotle University of Thessaloniki; submitted January 2018, revised and accepted March 2018. A systematic review of 14 retrospective studies with 857 patients (derived from Table I: 430 extraction, 427 nonextraction). Risk of bias was rated with ROBINS-I and the synthesis was narrative only. This is paper 4 of topic 5 (Treatment Rationale).

The headline is easy: four-premolar extraction does not change the skeletal vertical dimension. A good question therefore shows that you know what that "no" is built on:
- 14 retrospective cohorts, counted rather than pooled;
- outcomes that are mandibular-plane angles and face heights, with no molar measurement extracted;
- four significant results that split two for extraction and two against.

The text layer of this PDF prints "+" as "1" and "±" as "6" and drops minus signs, so every value below was read from the page images. Material from outside the paper is labelled (literature), (Proffit), (registry) or (topic N, the year's reading list), and marked VERIFIED if it was seen in a source this run, otherwise UNVERIFIED. Proffit items come from the user's study pages (wan292/proffit-study-pages), VERIFIED in those notes, unless marked as seen in the 7th-edition text this run. Arithmetic done for this file is labelled derived; the script is `checks.py` in this folder. The past-year exam questions that this paper explains are at the top of `exam-questions.md`; Q14 and the fire-back questions below use them.

How to ask. The session is a casual discussion and mixing Arabic with English is normal, so ask the way you talk. Name the table first and keep the question to one breath. For example: "بالـ Table III، الـ four significant results طلعوا two و two: Porto و Kumari لصالح الـ extraction، و Sivakumar و Paquette بالعكس. فهل الأصح نقول no consistent direction بدل no effect؟" Frame any wording or table issue as "typo, or a different measure?", never as an error. The senior author, Gkantidis, is first author of an included study (ref 8), and that study supplies the Discussion's explanation for the missing wedge effect. Several people in the room will have extracted in a high-angle case "for the vertical". Let the tables make the point, not you.

## Pocket numbers

**Search and selection (Methods p 176; flow chart p 177; Appendix p 187)**
- MEDLINE (Ovid and PubMed, 1946 to 2 August 2017), EMBASE, the Cochrane Oral Health Group's Trials Register and CENTRAL. Grey literature: ClinicalTrials.gov, the National Research Register and ProQuest dissertations. Reference lists hand-searched. No language or date limits. Protocol not registered.
- Flow chart:
  - 927 database records, cut to 231 after duplicates were removed.
  - 29 records from other sources (the same box says "19 studies identified after hand searching").
  - 260 screened; 226 excluded (183 at title, 43 at abstract); 34 full texts.
  - 20 full texts excluded: 4 had no vertical outcome, 2 soft tissue only, 3 not retrievable, 4 had no angles or lines, 6 had no treated nonextraction control, 1 had no posttreatment data.
  - 14 included; 0 in a meta-analysis.
- Eligibility (p 176): any design with a treated comparison group; full fixed appliances in both arches; four premolars (one per quadrant) against fixed treatment without extraction; skeletal vertical outcomes measured on radiographs before and after treatment. Excluded: animal and in-vitro studies, orthognathic surgery, case reports, and studies with fewer than 10 patients.

**Risk of bias (ROBINS-I; Table II, p 182; Results p 178)**
- 5 moderate (Gkantidis, Hayasaki, Kirschneck, Kocadereli, Porto).
- 5 serious (Kumari, Paquette, Hosseinzadeh-Nik, Bravo, Katsaros).
- 3 critical (Luppanapornlap, Sivakumar, Staggers).
- Basciftci was rated serious for its Class I group and critical for its Class II group.
- No study could be rated low on confounding "by definition of the tool". The outcome assessor was not blinded in 13 of the 14 studies (derived: only Kirschneck is rated low on outcome measurement).

**Other facts worth having ready**
- Extraction pattern (p 178): four first premolars in 11 studies. Bravo removed upper first premolars with lower first or second premolars. Basciftci and Katsaros did not say which premolars.
- Mean baseline angles never exceeded 40.0° SN-GoGn (Porto extraction group, Table I). There was no open-bite sample.
- Discussion (pp 181 and 184): apart from Paquette's 2° FMA decrease in the nonextraction group, no mean angular change in any group exceeded 1.2°.
- Wedge effect (p 184): mesial molar movement during space closure is said to rotate the mandible forward (ref 25, Fields, Proffit et al 1984). The authors' explanation for its absence, after Gkantidis (ref 8): molars move parallel to the occlusal plane, not along the maxillary and mandibular planes.
- Derived (`checks.py`):
  - An exploratory fixed-effect pool of the 12 mandibular-plane comparisons that have SDs gives Ex − Nonex −0.32° (95 % CI −0.68 to 0.04), I² = 0 %. It mixes SN-GoGn, FMA, SN-GoMe and MP-HP; the review itself pooled nothing.
  - Minimum detectable difference at 80 % power with an SD of 2.5°: 2.56° with 15 per group, 1.81° with 30 and 1.57° with 40.
  - The extraction group had the higher baseline angle in 10 of the 13 comparisons that give both values.
  - Extraction treatment lasted longer in 7 of the 8 studies that report duration by group (9 of 10 comparisons); Sivakumar is the exception (1.5 vs 2.9 y).
  - Magnification correction is "NA" in 8 of 14 studies (Table I); this affects linear measures only.

## Tier 1. Safe and sharp

### Q1. "The abstract lists four significant results. Kumari, N-Me +1.5 against +5.5 mm, and Porto, SN-GoGn −0.9 against +0.8°, favour extraction. Sivakumar, N-Me +2.3 against +0.9 mm, and Paquette, FMA +0.3 against −2.0°, go the other way. In each of the four, the second vertical measure did not agree. Is the fair one-liner 'no consistent direction' rather than 'no effect'?"
- **Why it lands:** It shows you read Table III, not just the conclusion.
  - Ten of the 14 studies found no difference (abstract).
  - The disagreeing second measures: Porto FMA −0.3 vs +0.7° (P .240); Kumari FMA −0.2 vs +0.6° and the N-ANS/ANS-Me ratio, both P > .050; Sivakumar FMA (printed P .985) and ANS-Me +1.3 vs +0.6 mm (P .080); Paquette N-Me and ANS-Me, both P > .050 (Table III).
  - The Results call the Sivakumar and Paquette differences "quite small and clinically questionable" and Porto's "clinically questionable" (p 178). Only Kumari's 4 mm is called clinically significant (Discussion, p 185).
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement. 1.4 to 4.0 mm and 1.7 to 2.3° are small, the directions cancel, and two different measures in the same patients never agreed. "No consistent effect" is the accurate wording.
- **Follow-up:** "The Discussion says refs 17 and 18 concluded that extraction affected the vertical but that their own data did not support it (p 181). Sivakumar's own abstract actually concludes that extracting only to reduce the mandibular plane angle might not be justified, the same message as this review (literature, VERIFIED abstract). So is the p 181 sentence really about Porto, and is the point that FMA disagreed, or that 1.7° is too small to matter?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did any study find a difference?" The abstract lists all four.

### Q2. "The only mean angular change above 1.2° in any group was Paquette's nonextraction FMA, −2.0° against +0.3° with extraction (P < .010). That is the opposite of the wedge prediction. It is the same Paquette sample that Stephens contrasts with in paper 5. What closed the nonextraction mandibular plane: growth, mechanics, or the baseline?"
- **Why it lands:** It links two papers in this topic and reads Table I and Table III together.
  - Paquette's groups were Class II division 1, normodivergent, about 12.5 years old at the start, treated 1.8 and 1.6 years (Table I). Ref 20's title calls them "borderline" Class II patients.
  - Paquette is one of only three comparisons where the nonextraction group started with the higher angle: 24.9 against 22.9° (Table I; derived count).
  - Treatment details are "NA", and "deviations from intended interventions" is rated "No information" (Table II).
  - The size is printed three ways: 1.7° in the Results (p 178), 2.3° in the Table III effect column, and "2°" in the Discussion (p 181), which is the within-group change.
  - Stephens 2005 (registry, `2005-stephens-long-term-profile-extraction-nonextraction`) cites Paquette 1992 for the profile result in borderline Class II patients.
- **What you will probably hear:** Forward mandibular growth in a normodivergent Class II sample, plus regression from a slightly higher start. Nobody can say more without the mechanics.
- **Follow-up:** "Is the 1.7° in the Results a typo carried over from Porto, which is also 1.7°? Which number should we quote: the 2.3° between groups?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Paquette is the profile study, right?" It is, but here it is a vertical study, and the question wastes the point.

### Q3. "Gkantidis 2011 stacked the mechanics against the theory. The extraction group had non-extrusive mechanics: Nance and Goshgarian arches, no Class II elastics, no low-pull headgear, no bite plates. The nonextraction group had low-pull headgear in every patient, anterior bite planes and Class II elastics. Both started hyperdivergent, SN-GoGn 38.4 and 39.1°. SN-GoGn changed −0.1 against +0.1° (P .616). Is this the most convincing single study in the review?"
- **Why it lands:** It picks the design that was built to show a difference and found none.
  - Table I gives the mechanics and baselines. Table II rates Gkantidis moderate, with low risk in 5 of 7 domains.
  - FH-MP −0.2 vs +0.1° (P .709); ANS-Me +4.0 vs +3.3 mm (P .643); LFH/TFH +0.1 vs +0.2 (P .822) (Table III).
  - The Results describe it as testing "opposite treatment approaches" expected to increase the effect (p 178).
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, with two caveats.
  - Growth: these were 11- to 12-year-olds (11.8 ± 0.9 and 11.0 ± 1.0 y), treated 3.2 and 2.4 years. Ramus growth can absorb molar extrusion. Proffit (Ch 16, VERIFIED in the notes): more than 2 mm of molar distalisation with headgear comes with extrusion, acceptable only with ramus growth.
  - Operator: Gkantidis's own abstract says the two groups came from two offices that used contrasting protocols (literature, VERIFIED abstract), so practitioner and protocol travel together.
- **Follow-up:** "Would the same contrast hold in a nongrowing 20-year-old? Three studies were mainly nongrowing (refs 15, 18 and 21, p 178), and none reported growing and nongrowing patients separately."
- **Avoid saying:** "Isn't that the last author's own study?" See "Do not ask these".

### Q4. "Who is actually in this review? The highest mean baseline is Porto's 40.0° SN-GoGn. Kocadereli and Kumari are only a 'hyperdivergent tendency'. There is no open-bite sample, and molar-extraction studies were excluded. Does 'regardless of vertical growth pattern' cover the patient with SN-MP 45° and an open bite, where people still extract for the vertical?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns the conclusion into a question about external validity.
  - Table I baselines: Basciftci Class II 39.3°, Gkantidis 38.4°, Katsaros SN-GoMe 38.6°, Kumari FMA 27.8°.
  - Molar extractions (refs 7, 19, 26) and two-lower-premolar extractions (ref 27) were left out because they had no treated nonextraction control (p 184).
  - The Discussion's closing sentence: "regardless of age, sex, malocclusion type, or vertical growth pattern" (p 185).
- **What you will probably hear:** The conclusion is for mild-to-moderate high angles in adolescents. The severe open bite is a different question. Aras 2002 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): in skeletal open bite, second-premolar or first-molar extraction (n = 17) gave a closing rotation when the open bite extended posteriorly; first-premolar extraction (n = 15) gave no change.
- **Follow-up:** "Proffit's extraction guidelines say that with less than 4 mm of crowding, extraction is rarely indicated except for severe incisor protrusion or a severe vertical discrepancy (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes). Is his 'vertical discrepancy' about the skeleton, or about closing an open bite dentally while the incisors are retracted?" (The second reading is my interpretation, UNVERIFIED.)
- **Avoid saying:** "Does extraction close an open bite?" The review did not measure overbite.

## Tier 2. Mechanics and biology

### Q5. "The wedge theory is about the molars: they move forward, and the mandible closes. But none of the outcomes in Table III is a molar measurement. They are mandibular-plane angles, face heights such as N-Me and ANS-Me, two ratios, and B point and pogonion to SN7. Yet at least two of the primary studies did measure the molars. Why were those data not extracted?"
- **Why it lands:** It separates the finding from the mechanism the authors offer for it, and shows you looked one step past the review.
  - The eligibility outcome was skeletal vertical change on radiographs (p 176), and the Table I outcome column lists no molar measure.
  - Kumari and Fida's abstract lists upper molar to palatal plane and lower molar to mandibular plane distances among its variables (literature, VERIFIED abstract).
  - Sivakumar and Valiathan's abstract reports that mesial movement of the posterior teeth came with enough extrusion to increase the vertical dimension, while the mandibular plane angle stayed unchanged (literature, VERIFIED abstract). That is the opposite of the wedge mechanism, measured.
  - The explanation in the review, molars moving parallel to the occlusal plane, comes from Gkantidis 2011 (ref 8), not from data in this review (p 184). The authors add that the face is not "a simple articulator" and point to neuromuscular balance (p 184).
  - Proffit (Ch 5, VERIFIED in the notes): 1 mm of posterior elongation opens the bite about 2 mm anteriorly. Face height follows the molars' vertical position, not their anteroposterior position (my reading).
- **What you will probably hear:** The protocol defined skeletal outcomes, so molar data were out of scope. Molar mesialisation in four-premolar cases is a few millimetres. Dzingle 2026 (literature, VERIFIED abstract): anchorage loss was 3.01 to 3.47 mm across facial types, with a trivial effect on the mandibular plane angle.
- **Follow-up:** "If the wedge worked, second-premolar extraction, with more molar movement, should beat first-premolar extraction. Kim 2005 (27 first- and 27 second-premolar cases, hyperdivergent) found no decrease in facial vertical dimension with either and called the wedge hypothesis invalid. Al-Nimri 2006 (lower first against lower second premolars, 26 and 26) found the mandibular plane unchanged in both (literature, VERIFIED abstracts). Does that settle the mechanism?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is the wedge effect?"

### Q6. "Almost every angle here is the mandibular plane against SN or FH, which is total rotation (two use the palatal plane and one a horizontal plane instead). Proffit says the core of the mandible rotates forward up to 10 to 15°, but surface remodelling hides most of it: the mandibular plane angle moves only 2 to 4° on average. Can a ±1° change in the mandibular plane angle detect a difference in true rotation?"
- **Why it lands:** It links this paper to topic 1 (superimposition).
  - Proffit Ch 4, Fig 4.14 (VERIFIED in the notes; the −10 to −15° core rotation was also seen in the 7th-edition text this run).
  - No included study used implant or structural superimposition. The topic-1 papers in the hub (registry: Nielsen 1989, Doppel 1994, Nguyen 2018) are about exactly that.
- **What you will probably hear:** The mandibular plane angle is what clinicians see and what changes the face, so for a clinical question it is the right outcome. A small difference in core rotation hidden by remodelling would not matter to the patient.
- **Follow-up:** "So the review answers the clinical question, 'does the face change?', but not the biological one, 'does the jaw rotate differently?'"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is Björk's rotation?"

### Q7. "Table II rates 'deviations from intended interventions' as 'No information' in 8 of the 14 studies, and the Results say most studies were problematic because of a lack of information about the mechanics used to close the extraction spaces. Proffit says Class II elastics and headgear distalisation extrude molars and rotate the mandible down unless the ramus grows. Could the mechanics, rather than the extraction, explain the small differences we do see?"
- **Why it lands:** It puts the confounder the authors themselves name at the centre.
  - "No information" on deviations: Basciftci, Bravo, Hosseinzadeh-Nik, Katsaros, Luppanapornlap, Paquette, Sivakumar and Staggers (Table II; derived count). The mechanics problem is stated in the Results (p 178).
  - Proffit (Ch 16, VERIFIED in the notes): Class II elastics carry a vertical force and are contraindicated in nongrowing patients who cannot tolerate downward-backward rotation. More than 2 mm of molar distalisation with headgear comes with extrusion.
  - Registry, `2026-khaleel-deep-bite-turbos-rcos-elastics`: Mn-Mx changed by +0.69 to +1.84° within the three deep-bite mechanics arms (no untreated control), and FH-Mn by +1.31° with posterior box elastics. That is the same order as the largest between-group differences here (1.7 and 2.3°).
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes. That is the real message: the vertical result follows molar control and growth, not the number of teeth.
- **Follow-up:** "Then in a high-angle extraction case the vertical plan is anchorage and molar control: skeletal anchorage, high-pull headgear, no Class II elastics. Not the extraction itself?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Do Class II elastics extrude molars?"

## Tier 3. Methodology

### Q8. "They declined a meta-analysis because of heterogeneity, and the Methods had planned mean differences and SDs (p 176). Twelve comparisons report a mandibular-plane change with SDs. Pooled crudely, fixed effect, extraction minus nonextraction is −0.32° with a 95 % CI of −0.68 to 0.04, and I² is 0 %. Would a pooled estimate with a CI have made the conclusion stronger than counting 10 against 4?"
- **Why it lands:** It turns vote counting into an effect size, and the effect size supports the authors.
  - Derived (`checks.py`). The pool mixes SN-GoGn (8 comparisons), FMA (Bravo, Sivakumar), SN-GoMe (Katsaros) and MP-HP (Staggers).
  - Kumari, Luppanapornlap and Paquette could not be weighted because they give no SDs. Their differences are −0.8, −0.9 and +2.3° (derived).
  - The review says the diversity of growth stages, skeletal patterns, extraction patterns, controls and outcomes made a quantitative synthesis impossible (p 178).
- **What you will probably hear:** The clinical heterogeneity justified a narrative synthesis. A methods-minded attending will agree that a CI tells you more than a tally.
- **Follow-up:** "Even the lower end of that interval, 0.68° in favour of extraction, is less than a degree. Is that a clinically meaningful 'no'?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why didn't they do a meta-analysis?" Page 178 answers it.

### Q9. "Group sizes run from 14 to 45. With an SD of about 2.5°, 15 per group can only detect about 2.6° at 80 % power, and 40 per group about 1.6°. The largest angular difference in any study was 2.3°. So each single 'no difference' was underpowered on its own. Is the agreement across studies the real evidence, rather than any one of them?"
- **Why it lands:** It is the statistical point, made with numbers.
  - Derived (`checks.py`). Smallest group: Hayasaki Class II extraction, 14. Largest: Staggers nonextraction, 45 (Table I).
  - The angular differences: Paquette 2.3°, Porto 1.7°, Hayasaki Class I 1.6° (P .071) (derived from Table III).
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes, and that is what "considerable agreement" in the Conclusions means. The narrow pooled interval in Q8 shows what the agreement adds up to.
- **Follow-up:** "Alrubaie 2026 is the kind of prospective study the authors asked for: 25 and 25 hyperdivergent Class I adults on CBCT, LAFH +1.35 ± 1.73 mm with extraction against +0.8 ± 1.8 mm without, no difference between groups (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Does a prospective design change our confidence, or only confirm it?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Were the studies powered?"

### Q10. "In Table II, Sivakumar is critical on confounding for differences in treatment time and sex (26 girls and 5 boys against 18 and 11; 1.5 against 2.9 y). Paquette's split is 20 girls and 13 boys against 11 and 19, and it is rated moderate on confounding. Staggers is critical overall with no single domain rated critical. How was the 'no information' rule applied?"
- **Why it lands:** It reads the risk-of-bias table cell by cell, which few people do.
  - Female proportion (derived): Paquette 61 % against 37 %; Sivakumar 84 % against 62 %.
  - The rule (p 178): studies missing information in at least one domain were judged between serious and critical overall. Staggers has "No information" for confounding and deviations, and serious for outcome measurement.
  - Methods (p 177) ignore sex differences *between* studies. That is a separate issue from sex imbalance *within* a study, so it is not a contradiction.
- **What you will probably hear:** ROBINS-I is judgement-heavy. The conclusion does not depend on the ratings, because every risk level gave the same answer.
- **Follow-up:** "Keep only the five moderate-risk studies and the only significant result left is Porto's 1.7°, which favours extraction, with FMA not significant (derived). Drop only the critical ones and Sivakumar's result against extraction goes. Does the answer move at all?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is ROBINS-I a good tool?"

### Q11. "The abstract's main 'pro-extraction' result is Kumari: N-Me +1.5 against +5.5 mm, P < .05, with no SDs. Table II notes that Kumari compared only posttreatment values, and Kumari and Fida's own abstract reports no significant vertical difference. Is the 4 mm a change score the reviewers calculated from group means, with a P value from a different comparison?"
- **Why it lands:** It checks the abstract's headline against the paper's own risk-of-bias note.
  - Table II, Kumari, "bias in selection of reported result": serious, "only posttreatment values compared".
  - Kumari and Fida 2010, 40 and 41 patients (literature, VERIFIED abstract): no significant difference in the vertical changes, which increased in both groups. Their abstract also calls the design a cross-sectional comparative study.
  - The Discussion calls the 4 mm "clinically significant" but notes serious risk of bias and inadequate reporting (p 185).
- **What you will probably hear:** Probably calculated from the reported means. Either way it is the weakest of the four results.
- **Follow-up:** "If Kumari is set aside, the score of the significant results is one for extraction, Porto, and two against. Does that make the 'wedge' look even weaker?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The review misreported Kumari."

### Q12. "The abstract calls the quality of evidence 'moderate to low'. No GRADE assessment is described. All 14 studies are retrospective, risk of bias runs from moderate to critical, and the significant results point both ways. On GRADE, observational evidence starts low and comes down for risk of bias. Would 'low to very low certainty' be the standard wording?"
- **Why it lands:** It corrects the vocabulary without touching the conclusion.
  - Abstract (p 175) and Conclusions (p 185) give the quality wording. Results (p 178) give the risk-of-bias spread.
  - Whether ROBINS-I guidance excludes critical-risk studies from a synthesis is UNVERIFIED this run.
- **What you will probably hear:** Yes. A formal GRADE would likely say low to very low (appraisal judgement, derived). The direction of the answer would not change, only how firmly it is stated.
- **Follow-up:** "A letter and the authors' reply were published in AJODO 2019;155(3):305-6. I have not read them (literature, content UNVERIFIED). Does anyone know what was raised?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is GRADE?"

## Tier 4. Proffit, the exam and the rest of the year

### Q13. "Proffit's guidelines: with under 4 mm of crowding, extraction is rarely indicated except for severe protrusion or a severe vertical discrepancy. This review says an extraction protocol to control the vertical is not evidence-based. How would you put the two together in an exam answer?"
- **Why it lands:** It pairs the textbook with the review, which is what an examiner wants.
  - Proffit (Ch 7 extraction guidelines, VERIFIED in the notes and in the printed 7th edition p 221).
  - Proffit's vertical-control tools are all about posterior eruption:
    - Ch 7 (7th-edition text, seen this run): depress the elongated posterior teeth or stop their eruption, so the mandible rotates up and forward.
    - Ch 14 (VERIFIED in the notes): high-pull headgear impedes posterior eruption; bite blocks on functional appliances stretch the soft tissues and impede it too. For severely affected long-face children, only posterior intrusion on skeletal anchorage or a segmental maxillary osteotomy is successful.
  - The term "wedge effect" does not appear in the 7th- or 6th-edition text (searched this run). The review cites Fields, Proffit et al 1984 (ref 25) for it; whether that paper describes it is UNVERIFIED.
- **What you will probably hear:** Extract for crowding, protrusion and profile. Control the vertical with the molars and growth. Proffit's "severe vertical discrepancy" is the exception you keep for the severe open-bite case, and even there the evidence is about molar or second-premolar extraction, not first premolars (Aras 2002, literature, VERIFIED abstract).
- **Follow-up:** "So the model answer for 'can extraction reduce face height?' is no, not by itself, with the mechanism: the vertical is set by molar eruption against ramus growth?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does Proffit say extraction closes the bite?"

### Q14. "A past SBO recall (Ch 8, 2019): a high vertical pattern, first premolars extracted, maximum anchorage needed. The keyed answer is a bone screw. If extraction does not reduce the vertical, letting the molars slide forward to 'wedge' the bite closed does not help either. Is that keyed answer consistent with this review?"
- **Why it lands:** It connects the paper to a question that has already been on the exam.
  - The recall is on the Proffit study pages, Ch 8, "Past-Year SBO Recall" (VERIFIED in the notes).
  - The review finds no consistent skeletal vertical effect of the extraction (Conclusions, p 185). Dzingle 2026: 3.01 to 3.47 mm of anchorage loss with a trivial mandibular-plane effect (literature, VERIFIED abstract).

### Q15. "Topic 18 in our list has Kusnoto and Schneider on controlling the vertical. They describe two ways of using the 'wedge principle' with extractions: let the anchor molars come forward into the wider part of the wedge, or extract first or second molars to lower the posterior height. - **Why it lands:** It shows the wedge idea has a named source in our own reading list and connects this paper to topics 17 and 18.

They attribute the idea that premolar extraction lets the posterior teeth drift "out of the wedge", so the mandible hinges closed, to Pearson. Kouvelis cites Pearson 1978 (ref 5) among the sources of the wedge-type effect (p 175).
  - They also report Yamaguchi and Nanda's finding that molar movement depended on the force system, not on extraction (as reported there; primary paper not read, UNVERIFIED).
  - Vaden and Pearson, Semin Orthod 2002;8:120-129, p 127 (topic 18, paper 1; VERIFIED): in patients with excessive lower anterior face height, extractions "may be necessary". The passage is about uprighting the lower incisors to resolve lip procumbency.
  - Sankey et al, AJODO 2000;118:317-27, p 318 (topic 17, paper 2; VERIFIED): extraction produces dentoalveolar compensation, but molar eruption during space closure could cancel the gain in face height.
- **What you will probably hear:** The older papers were expert reviews. Kouvelis adds a systematic search and a risk-of-bias assessment, and the clinical message is the same: if you extract in a long face, it is for the incisors and lips, and the molars must be held vertically.
- **Follow-up:** "So Vaden and Pearson's 'extractions may be necessary' and Proffit's 'severe vertical discrepancy' both read as extraction for incisor position, not for face height?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Wasn't this already known?" It sounds dismissive of the paper.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"Summarise the review in one breath."**
A systematic review, not registered, searching four databases and the grey literature with no language limit up to August 2017. It included 14 retrospective studies of about 860 patients comparing four-premolar extraction with nonextraction fixed treatment on skeletal vertical cephalometric measures. Risk of bias by ROBINS-I was moderate in 5, serious in 5 and critical in 3, with one study split. Nothing was pooled. Ten studies found no difference; four found small differences, two in each direction. The authors conclude that extraction has no specific effect on the vertical and that extracting to control the vertical is not evidence-based.

**"What is the wedge effect, and what does the review say about it?"**
- The idea: when the extraction spaces close, the molars come forward into the wider, anterior part of the wedge between the jaws (which converge posteriorly), the mandible rotates counterclockwise, and face height falls (Introduction p 175, refs 3 to 5; Discussion p 184, ref 25).
- The review finds no consistent skeletal effect: no mean angular change above 1.2° except Paquette's −2.0° in a nonextraction group.
- The authors' explanation, from Gkantidis 2011: the molars move parallel to the occlusal plane, not along the maxillary and mandibular planes. Neuromuscular balance matters more than the number of teeth.
- The review did not extract molar movement, so this is an explanation, not a test. Where a primary study did look (Sivakumar, VERIFIED abstract), the molars extruded as they came forward.

**"Which studies found a difference?"**
- For extraction: Porto 2012, SN-GoGn −0.9 vs +0.8° (P .020) but FMA not significant; Kumari 2010, N-Me +1.5 vs +5.5 mm (P < .05) but FMA not significant.
- Against: Sivakumar 2008, N-Me +2.3 vs +0.9 mm (P .033); Paquette 1992, FMA +0.3 vs −2.0° (P < .010) (Table III).
- The authors call Porto, Sivakumar and Paquette clinically questionable (p 178), but Kumari's 4 mm clinically significant, with serious risk of bias (p 185).

**"Why was there no meta-analysis, and was that right?"**
- High clinical heterogeneity: growth stage, skeletal and vertical pattern, extraction pattern, controls and outcome measures differed (p 178). Poor reporting was the other reason (p 185).
- It was defensible. Even so, 12 comparisons share a mandibular-plane outcome with SDs, and an exploratory pool gives −0.32° (−0.68 to 0.04), I² 0 % (derived). That supports the authors' conclusion more precisely than their vote count does.

**"So in a high-angle patient, do you extract or not?"**
- The extraction decision is made on crowding, incisor protrusion and the soft-tissue profile (Proffit Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes; past recalls 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024 key the chin and lip relationship as the deciding factor).
- The vertical is managed separately, by controlling posterior eruption: high-pull headgear, bite blocks, skeletal anchorage for molar intrusion, and avoiding Class II elastics and other extrusive mechanics (Proffit Ch 14 and Ch 16, VERIFIED in the notes).
- If I extract, I choose anchorage for the anteroposterior plan; the past-exam answer for maximum anchorage in a high-angle case is a bone screw (Ch 8 recall, 2019).

**"A patient with an open-bite tendency has root-treated upper first molars, a root-treated lower right second premolar with an apical lesion, impacted lower third molars and supernumeraries. Which teeth do you extract?"**
This stem is in the recall file "Promotion 2021" (p 16, Q1) with three options: upper first molars; upper first molars plus lower premolars and third molars; upper and lower first premolars. No key is recorded.
- I would extract the teeth with the poorest prognosis: the root-treated upper first molars and the lower premolars, including the one with the lesion, plus the impacted third molars (option 2; my answer, not a recorded key).
- This review gives no vertical reason to sacrifice sound first premolars instead: four-premolar extraction has no specific skeletal vertical effect (Conclusions, p 185).
- If anything, molar extraction suits a posterior open-bite tendency: Aras 2002 found a closing rotation with second-premolar or first-molar extraction when the open bite extended posteriorly (literature, VERIFIED abstract). Kouvelis excluded molar-extraction studies (p 184), so it says nothing about that pattern.
- Either way I would hold the molars vertically during space closure, with miniscrews for intrusion if the bite opens (past recall Ch 14, 2023).

**"What level of evidence is this, and how certain?"**
- A systematic review of non-randomised, retrospective cohorts: Oxford CEBM 2009 level 2a with a minus for heterogeneity, and level 3 on the 2011 table (appraisal mapping, derived).
- The authors say "moderate to low" quality without a formal method. A GRADE-style judgement would be low to very low certainty (derived).
- What makes it believable is consistency. Later work agrees (literature, VERIFIED abstracts):
  - Rüedi 2024, 45 and 31 hyperdivergent patients: SN-ML difference 0.07° (95 % CI −0.90 to 1.01).
  - Ferrillo, Pandis and Fleming 2026, 168 patients: four-premolar effect on MMPA −0.40° (−1.75 to 0.96).
  - Alrubaie 2026, prospective CBCT, 25 and 25 adults: no difference in LAFH.

**"Does Proffit support the wedge effect?"**
- The term is not in the textbook (searched this run). It is in our year's reading list: Kusnoto and Schneider (topic 18) describe the "wedge principle" and conclude that its extraction-based use has not been scientifically supported (VERIFIED, read this run).
- Proffit's vertical mechanism is posterior eruption against ramus growth:
  - 1 mm of posterior elongation opens the bite about 2 mm (Ch 5, VERIFIED in the notes).
  - Open bite is treated by depressing the posterior teeth or stopping their eruption so the mandible rotates up and forward (Ch 7, 7th-edition text seen this run).
  - Severe long faces need posterior intrusion on skeletal anchorage or surgery (Ch 14, VERIFIED in the notes).
- His one extraction-related line is that a severe vertical discrepancy can justify extraction even with little crowding (Ch 7, VERIFIED in the notes). That fits the severe open bite, which this review did not study.

## Ten-second summary

"Fourteen retrospective studies, about 860 patients, all comparing four-premolar extraction with nonextraction on skeletal vertical measures. Ten found nothing. The four that found something split two ways, and no difference was larger than 2.3° or 4 mm. Risk of bias was moderate to critical, and nothing was pooled. Bottom line: extraction is not a vertical-control tool. Molar control and growth decide the vertical, not the number of teeth."
