# Questions to ask about this paper

Doppel 1994 is a landmark-drift table, not a trial, so the good questions are about what the table can and cannot see. The paper hands you its own openings: the best number in Table II (internal palate, 0.85 ± 0.43 mm) is overruled by eye, the palate borders are "next in reliability" on page 164 and "not reliable" on page 168, the posterior key ridge moved posteriorly in 50 of 50 cases and is still the recommended structure, and the 1.5 : 1 ratio in Conclusion 2 has no table behind it. Every number below was checked against the printed tables (pages 163 to 164); anything from outside the paper is labelled (literature), (Proffit), (registry), derived, or my read.

How to ask: name the table or figure, keep the question to one breath, and frame the methodological ones as "what did the metric miss?" rather than "why is this wrong?". Nielsen 1989 and Nguyen 2018 are on the same table in this session, so a question that puts a Doppel number next to a Nielsen or Nguyen number lands better than one about Doppel alone. The consultants trained on the ABO structural method, which is this paper's conclusion written into a protocol, so do not argue against the zygomatic process; ask what it costs.

## Tier 1 safe and sharp

### Q1. "Table II gives the internal palate the smallest deviation of all seventeen structures, 0.85 ± 0.43 mm, and the Discussion then says it did not superimpose well and is of limited value. What did the maximum-distance metric miss that the eye caught?"
- **Why it lands:** It shows you read Table II against Conclusion 5 and the Discussion paragraph "Internal maxilla structure". My read: a palate that resorbs on the nasal side and apposes on the oral side keeps its contour shape while descending as a block, so a contour-to-contour distance measured after implant registration can be small locally while the whole region has moved. Nielsen measured that block error: implant minus best fit, vertical, 1.08 mm at PNS, 1.88 mm at ANS and 2.24 mm at A point over 4 years (Nielsen Table III). Doppel's own Fig 4 says the internal-palate method behaves like registration at ANS: incisors appear lingual, molars vertical, occlusal plane up and forward.
- **What you will probably hear:** That the internal palate is a diffuse, low-contrast trabecular area, so a best fit is a judgement; that the authors preferred larger structures with a consistent fit (page 165); and that this is why the ABO uses the superior surface of the palatal floor as a vertical guide rather than as the registration area (ABO 2025 workshop deck; literature, VERIFIED).
- **Follow-up:** "If one row can be overruled by eye, how much weight do the other sixteen carry? The Discussion itself says the statistics depend on empirical observations of best fit."
- **Avoid saying:** "Why did they reject their best result?" It reads as a gotcha. Ask what the metric measures.

### Q2. "On page 164 the palate borders are next in reliability at 1.05 and 1.14 mm; in Conclusion 6 they are not reliable. The posterior key ridge passed at 0.97 mm. Is there a threshold, and what does a cut-off near 1 mm mean for the nasal floor we all trace?"
- **Why it lands:** 0.97 and 1.05 mm are 0.08 mm apart, a third of the stated tracing precision (± 0.25 mm, Methods). You are pointing out that the verdict changed between Results and Conclusions while the data did not, and the paper never states a threshold. The palate is the most common clinical maxillary reference; Proffit's maxillary superimposition figure is transcribed as "on the maximum contour of the maxilla" in the study pages (Proffit, Fig 6.59, wording UNVERIFIED against print).
- **What you will probably hear:** That the ranking is three tiers by eye: zygomatic process accepted, palate borders borderline, everything from 1.27 mm up rejected. A biology-minded consultant will say the palate borders fail on direction, not on size: the nasal floor resorbs and the oral side apposes (Björk & Skieller 1972, cited as ref 14), so the 1 mm is a one-way drift, which leads straight into Q3.
- **Follow-up:** "If a 1 mm one-way drift over four years disqualifies the palate, why does it not disqualify the posterior key ridge, which moved posteriorly in every case?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The conclusions contradict the results." Give the two page numbers and let them say it.

### Q3. "The posterior key ridge is called most reliable at 0.97 ± 0.43 mm, and it moved posteriorly in 50 of 50 cases. That is a consistent bias, not stability. When you use it, how do you allow for the apposition?"
- **Why it lands:** Results page 164 and Conclusion 1 ("always demonstrated apposition"). The Introduction cites Björk & Skieller (ref 14) for apposition on the posterior surface, greatest inferiorly, and Nielsen rejected Riedel's infratemporal-fossa registration for exactly that reason. Fig 6, registration on the posterior process plus the internal palate, shows the implants drifting superiorly and anteriorly, which is the bias in action. The ABO traces both legs but orients anteroposteriorly on the anterior surface (ABO deck; literature, VERIFIED).
- **What you will probably hear:** "You do not register on it, you keep it parallel." The practical answer is the ABO one: the anterior surface sets the anteroposterior position, the posterior contour is kept parallel with the T2 line allowed to sit slightly behind the T1 line, and the vertical is set by the orbital floor and palatal floor. Some will add that 0.97 mm over 4.2 years is small and predictable, so a consistent drift is easier to live with than a random one.
- **Follow-up:** "Doppel measured unsigned distance. A surface drifting 1 mm the same way every time and one wobbling ± 1 mm at random get the same score. Which rows of Table II are which?"
- **Avoid saying:** "So is the posterior key ridge unreliable?" The paper says it is reliable; your point is what reliable means here.

### Q4. "The 1.5 : 1 ratio of orbital-floor rise to palatal-plane lowering is Conclusion 2 and the basis of the recommended method, but there is no table or measurement for it. Is it Björk & Skieller's 3 : 2 restated, and how do you apply it at the tracing table?"
- **Why it lands:** The Methods describe no measurement of orbital apposition or nasal-floor resorption. The only tabulated pair that resembles it is orbital floor 1.40 ± 0.98 mm (N = 47) against palate-superior 1.05 ± 0.52 mm, a ratio of 1.33 : 1 (derived, and not the same quantity, because deviations are unsigned). 3 : 2 is 1.5 : 1 exactly (Björk & Skieller 1977, nine boys; literature as cited by the paper). The Discussion says the ratio agrees with Björk & Skieller and Nielsen, but Nielsen set apposition equal to resorption (1 : 1) and reported a small vertical over-read of −0.04 to −0.33 mm, not significant (Nielsen Tables III and IV), so the agreement with Nielsen is loose. The ABO deck prints 3/5 : 2/5 (literature, VERIFIED). Page 165 prints the ratio as "1:5 to 1 mm", a typographical slip.
- **What you will probably hear:** That the ratio was observed on the implant superimpositions and reported as confirmation of Björk, not measured independently; and the tracing-table instruction: register anteroposteriorly on the anterior zygomatic surface, then set T2 so the orbital floor sits three-fifths of the vertical gap above the T1 floor and the palatal floor two-fifths below. Someone may add that the ratio was derived in untreated boys and survived a mostly treated sample here, which is reassuring but unmeasured.
- **Follow-up:** "Is 1.5 : 1 a population mean or an individual rule? The SDs in Table II are 0.98 and 0.52 mm, and Julius found the palatal plane rotates about 3° at its anterior end (ref 17). Does a 3 : 1 case exist?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Where did 1.5 come from?" Ask whether it was measured or inherited.

## Tier 2 mechanics and biology of remodeling

### Q5. "Björk & Skieller had the anterior zygomatic surface stable in nine of nine, Nielsen found no resorption on it in eighteen, and Doppel got anterior change in 27.5 %, posterior change in 35 % and none in 37.5 % of forty films. Resorptive fields or transverse rotation of the two maxillae: which do you buy, and what does it do to the anteroposterior registration?"
- **Why it lands:** Results page 164 and the Discussion paragraph "Zygomatic process" offer four explanations: a larger sample, a treated sample, exact fit on all three implants rather than Björk's lateral-implant registration, and head rotation. The Introduction cites Björk & Skieller (ref 4) for shortening of the anterior-lateral implant distance as the two maxillae rotate about the midpalatal suture, and Kurihara, Enlow and Rangel (ref 15) for person-variable resorptive fields on the anterior zygomatic surface. Korn & Baumrind 1990 (literature, VERIFIED abstract) give the transverse rotation a size: posterior maxillary widening 0.43 mm per year in 31 implant subjects.
- **What you will probably hear:** Most will say the surface is stable enough, 0.95 ± 0.57 mm with no preferred direction, and that a split of 27.5 / 35 / 37.5 is noise about zero, which is the property you want in a registration surface and the opposite of the posterior leg. A biology-minded consultant will say both happen: a resorptive field in some patients, transverse rotation projecting as apparent forward movement in others.
- **Follow-up:** "If the direction is random, the anterior surface is the ideal registration surface in Doppel's own terms. Why does the abstract rank it with the posterior leg rather than above it?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is the anterior zygomatic surface stable or not?" The numbers answer that; the question is the mechanism.

### Q6. "Table III: the palatal plane rotated +0.92 ± 2.10° relative to the implant line, range −5° to +8°. The paper calls that variable, but a one-sample t on their own numbers gives t of about 3.1 and P of about .003, so it also rotates systematically anterior-down, about 1° in four years, in the same direction as Julius's 3°. Should we ever read maxillary rotation off ANS-PNS?"
- **Why it lands:** The test is derived from Table III (SE 2.10/√50 = 0.30°); the paper has no inferential statistics anywhere. Julius (ref 17, cited) found downward and backward rotation of the palatal plane of about 3° at its anterior end. Baumrind 1987 (ref 18, cited; literature) found variability in the rotation of ANS-PNS. Proffit Ch 4 (study pages, [6th p88]) describes external rotation of the maxilla by differential palatal remodelling that masks the internal rotation seen with implants, so the palatal plane changes little in most patients.
- **What you will probably hear:** "No. SN transferred by the cranial-base superimposition, then the implant line or the zygomatic structural fit, is the reference; ANS-PNS remodels at both ends." Some will say the mean is small and the SD is what matters: ± 2° means an individual case can be 5° off, and at a molar 40 mm from the registration area 5° is about 3.5 mm (derived, tan 5° × 40 mm).
- **Follow-up:** "SN to the implant line was −0.56 ± 2.19°, slightly up and forward on average, while the palatal plane rotated anterior-down inside the maxilla. Are those Proffit's internal and external rotations, and do they cancel in a treated sample?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did the palatal plane rotate?" Table III says yes.

### Q7. "A point deviated 2.13 ± 1.63 mm, up to 6.55 mm, and the paper blames tooth movement and the forces of treatment. After headgear or Class II elastics, how much of a 2° change in SNA is skeletal?"
- **Why it lands:** Table II: A point 2.13 ± 1.63, ANS horizontal 1.43 ± 1.14, ANS vertical 1.29 ± 0.86 mm; Discussion "Other structures". Proffit Ch 2 (study pages): the anterior surface of the maxilla is resorptive except near ANS, and A point sits on alveolar bone that follows the incisor roots. Nielsen's largest skeletal under-read was A point, 2.24 mm (Nielsen Table III). A point is roughly 5 to 6 cm from nasion (anatomical approximation, UNVERIFIED), so 1° of SNA is about 1 mm at A point (derived), the same order as the 4-year drift measured here.
- **What you will probably hear:** That a 1 to 2° SNA change in a headgear study cannot be split into restraint and alveolar remodelling without implants or a structural superimposition, and that Baumrind (ref 18) found remodelling differs between treated and untreated subjects. The practical advice: read maxillary displacement from the cranial-base superimposition of the zygomatic structures, and use A point only to describe what happened to the alveolus.
- **Follow-up:** "Would you report headgear restraint as displacement of the zygomatic process on the cranial-base superimposition rather than as a change in SNA?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is A point reliable?" Table II says no.

### Q8. "PNS deviated 2.27 ± 1.75 mm and was visible in about half the films; PTM moved 2.39 mm vertically. The paper says the maxilla lengthens at PNS. If someone reads molar distalisation after headgear on a PTM-registered superimposition, which way does the error run?"
- **Why it lands:** Fig 2 (palatal plane registered at PTM): the implants appear to move anteriorly and superiorly, and incisors and molars appear to move labially with minimal descent. My read: PTM registration subtracts the posterior growth of the maxilla, so teeth look more anterior relative to the maxilla, distalisation is under-read and mesial drift over-read. Nielsen: PNS moved back 2.34 mm in 4 years on implants but 1.18 mm on best fit (Nielsen Table I). Riedel abandoned the infratemporal-fossa method in 1974 because of the lengthening at PNS (Introduction). Proffit Ch 2 (study pages): bone is added at the tuberosity posteriorly to make room for the molars.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that PTM and PNS are the posterior growth front, plus the observation that PNS is often hidden by developing second molars (Brodie, cited) and PTM by double contours. Some will add that over a short interval in a 16-year-old the error is small, but over two years of headgear at age 10 it is of the order of 1 mm (my scaling of Nielsen's 2.3 mm per 4 years; derived, crude).
- **Follow-up:** "Fig 3, the infratemporal fossa method, rotates the occlusal plane markedly down and back. Which of the older headgear papers used PTM or the fossa as the maxillary reference, and should their molar numbers be re-read?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why not use PTM?" The paper says why.

## Tier 3 methodology

### Q9. "The fifty were the 7 % of about seven hundred records in which all three implants still superimposed perfectly. Björk & Skieller showed the anterior-lateral implant distance shortens with growth, and the Discussion admits some implants may have matched only because of head position. Does selecting on the gold standard behaving flatter the anatomy?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods page 163 (selection criteria) and Discussion pages 166 to 167 ("possibility for a systematic error"). Björk & Skieller registered on the lateral implant and oriented on the implant line to absorb transverse rotation; Doppel fitted all three. Julius (ref 17): the zygomatic implant shifts up to 3 mm with head rotation, and patients rotate up to 3° and tip up to 5° in a cephalostat. Korn & Baumrind 1990 (literature): 0.43 mm per year of posterior widening, so a four-year pair with real rotation should not fit on three points unless head position cancelled it.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Most rejections were for implant loss or migration, not rotation", which is what the Methods say. A careful consultant will concede that a three-point criterion selects either low-rotation cases or head-position-matched pairs, and that in either case the anatomy, particularly the anterior key ridge, looks better than it would on routine films. Then the standard defence: fifty is still the largest anatomical-landmark-versus-implant series (Björk & Skieller 9 and 21, Baumrind 31, Nielsen 18).
- **Follow-up:** "Bilateral implants, which the authors say would have helped, were never placed here. Does the ABO's requirement to fit both zygomatic processes and both orbital floors do the same job?"
- **Avoid saying:** "The sample is biased." Say what it was selected on.

### Q10. "Table II is titled maximum distance, but curved surfaces were averaged over several points. The winners are all surfaces and the losers are all points. With tracing error at 0.5 mm and the best means at 0.85 to 0.97 mm, how much of the ranking is the measurement rule?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods page 163: point landmarks scored as a single maximum, curved surfaces as an average of several points, and an average is never larger than a maximum. The retrace of 10 films gave ± 0.25 mm, restated as 0.5 mm, with no numeric table; 0.5/0.85 = 59 % of the best mean (derived). Five rows share a minimum of 0.15 mm. Distances are unsigned, so a one-way drift and random wobble score the same. There is no inferential statistic in the paper; "significant amount of change" (page 164) is a phrase, not a test.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that the paper is descriptive, with the defence that the clinically relevant gap survives the metric: key ridge about 1 mm against A point, PNS and PTM at 2.1 to 2.4 mm is a difference of three tracing errors. Someone will quote Doppel's own caveat that closely spaced implants make the error a function of distance from the implants (page 167): for each 1° of rotational misfit a structure 30 mm away moves 0.5 mm and one 60 mm away about 1 mm (derived, tan 1°), which is part of why the cranial-base rows are large.
- **Follow-up:** "Would a signed per-year drift with a Dahlberg error have changed any conclusion, or only the confidence in it?"
- **Avoid saying:** "There are no statistics." True, but blunt; ask what the ranking can support.

### Q11. "The Methods say the majority were treated, the Results say the fifty treated cases, and the −6° to +9° rotation of SN to the implant line is attributed to treatment variation. Baumrind found treated and untreated subjects remodel differently. Without the split, is this a growth paper or a treatment-effects paper?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods page 162, Results page 164, Discussion "Maxilla rotation" ("may be a result of the treatment"), Baumrind 1987 (ref 18). Headgear, Class II and III elastics and extractions all load the maxilla and its alveolar process; A point and ANS are the most treatment-sensitive rows in Table II. T1 ranges from 8.7 to 16.0 years with no maturity indicator, and the interval from 3.0 to 6.4 years with no per-year rate.
- **What you will probably hear:** "It is a landmark paper about the patients we treat, which is the point." A growth-minded consultant will separate the two messages: the zygomatic stability and the 1.5 : 1 ratio were found by Björk in untreated boys and survive treatment here, which is useful; the rotation ranges mix biology with headgear and elastics and cannot be interpreted.
- **Follow-up:** "High-pull headgear loads exactly the structures in the 1.5 : 1 ratio. Would you expect the ratio to survive it, and did anyone check?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why did they not separate treated from untreated?" The answer is sample size; ask what the mix does to each conclusion.

## Tier 4 Proffit and the exam

### Q12. "Proffit describes the maxillary superimposition on the maximum contour of the maxilla; the ABO asks for both zygomatic legs, both orbital floors and the palatal floor with a 3/5 : 2/5 vertical rule. Which version do we give in the exam, and is Doppel the reason the ABO version exists?"
- **Why it lands:** The ABO superimposition page cites Doppel 1994 and Nielsen 1989 as its validity references, and its 2025 workshop deck acknowledges Robert Little, Doppel's co-author (literature, VERIFIED). The paper's recommended method, anterior and posterior zygomatic contours with the orbital floor rising 1.5 times the palatal lowering (Clinical implications, Fig 7), is the ABO method with the ratio written differently. Proffit's figure wording (Fig 6.59, 7th edition, study pages) is UNVERIFIED against print and may read "palatal vault".
- **What you will probably hear:** "Describe Björk's structural method by structure, not by plane": cranial base on the anterior cranial fossa and the anterior wall of sella, maxilla on the anterior zygomatic surface with the 3 : 2 vertical rule, mandible on the inner cortex of the symphysis and the chin. Someone may cite Graf 2022 (literature, VERIFIED abstract), a systematic review in which 25 of 27 superimposition studies were at high risk of bias and no method was proven accurate, as the counterweight to the ABO wording.
- **Follow-up:** "If an examiner asks for the evidence that the structural method is valid, is Doppel's fifty and Nielsen's eighteen enough, given that neither tested the combined ABO method against implants?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What does Proffit say?" You should know; ask which version to present.

### Q13. "Proffit's growth chapter says the nasal floor resorbs, the oral palate apposes and the orbital floor apposes, so the palate moves down more than the maxilla is displaced. Is Doppel's 1.5 : 1 and Nielsen's 30 to 50 % just that paragraph with numbers on it, and how do I say it in one breath?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit Ch 2 (Fig 2.30) and Ch 4 (study pages) say exactly this. Doppel's orbital floor 1.40 mm against palate borders 1.05 and 1.14 mm (Table II) and the 1.5 : 1 ratio, Nielsen's 1.08 to 2.24 mm under-read (Nielsen Table III) and Björk & Skieller's 3 : 2 are the same biology measured three ways. You are tying the growth chapter to the diagnosis chapter, which is what the exam rewards.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Yes, and the one-breath answer is: a tracing registered on the palate rides down with it, so eruption and alveolar growth are under-read; the structural method puts the descent back by letting the orbital floor rise three units for every two the palate drops." Some will add Proffit's internal and external rotation of the maxilla (Ch 4, [6th p88]) as the same mechanism seen as rotation.
- **Follow-up:** "Proffit says the maxilla's internal and external rotations are usually equal and opposite, so the palatal plane changes little. Doppel's ± 2.10° and 13° range say that is an average, not an individual. Do we teach the mean or the spread?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does Proffit cover superimposition?"

### Q14. "Khaleel 2026, which we read earlier, superimposed the maxilla on the palatal vault after Broadbent over 44 to 94 days and reported upper posterior extrusion of 0.07 to 0.42 mm with SDs of 0.07 to 0.29 mm. Doppel's internal palate drifts 0.85 mm in four years and his tracing error is 0.5 mm. Over six to thirteen weeks, is the method bias the problem, or the resolution?"
- **Why it lands:** Registry: Khaleel's key-numbers file gives the method and the values. Doppel's Fig 4 says the internal-palate method behaves like ANS registration, so the bias direction is known: descent of the maxillary teeth is under-read. Its size over Khaleel's intervals is negligible: 0.85 mm per 4.2 years is about 0.2 mm per year, so 0.03 to 0.05 mm over 44 to 94 days (derived, linear, and in 16- to 17-year-olds probably less). The resolution is the problem: a 0.5 mm tracing error against between-arm differences of 0.2 to 0.35 mm. My read: the direction of Khaleel's upper-arch finding is plausible and its magnitude is below what a 2D tracing can resolve.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that the choice of maxillary method hardly matters for short intervals in older adolescents, and that the real point is precision: a hand-traced cephalogram cannot support 0.2 mm differences. Someone will say this is why Nguyen's 0.12 ± 0.11 mm interobserver error in 3D matters.
- **Follow-up:** "Should a short-interval treatment paper report its superimposition error in millimetres rather than an intra-examiner ICC?"
- **Avoid saying:** Anything that sounds like re-litigating Khaleel. One neutral sentence, then back to Doppel.

## Do not ask these

1. "Which structure was most reliable?" The abstract and Conclusion 1 say the posterior and anterior zygomatic process.
2. "Where were the implants placed?" The Methods list Björk's sites: below ANS, in the zygomatic process, and at the palate-alveolar border medial to the first molar.
3. "How many subjects, and were they growing?" Table I: 50, T1 11.9 ± 1.4 years, interval 4.2 ± 0.96 years; the Methods say adolescent implanted patients.
4. "Why did they not use CBCT or 3D?" The films date from 1967 to 1975 and the paper is from 1994. The 3D analogue is Nguyen 2018, which is on the same table.
5. "Was it randomised, and was there a control group?" It is a descriptive validation of landmark drift against implants; there is nothing to randomise and no treatment to control. The question shows the design has not been placed.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"What did they measure, and against what?"**
Fifty pairs of lateral cephalograms at least 3 years apart (mean 4.2 ± 0.96 years, Table I) from University of Washington patients with three maxillary tantalum implants at Björk's sites, chosen from about 700 records because all three implants superimposed perfectly. After registering the two tracings on the implants, one operator measured the maximum distance each of 17 anatomical structures deviated between films, averaged over several points for curved surfaces (Table II), and the rotation of ANS-PNS and of transferred SN relative to the lateral-to-anterior implant line (Table III). There is no hypothesis test; the ranking is by mean deviation, with tracing error stated as 0.5 mm.

**"Give me the numbers for the structures you would use and the ones you would avoid."**
Use the zygomatic process: posterior contour 0.97 ± 0.43 mm, traceable in all films but appositional in every case, and anterior contour 0.95 ± 0.57 mm, traceable in 80 % with no preferred direction of change (Table II, Results). Avoid A point 2.13 ± 1.63, PNS 2.27 ± 1.75 (visible in about half), PTM vertical 2.39 ± 1.69, infraorbital foramen 1.67 ± 1.31 and ANS 1.29 to 1.43 mm. The internal palate has the smallest number, 0.85 ± 0.43 mm, but the authors found it did not superimpose well and called it of limited value, and the palate borders at 1.05 and 1.14 mm are listed as unreliable in Conclusion 6.

**"What happens to the apparent tooth movement if you register on the palatal plane at ANS?"**
Doppel's Fig 1: the implants appear to move posteriorly and superiorly, the incisors appear to move lingually, the molars appear to descend vertically and the occlusal plane rotates up and forward, so the vertical descent of the maxillary dentition is under-read. Nielsen put a size on it in 18 implant subjects over 4 years: 1.33 mm at the molar crown and 1.84 mm at the incisor edge, about 30 % of molar and 50 % of incisor eruption, with skeletal landmarks under-read by 1.08 to 2.24 mm (Nielsen Tables III and IV). The horizontal direction is case-dependent, because the palatal plane itself rotated −5° to +8° relative to the implants (Table III).

**"What is the vertical rule, and where does it come from?"**
The paper says the orbital floor rose more than the palatal plane lowered in a ratio of 1.5 : 1 (abstract, Discussion, Conclusion 2) and recommends superimposing on the anterior and posterior zygomatic contours with that allowance (Fig 7). It is Björk & Skieller's 3 : 2 apposition-to-resorption ratio from nine implanted boys, and the ABO teaches it as 3/5 orbital-floor apposition to 2/5 nasal-floor resorption (literature, VERIFIED). No table in the paper measures it; the closest tabulated pair, orbital floor 1.40 mm against palate-superior 1.05 mm, gives 1.33 : 1 (derived) and is not the same quantity.

**"The cranial base rows are 2.5 mm. Is the anterior cranial base unstable?"**
No. The clinoid (2.64 ± 1.43 mm), anterior cranial base (2.52 ± 1.13 mm) and ethmoid triad (2.52 ± 2.01 mm) were measured after registration on the maxillary implants, so they record the displacement of the maxilla away from the cranial base over 4.2 years, plus the lever-arm effect the authors describe for structures far from the implants (page 167). SN rotated −0.56 ± 2.19° relative to the implant line (Table III), which is maxillary rotation relative to the cranial base, not cranial-base error. The paper transferred SN using Björk's cranial-base superimposition, which treats those structures as stable.

**"Give me the limitations in three sentences."**
The fifty are the 7 % of records in which the gold standard behaved, there is no inferential statistic or stated reliability threshold, and the metric mixes maxima for points with averages for surfaces and is unsigned, so it cannot separate a one-way drift from random error. One unblinded operator traced, superimposed and judged best fit, and the best number in Table II was overruled by that judgement. Treated and untreated subjects are mixed without a count, ages at T1 run from 8.7 to 16.0 years with no maturity staging, and the 1.5 : 1 ratio and the recommended combined method appear in no table.

## Ten-second summary

"Fifty implant cases, four years of growth and treatment: the only maxillary structures that stayed within about 1 mm of the implants were the anterior and posterior contours of the zygomatic process; ANS, PNS, PTM, A point and the infraorbital foramen drifted 1.3 to 2.4 mm; the palate scored well but did not fit; and the palatal plane rotated −5° to +8° inside the maxilla. The recommended method, both zygomatic contours with the orbital floor rising 1.5 times what the palate drops, is the ABO method. The ranking is descriptive, selected on perfect implant fit, measured with a 0.5 mm tracing error and never tested, so I take the structures and leave the decimals."
