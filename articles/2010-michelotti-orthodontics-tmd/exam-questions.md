## Part 1. Multiple-choice questions

### Q1 (R). Age and TMD

According to Michelotti and Iodice 2010, TMD signs and symptoms are already frequent in children and adolescents. In which age band does the review report increased prevalence?

A. 6 to 12 years
B. 15 to 45 years
C. 45 to 65 years
D. Over 65 years
E. Prevalence is constant across ages

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The abstract says prevalence rises between 15 and 45 years, with TMJ sounds already common in children and adolescents (abstract).

### Q2 (N). Why the orthodontist gets blamed

A 15-year-old develops a TMJ click 14 months into fixed-appliance treatment, and the parents blame the braces. Which explanation best fits the evidence this review summarises?

A. Fixed appliances push the condyle back and displace the disc.
B. Treatment lasting about 2 years falls in the age band when TMD signs naturally appear and come and go, so the timing is likely coincidence.
C. Clicking is caused by the occlusal interferences that always occur during levelling.
D. Treatment in adolescence doubles the risk of TMD in adulthood.
E. The click proves the finished occlusion will not be in centric relation.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:**
- The abstract sets out the timing: TMD signs are common in children and adolescents and rise between 15 and 45 years; treatment lasts about 2 years; so patients may complain during or after it and blame the orthodontist (abstract). "Likely coincidence" is the inference the cohorts below support.
- In Macfarlane 2009 (cited in the review, ref 92), TMD rose from 3.2 % to 17.6 % between 11-12 and 19-20 years, with no association with orthodontics (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract).
- Henrikson 2000 found that clicking increased over 2 years in all three groups: treated girls, untreated Class II girls and normal-occlusion girls (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract).
- D is wrong: Macfarlane 2009 found no association between orthodontics and new or persistent TMD, and Egermark 2003 no higher risk later in life (VERIFIED).

### Q3 (R). Does TMD cause malocclusion?

The review reverses the usual question and asks whether TMD can cause malocclusion. Which TMJ conditions does it give as causes?

A. Myalgia, myofascial pain and arthralgia
B. Disc displacement with reduction, subluxation and bruxism
C. Condylar hyperplasia, osteochondroma and rheumatoid arthritis
D. Costen's syndrome, tinnitus and otalgia
E. Occlusal dysesthesia, hypervigilance and somatisation

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:**
- The 2018 interview names this section of the 2010 review and these three conditions, calling them relatively uncommon and often aggressive (VERIFIED, PMC6018446).
- The review's reference list includes papers on condylar hyperplasia (refs 101-105) and juvenile idiopathic arthritis (refs 106-116) (VERIFIED, Crossref).
- How each changes mandibular growth or the bite (asymmetry, crossbite, open bite) is UNVERIFIED for the 2010 text.
- In the same interview the author adds that disc displacement in growing patients may slow condylar growth, but the effect is rare and small.

### Q4 (A). TMD signs during treatment

A 17-year-old girl, 10 months into fixed appliances with Class II elastics, reports pain in front of the right ear and in the right cheek on chewing for 3 weeks. Palpation of the right masseter reproduces her familiar pain. What is the most appropriate next step?

A. Increase the elastic force to finish the Class II correction quickly.
B. Selectively grind the premature contacts to balance the occlusion.
C. Make a differential diagnosis, pause active forces (elastics and activations), and start conservative care; resume once she is pain-free.
D. Debond permanently and refer for TMJ surgery.
E. Order MRI and CBCT of both joints before any other step.

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:**
- The author's protocol (VERIFIED, 2018 interview) has four steps:
  1. Evaluate and make a differential diagnosis.
  2. Temporarily stop active treatment.
  3. Give conservative care: medication, counselling, behavioural therapy, home exercises, physical therapy, and a splint if needed.
  4. Continue or modify treatment when she is pain-free.
- Fernández-González 2015 gives the same steps in a paragraph that cites this review; its citation sits on the "do not start while in pain" sentence (VERIFIED). The 2010 wording is UNVERIFIED.
- The interview also says that TMD signs during elastic wear can be managed with a temporary pause, and that bilateral Class II elastics do not cause TMD in the long term (VERIFIED).
- B is wrong: occlusal interventions lack evidence (Cochrane 2024, 57 RCTs) and irreversible dental treatment is not routinely recommended (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture).
- E is wrong: imaging is not routine and is used only to confirm a clinical diagnosis when needed (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

### Q5 (N). Experimental occlusal interferences

Healthy women fitted with an active occlusal interference for 8 days reduced their masseter activity and none developed TMD (Michelotti 2005). In another trial, women with a past TMD history developed significantly more clinical signs after a true interference for 2 weeks, while those without a history adapted (Le Bell 2002). What is the best interpretation?

A. Occlusal interferences are the main cause of TMD in the general population.
B. Interferences are harmless for everyone, so occlusion never matters.
C. Most people adapt to an occlusal change; vulnerability lies in the individual (TMD history, parafunction, psychological factors), not in the occlusion alone.
D. A dummy interference causes more pain than an active one.
E. Interferences cause disc displacement within 2 weeks.

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:**
- Michelotti 2005: 11 healthy women, active interference against dummy, 8 days each; masseter activity periods and amplitude fell, and no one developed TMD (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract).
- Le Bell 2002: 26 women without and 21 with a TMD history; only the TMD-history group with a true interference had a significant rise in signs (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract).
- The author's 2025 lecture draws the same conclusion: response depends on individual parafunction and adaptability (VERIFIED, PMC12573241).
- Both trials are cited in the review (refs 30, 31; VERIFIED, Crossref). How the review frames them is UNVERIFIED.

### Q6 (R). Occlusal risk features

In the multifactorial analysis of Pullinger 1993, which feature was NOT associated with increased TMD risk?

A. Anterior open bite
B. Unilateral maxillary lingual crossbite
C. Overjet greater than 6-7 mm
D. Deep overbite
E. RCP-ICP slide greater than 2 mm

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:**
- Deep overbite, minimal overjet, slides of 2 mm or less, midline discrepancy and up to 4 missing teeth did not contribute (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract).
- The five risk features were A, B, C, E and ≥5-6 missing posterior teeth. Even these explained little of the variation in TMD.
- Anterior open bite in osteoarthrosis was judged a result of the disease, not its cause.
- The review cites this study (ref 60; VERIFIED, Crossref). What it concludes from it is UNVERIFIED.
- Okeson's list gives overjet as >4 mm (hub bank Q355, UNVERIFIED). Answer with the number the question uses.

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q7 (N). Does orthodontic treatment cause, prevent or cure TMD?

Answer the question using the evidence that bears on Michelotti 2010.

**Model answer:**

**Why the question arises**
- TMD is the leading non-dental cause of orofacial pain. Its aetiology is poorly understood and multifactorial, and occlusion is the factor most often blamed (abstract).
- Treatment lasts about 2 years in the age band when TMD signs rise, so patients blame the orthodontist and legal cases follow (abstract).
- Example: Brimm v Malloy, 1987, $850,000 (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture). The 2010 review cites the report of this award (Pollack 1988, ref 10; VERIFIED, Crossref).

**Cause.** No.
- Macfarlane 2009 started with 1,018 children aged 11-12 (337 seen at 20 years): no association with new or persistent TMD. The only predictors at 30-31 years were female sex (OR 3.0) and TMD in adolescence (OR 4.5) (VERIFIED).
- Egermark 2003, at 20 years: no difference between treated and untreated people (VERIFIED).
- Kim 2002 meta-analysis of 31 studies: no rise in prevalence, though the studies were too heterogeneous to pool (VERIFIED).
- Henrikson 2000: treatment did not raise the risk or worsen pre-existing signs (VERIFIED).

**Prevention.** No evidence.
- Macfarlane 2009 states it directly: orthodontics neither causes nor prevents TMD (VERIFIED).
- Fernández-González 2015 (9 studies) found no evidence that treatment improves or prevents TMD (VERIFIED).

**Cure.** Not supported.
- Henrikson 2000 found fewer muscular signs in treated Class II girls at 2 years; the authors call it a functional benefit over 2 years (VERIFIED). A short-term change in one sample is not evidence of a cure (interpretation).
- Improvement during treatment fits placebo and non-specific effects (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).
- Cochrane 2024 found insufficient evidence for occlusal interventions (57 RCTs) (VERIFIED, via 2025 lecture).

**Conclusion.** The review's own verdict is UNVERIFIED (full text not read). The author's 2025 lecture states that no orthodontic approach has been proven to cause, cure or prevent TMD (VERIFIED).

**Marking points (5):**
1. Multifactorial aetiology.
2. The timing coincidence.
3. At least two cohorts with numbers.
4. Cure not supported, with the placebo or non-specific explanation.
5. A clear three-part verdict.

### Q8 (A). Screening, consent and management around orthodontic treatment

Describe what you do before, during and after orthodontic treatment to deal with TMD.

**Model answer:**

**Before treatment**
- Screen with 3Q/TMD: pain in the temple, face, jaw or jaw joint once a week or more; pain on opening or chewing once a week or more; locking or catching once a week or more (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture).
- If positive, do a DC/TMD exam: opening, palpation of muscles and joints, joint sounds. Use MRI (disc) or CT (degenerative joint disease) only to confirm a diagnosis when needed (VERIFIED, 2018 interview and 2025 Table 1).
- Record every finding, including clicks, deviation and pain, for medico-legal reasons (VERIFIED, 2018 interview). The 2010 review cites risk-management papers on informed consent and record keeping (Machen 1990, 1991; refs 95-96; VERIFIED, Crossref).

**Consent**
- TMD is common and comes and goes.
- Its cause is multifactorial, so no causal link to treatment can be established (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

**If painful TMD is present**
- Treat it first: education, self-care, jaw exercises, habit control, and a provisional splint if needed (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture).
- Do not start orthodontics while facial pain persists (VERIFIED attribution, Fernández-González 2015).
- Refer if there is no response (VERIFIED, 2025 flowchart).

**During treatment**
- Monitor at visits, because TMD fluctuates (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture).
- If signs appear: diagnose, pause active forces, give conservative care, then resume or modify (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

**After treatment**
- Assess and treat TMD as you would in someone never treated orthodontically, and rule out other causes of facial pain (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

**Marking points (6):**
1. Screening tool.
2. Exam and selective imaging.
3. Documentation.
4. Consent wording.
5. Pain before braces.
6. The pause-and-manage protocol.

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q9 (A). "Will braces fix my jaw, or make it worse?"

**Case.** A 23-year-old woman with a Class II division 1 malocclusion, 8 mm overjet and moderate crowding wants fixed appliances for aesthetics.
- History: a right TMJ click for 3 years. For the past 2 months, jaw pain on chewing about twice a week. She clenches during the day at work.
- Examination:
  - reciprocal click in the right TMJ;
  - familiar pain on palpation of the right masseter;
  - maximum assisted opening 45 mm;
  - no locking.
- She asks: "Will braces fix my jaw? Or could they make it worse?"

**Examiner question 1. What is your diagnosis?**
- DC/TMD diagnoses:
  - Myalgia of the right masseter: familiar pain on palpation, modified by function.
  - Right disc displacement with reduction: a click in the history and on examination.
- Opening of 45 mm is ≥40 mm, so there is no limitation (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture Table 1).
- Screen for other pain conditions, and use Axis II screening for stress and anxiety (UNVERIFIED that the 2010 review covers Axis II).

**Examiner question 2. Will braces fix her jaw?**
- No. The abstract says claims that orthodontics cures TMD need good evidence; the review's verdict is UNVERIFIED (full text not read). The author states in 2025 that no orthodontic approach has been proven to cure TMD (VERIFIED).
- Any improvement during treatment may be placebo or non-specific, not proof of an occlusal cause (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).
- Do not promise to recapture the disc: recapture and its long-term stability cannot be guaranteed, and the patient must be told it may fail or relapse (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

**Examiner question 3. Could they make it worse?**
- Cohorts show no causal link (Macfarlane 2009; Egermark 2003; Kim 2002; VERIFIED).
- But TMD fluctuates, and she has active pain and daytime clenching. People with a TMD history adapt less well to occlusal change (Le Bell 2002, VERIFIED). High parafunction means more discomfort with occlusal change (2025 lecture, VERIFIED).
- So her pain could flare during treatment by coincidence or overload. Plan for that, and tell her.

**Examiner question 4. Her overjet is 8 mm. Pullinger listed overjet >6-7 mm as a risk feature. Should you correct it for her joints?**
- No. It is an association, it explained little of the variation, and correcting it has not been shown to prevent or treat TMD (Pullinger 1993, VERIFIED).
- Correct the overjet for aesthetics, function or trauma risk, not as TMD therapy.

**Examiner question 5. When do you start?**
- After the pain is controlled and stable (VERIFIED attribution, Fernández-González 2015; 2025 flowchart).
- First, conservative care (VERIFIED, 2025 lecture and 2018 interview):
  - education and reassurance;
  - self-care;
  - jaw exercises;
  - habit reversal for daytime clenching (keep the teeth apart);
  - short-term analgesics if needed;
  - a provisional splint if needed.
- Note that BMJ 2023 applies to chronic pain of ≥3 months. Her 2 months is not yet chronic. For chronic TMD pain the guideline makes a conditional recommendation against reversible occlusal splints and a strong recommendation against irreversible ones (VERIFIED, abstract).

**Examiner question 6. Pain returns mid-treatment. What then?**
- Re-evaluate and make a differential diagnosis.
- Pause active forces (elastics, activations).
- Restart conservative care.
- Resume or modify when she is pain-free; refer to an orofacial pain specialist if there is no response (VERIFIED, 2018 interview and 2025 flowchart).

**Examiner question 7. What do you write in the notes and tell her?**
- Record the baseline click, myalgia, opening and screening answers.
- Explain that TMD is common and fluctuates, that orthodontics is not expected to cure or cause it, and that a pause may be needed during treatment.
- These records and this consent are what protect both of you (VERIFIED, 2018 interview).

**Examiner question 8. How strong is the evidence behind your answers?**
- Michelotti 2010 is a narrative review (no systematic search described: UNVERIFIED).
- The evidence base is mainly observational cohorts and cross-sectional studies with mixed diagnostic criteria before DC/TMD.
- It shows no detectable harm or benefit rather than proof of none.
- What makes the conclusion credible is consistency across long cohorts (Macfarlane 20 years, Egermark 20 years), a meta-analysis (Kim 2002) and a later systematic review (Fernández-González 2015) (studies VERIFIED; the judgment is interpretation).
