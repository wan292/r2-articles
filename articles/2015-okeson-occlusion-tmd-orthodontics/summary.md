## Bottom line

- Well-controlled orthodontics has not been shown to raise TMD risk, but calling it 'unrelated' is too simple [§2].
- TMD has at least five major etiologic factors (occlusion, trauma, emotional stress, deep pain input, parafunction) plus patient adaptability. Orthodontics routinely changes only occlusion [§2].
- So the orthodontist should finish in **orthopedic stability**: maximum intercuspation with the condyles in the stable joint position [Abs; Int-2018 Q2].
- This minimises one dental risk factor. It does not mean the patient will always be free of TMD [§2; Int-2018 Q2].

## What it is and what they did

- **Design:** narrative review by one author, an orofacial pain programme director (University of Kentucky) writing as a nonorthodontist [Intro; PubMed]. May 2015 AJODO supplement.
- **Sample:** none. No patients, no data, no search strategy; he says a thorough review of the studies is not his purpose [§2].
- **Structure:** 5 sections: history; role of orthodontics in TMD; functional treatment goals; future; conclusions [Intro].
- **Evidence level:** expert opinion (level 5, derived from the design).

## What it says

**Why orthodontists should care [Intro].** Orthodontics changes the whole occlusion, like a full-mouth reconstruction in natural teeth, mostly in young people who keep the result for life.

**History [§1]**

| When | What happened | Joint considered? |
|---|---|---|
| 1900 | Angle opens his school in St Louis, coins the term malocclusion, defines Classes I, II and III | No; the aim was esthetics |
| Later | Andrews' six keys become the finishing guideline; casts trimmed and set in maximum intercuspation | No reference to joint position |
| Mid-1970s to early 1980s | Roth's 5 goal groups: facial esthetics, dental esthetics, functional occlusion, periodontal health, stability; urges articulators (controversial) | Yes: some orthodontists, borrowing from prosthodontics |
| 1987 | A patient wins a lawsuit claiming her orthodontist caused her TMD, with sizable compensation | Orthodontic organisations fund research |
| Mid-1990s | Studies find orthodontics is not a significant TMD risk factor [§2] | Anxiety falls |

**Why 'unrelated' is too simple [§2]**

| Reason | Okeson's point |
|---|---|
| Who treated | Almost all long-term studies came from supervised university graduate programmes; poorly finished treatment might carry risk |
| Who was treated | Young, healthy, adaptive patients; a developing system may adapt to new occlusal and joint positions |
| Many causes | Occlusion is one of at least 5 factors; at least 4 lie outside the orthodontist's control |
| Adaptability | COMT gene variation affects pain responsiveness; people fall into 3 clusters. He describes 186 post-orthodontic women in whom the pain-sensitive cluster developed more TMD symptoms, and reads this as vulnerability in the patient rather than the treatment |

**Check on that cohort.** It matches Slade 2008 (ref 22). Its abstract describes 186 females, treated and untreated: among carriers of a COMT variant, those with a history of orthodontics had a higher TMD risk (P = 0.04). That is a gene-treatment interaction, in which treatment mattered for the vulnerable genotype (Slade 2008, PubMed abstract).

**Orthopedic stability [Abs; Int-2018 Q2, Q3, Q13]**

- **Stable joint position** (musculoskeletally stable, MS): condyles most superior and anterior in the fossae, against the posterior slopes of the articular eminences, discs interposed, as set by the muscles that load the joints. Temporalis seats the condyles up; masseter and medial pterygoid up and forward; with the lateral pterygoid added, they sit against the eminences [Int-2018 Q2, Fig 1].
- **Stable occlusal position:** maximum intercuspation. Orthopedic stability = both at once.
- **Size of the mismatch:** most people have 1-2 mm, not shown to correlate with TMD. Slides over 3-4 mm correlate more, mostly in epidemiological studies. Bigger slides demand more adaptation, which fewer patients have. In adolescents the occlusion is often finished before the condyles mature into the MS position, so large discrepancies are rare [Int-2018 Q3].
- **The occlusion he means** (his splint criteria): even, simultaneous contacts with the condyles in MS, forces along the long axes, anterior guidance in excursions [Int-2018 Q13]. Whether section 3 sets these as finishing goals: UNVERIFIED (not read).
- **Loading:** his textbook adds that instability matters mainly under joint loading (clenching, bruxism), and that an acute occlusal change can trigger protective co-contraction (UNVERIFIED).

## Why it matters for an orthodontist

- Screen before treatment: history, muscle and joint palpation, range of movement. Record and discuss the findings; they are the baseline if the braces are blamed later [Int-2018 Q4].
- Manage significant TMD first; a stabilisation appliance may help. Once symptoms resolve, find the stable joint position and plan the occlusion to it. If the appliance does not resolve them, occlusion is unlikely to be the cause [Int-2018 Q5].
- Finish with maximum intercuspation matching the seated condyles, without a large slide (Okeson's 2015 finishing wording: UNVERIFIED, not read).
- Do not promise that braces will prevent or cure TMD [Int-2018 Q2, Q5].

## The course's question answered

Orthopedic stability means the teeth are in maximum intercuspation while the condyles are in the MS position [Int-2018 Q2]. Orthodontics can create or break that match, so the abstract makes it a routine goal, and §2 calls it minimising a dental risk factor. Occlusion is one of at least five factors and a potential source of TMD only in some patients [§2]. It may matter with:
- a large slide from MS into intercuspation: over 3-4 mm (Okeson [Int-2018 Q3]); over 4 mm in McNamara 1995, which Okeson co-wrote and cites (ref 18); over 2 mm in Pullinger 1993, which the paper does not cite;
- skeletal anterior open bite, overjet over 6-7 mm, unilateral lingual crossbite, or 5 or more missing posterior teeth (McNamara 1995; Pullinger 1993 similar). Open bite, large overjet and large slides may follow joint disease rather than cause it (McNamara 1995);
- poorly finished treatment, which the university-based studies did not test [§2];
- low adaptability, such as a pain-sensitive COMT genotype [§2];
- per his textbook, joints loaded by parafunction or a sudden occlusal change (UNVERIFIED).

It matters little for ordinary adolescent treatment, extraction or any particular mechanics (McNamara 1995). Even when present, occlusion leaves most of the variation in each TMD group unexplained (Pullinger 1993).

## What the presenter may ask

1. **CR (the MS position) in Okeson's terms?** A joint position, not a tooth position: condyles superoanterior against the eminences, discs interposed.
2. **Does orthodontics cause TMD?** The mid-1990s studies found no significant risk, but in well-controlled cases and young, adaptive patients [§2].
3. **Gnathology or evidence: where does Okeson stand?** In the middle. Occlusion is not *the* cause, but a stable joint-occlusion match is still the goal.
4. **Which occlusal features carry risk?** McNamara 1995's five (or Pullinger 1993's). Some may be a result of joint disease, not its cause.
5. **A TMD patient wants braces to fix the pain.** Manage the TMD first, then decide. No promises [Int-2018 Q5].

## Limits

- Expert opinion: no systematic search, no new data, selective citation in the parts read.
- Orthopedic stability is logical, but the sections read cite no trial showing that finishing in MS prevents TMD.
- His reading of the 186-female cohort differs from its abstract (see the check above).
- Newer evidence moves away from occlusal treatment for chronic TMD pain: the 2023 BMJ guideline is conditionally against reversible occlusal splints and strongly against irreversible oral splints (Busse 2023, abstract).
