## Part 1. Multiple-choice questions

### Q1 (R). Orthopedic stability

According to Okeson, orthopedic stability in the masticatory system exists when:

A. Molars and canines are Class I and Andrews' six keys are met.
B. The teeth are in maximum intercuspation while the condyles are in their musculoskeletally stable position: most superoanterior in the fossae, against the posterior slopes of the eminences, with the discs interposed.
C. The condyles are in their most retruded position with the teeth just touching.
D. Canine guidance separates all posterior teeth in lateral excursions.
E. The condyles look centred in the glenoid fossae on CBCT.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The abstract sets orthopedic stability as a routine goal, and Okeson defines it as the stable joint and stable occlusal positions coinciding [Int-2018 Q2]. A is dental only; Okeson notes that Andrews' keys ignore the joint [§1]. D is part of a functional occlusion, not the definition. C is an older, retruded concept of CR (UNVERIFIED history).

### Q2 (R). The five etiologic factors

Okeson lists five etiologic factors that can be associated with TMD. Which option gives the correct five and the one that orthodontics routinely changes?

A. Occlusion, trauma, emotional stress, deep pain input, parafunction; orthodontics changes occlusion.
B. Occlusion, trauma, emotional stress, genetics, parafunction; orthodontics changes occlusion.
C. Occlusion, trauma, emotional stress, deep pain input, parafunction; orthodontics changes occlusion and parafunction.
D. Extractions, Class II elastics, trauma, stress, bruxism; orthodontics changes the first two.
E. Occlusion, age, sex, trauma, stress; orthodontics changes occlusion.

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** [§2] gives the five factors and says orthodontics routinely affects only occlusion, with at least 4 others outside the orthodontist's control. Adaptability (partly genetic) is an extra factor, not one of the five, so B is wrong.

### Q3 (A). Occlusal features and TMD risk

An adult is assessed for orthodontics. In Pullinger 1993, the occlusal data usually set beside Okeson's view, which finding did NOT increase the odds of TMD?

A. Anterior open bite
B. Unilateral maxillary lingual crossbite
C. Overjet of 8 mm
D. Deep overbite of 6 mm
E. RCP-ICP slide of 3 mm

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** In Pullinger 1993 (VERIFIED, PubMed abstract), anterior open bite, unilateral maxillary lingual crossbite, overjet over 6-7 mm, 5-6 or more missing posterior teeth and slides over 2 mm raised the odds; deep overbite and minimal overjet did not. This comes from outside the Okeson sections read. A 2024 promotion recall in the hub's exam bank asked which type of malocclusion carries the most risk.

### Q4 (A). A small slide at debond

At debond a 17-year-old has no TMD signs and a 1.5 mm slide from the seated condylar position into maximum intercuspation. Following Okeson's reasoning, the best course is to:

A. Treat it as orthopedic instability and equilibrate before debond.
B. Accept it: a 1-2 mm discrepancy is found in most people and has not been tied to TMD; record it and finish.
C. Treat it as a Pullinger risk factor, because it exceeds 1 mm.
D. Continue treatment until the slide is zero.
E. Order MRI of both joints before debond.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** Okeson says most patients have 1-2 mm, not significantly correlated with TMD, and that over 3-4 mm correlates more [Int-2018 Q3]. In Pullinger 1993, slides of 2 mm or less did not contribute (VERIFIED). The number is from the interview, not from the 2015 sections read.

### Q5 (N). Reading the null studies

Okeson argues that the conclusion 'orthodontic therapy is unrelated to TMD' is too simple. Which is NOT one of his reasons?

A. Almost all long-term studies were of supervised treatment in university graduate programmes.
B. The treated patients were mostly young, healthy and adaptive.
C. Occlusion is only one of several etiologic factors.
D. The long-term studies followed patients for less than 1 year after debond.
E. Pain sensitivity varies with genotype, so the patient may matter more than the treatment.

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** [§2] gives A, B, C and E (the COMT cohort of 186 post-orthodontic women). Follow-up length is not among his reasons in the sections read.

### Q6 (N). Strength of the main recommendation

Which best describes the evidence behind Okeson's recommendation that orthopedic stability be a routine orthodontic treatment goal?

A. Randomised trials show that finishing in the MS position lowers TMD incidence.
B. It is expert opinion resting on biomechanical reasoning and indirect evidence: logical and low-risk, but not shown by trials to prevent TMD.
C. It was refuted by the mid-1990s studies, which showed occlusion is unrelated to TMD.
D. It rests on the 186-patient prospective cohort cited in the paper.
E. It is a GRADE-based guideline recommendation.

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** This is a one-author narrative review with no search strategy (design; [§2]). C misreads the studies: they found orthodontics was not a significant risk factor, and Okeson warns against reading that as unrelated [§2]. D is wrong because that cohort tested COMT genotype, not stability. Flag: no trial was cited in the sections read, but sections 3-5 were not read.

## Part 2. Short-answer questions

### Q7 (A). Orthopedic stability, and when occlusion matters

Define orthopedic stability as Okeson uses it, explain why it concerns orthodontists, and list the situations in which occlusion may matter for TMD.

**Model answer**
- **Definition [Abs; Int-2018 Q2].** The stable joint position and the stable occlusal position coincide.
  - Stable joint position (MS): condyles most superior and anterior in the fossae, against the posterior slopes of the articular eminences, discs properly interposed, as seated by the elevator muscles.
  - Stable occlusal position: maximum intercuspation.
- **Why orthodontists [Intro; §2].** Orthodontics changes the whole occlusion, mostly in young patients, for life. Of the five etiologic factors it routinely changes only occlusion. Finishing in orthopedic stability keeps down the one dental risk factor it controls, and the abstract makes this a routine goal.
- **When occlusion may matter:**
  1. A large slide from the stable joint position to intercuspation: over 3-4 mm per Okeson [Int-2018 Q3]; RCP-ICP over 2 mm per Pullinger 1993 (VERIFIED abstract).
  2. Pullinger's other features: anterior open bite, unilateral maxillary lingual crossbite, overjet over 6-7 mm, 5-6 or more missing posterior teeth (VERIFIED abstract).
  3. Low adaptability: the COMT pain-sensitive cluster in 186 post-orthodontic women [§2].
  4. Poorly finished treatment, which the university-based studies did not test [§2].
  5. Loading by parafunction, or an acute occlusal change (Okeson textbook, UNVERIFIED).
- **Caveats.**
  - Most people have a 1-2 mm discrepancy without TMD [Int-2018 Q3].
  - Occlusion explains little of the variation within TMD groups, and anterior open bite in osteoarthrosis is a result, not a cause (Pullinger 1993, VERIFIED).
  - Stability does not guarantee freedom from TMD [Int-2018 Q2].

**Marking points (6):**
1. The definition, with both halves.
2. The MS position described.
3. The one-of-five-factors argument.
4. A slide threshold, with its source.
5. At least two Pullinger features.
6. The caveat that stability is not prevention.

### Q8 (N). The history, and how to read the evidence

Outline the history of occlusion and TMD in orthodontics as Okeson tells it. Explain why he says the mid-1990s evidence should not be read as 'orthodontics is unrelated to TMD', and give one weakness of his own argument.

**Model answer**
- **History [§1].**
  - 1900: Angle's school; malocclusion and Classes I to III. The focus was esthetics and a stable intercuspation.
  - Andrews' six keys: finishing guidelines with no reference to joint position. Casts were trimmed and set in maximum intercuspation.
  - Mid-1970s to early 1980s: Roth adds functional occlusion to his five goals and urges articulators, which was controversial.
  - 1987: a patient wins a lawsuit claiming her orthodontist caused TMD, and orthodontic organisations fund research. A 1988 AJODO report gives a Michigan award of $850,000 (VERIFIED, citation title only).
  - Mid-1990s: studies show orthodontics is not a significant risk factor, and anxiety falls [§2].
- **Why not 'unrelated' [§2].**
  1. Nearly all long-term studies came from supervised university programmes; poorly finished treatment may differ.
  2. The patients were young, healthy and adaptive.
  3. Occlusion is one of at least five factors, with adaptability on top.
  4. Adaptability varies with genotype (COMT; 186 post-orthodontic women).
  - He concludes that believing orthodontics is completely unrelated is naive. The useful question is how to minimise the one risk factor it controls.
- **Weaknesses of his argument.**
  - Expert opinion: one author, no systematic search, selective citation.
  - His alternative, that orthopedic stability lowers TMD risk, is also untested by trials in the sections read; the same null studies do not support it directly.
  - Newer guidance moves away from occlusal treatment for chronic TMD pain: the 2023 BMJ guideline is conditionally against reversible occlusal splints and strongly against irreversible oral splints (VERIFIED abstract).
  - The MS position is hard to locate reliably at the chair (UNVERIFIED).

**Marking points (6):**
1. Four or more history steps, in order.
2. The 1987 lawsuit and the funded research.
3. The mid-1990s finding.
4. Two or more reasons from [§2].
5. The 'naive' conclusion.
6. One valid weakness.

## Part 3. Case-based oral defence

### Q9 (A). 'Will braces fix my jaw pain?'

**Case.** A 22-year-old woman has a Class II division 1 malocclusion, an 8 mm overjet and a unilateral right maxillary lingual crossbite. On guided closure she contacts first on the right premolars, then slides about 3 mm forward and to the right into maximum intercuspation. For 2 months she has had morning pain in both masseters, worse during exams, and she clenches. The left TMJ clicks painlessly on opening and closing. Maximum opening is 42 mm. She asks whether braces will fix her jaw pain.

**Examiner question 1. What do you do before planning treatment?**
- A TMD screening history: pain, sounds, locking, headache, parafunction, stress, sleep.
- An examination: range of movement, palpation of the masticatory muscles and TMJs, joint sounds (the DC/TMD protocol taught on this course).
- Record everything as a baseline, and document that it was discussed with her. Okeson stresses this baseline in case symptoms are later blamed on treatment [Int-2018 Q4].

**Examiner question 2. What is your working diagnosis?**
- Myalgia of the masseters, with clenching and stress as likely contributors.
- Left disc displacement with reduction: a reciprocal, painless click with full opening (DC/TMD terms; the criteria were not checked this run, UNVERIFIED).
- Painless clicking is common at her age and often fluctuates [Int-2018 Q4]. On its own it needs no treatment (Okeson textbook, UNVERIFIED; matches a 2021 promotion recall).

**Examiner question 3. Does her occlusion matter?**
- She has three of Pullinger's features: overjet over 6-7 mm, a unilateral maxillary lingual crossbite and an RCP-ICP slide over 2 mm (Pullinger 1993, VERIFIED abstract). Her 3 mm slide is at the edge of Okeson's 3-4 mm range [Int-2018 Q3].
- But occlusion is one of five factors, and parafunction and emotional stress are visible here [§2]. Occlusion explains little of the variation in TMD (Pullinger 1993).
- So her occlusion is a possible contributor, not a proven cause.

**Examiner question 4. TMD first, or orthodontics first?**
- TMD first. Okeson manages significant symptoms before orthodontics. A stabilisation appliance can help, and once the symptoms settle, a stable joint position can be found to plan the occlusion around [Int-2018 Q5].
- First-line care is explanation, reassurance, self-care, jaw exercises and habit awareness. For chronic pain, the 2023 BMJ guideline strongly favours CBT, supervised jaw exercise and usual care (VERIFIED abstract).
- Her pain is 2 months old and the guideline covers pain of 3 months or more, so a splint is still a reasonable short-term option.
- A 2024 promotion recall tested the same point: do not start until the pain is controlled.

**Examiner question 5. Will braces fix her pain? What do you tell her?**
- No promise. If conservative care and a splint settle the pain, orthodontics can then aim for a stable result. If the splint does not help, occlusion is probably not the cause, and orthodontics is unlikely to change the pain [Int-2018 Q5].
- She can still choose orthodontics for esthetics and function, with realistic expectations stated and documented [Int-2018 Q5].
- Orthodontics is not presented as TMD prevention or cure [§2; Int-2018 Q2].

**Examiner question 6. What occlusal end-point do you aim for?**
- Orthopedic stability: maximum intercuspation with the condyles in the MS position [Abs; Int-2018 Q2].
- Correct the crossbite, remove the large lateral slide and reduce the overjet.
- Even, simultaneous posterior contacts, forces along the long axes and anterior guidance in excursions. These are Okeson's splint criteria [Int-2018 Q13]; whether the 2015 paper states them as finishing goals is UNVERIFIED (sections 3-5 not read).
- Before debond, check the seated-condyle position against intercuspation. A residual 1-2 mm is common and acceptable [Int-2018 Q3].

**Examiner question 7. Her pain flares during treatment. What then?**
- Re-examine her and compare with the baseline. Reduce or pause active forces and elastics, restart conservative care and consider a splint. Refer her to an orofacial pain clinician if it persists. (General practice, not confirmed as Okeson's wording: UNVERIFIED.)

**Examiner question 8. How strong is the Okeson paper as a basis for this plan?**
- It is expert opinion: one author, no systematic search, no data (level 5).
- His history and his reading of the 1990s studies are reasonable. The claim that orthopedic stability reduces TMD risk is logical but untested by trials in the sections read.
- The plan rests more on pain-management evidence (BMJ 2023) and on Pullinger's occlusal data than on this paper.
