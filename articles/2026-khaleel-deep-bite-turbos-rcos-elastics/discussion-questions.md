# Questions to ask about this paper (and how to ask them)

The rule for a good question here is that it shows you read the tables, not just the abstract, and that it connects to a decision at the chair. This paper rewards that more than most. The abstract says three equal groups, 1 mm of true incisor intrusion with the reverse-curve wire, and minimal incisor change with elastics; Tables 2 to 5 say something different on each point. Where the text and a table disagree, I give both and say which I trust. Every number below was checked against the printed tables; anything from outside the paper is labelled as literature, Proffit, or my own arithmetic.

How to ask: lead with the table number, keep the question to one breath, and phrase the methodological ones as "typo or design?" rather than as an accusation. The people in the room wrote or reviewed papers like this one.

## Tier 1 — Safe, sharp questions (ask these first)

### Q1. "Point I moved 0.99, 0.71 and 0.86 mm in the three groups in Table 4, and I could not find a between-group test on it. Is the millimetre of true intrusion a reverse-curve effect, or is it what turbos and a 0.014 wire do on their own?"
- **Why it lands:** Point I is the paper's own measure of true intrusion (0.66 × incisor length from the tip, after Greig, roughly the centre of resistance). Group III had a round 0.014 NiTi and turbos only and still registered 0.86 mm. The only incisor vertical variable with a printed between-group difference is the incisal tip (−2.46 vs −1.02 and −1.25 mm, P < .001), which is what 7.46° of tipping does to the edge; inclination (P < .001) and horizontal position (I vs II, P = .027) are also tested, but Point I is the one incisor row with no test. My read: the reverse-curve-specific component is 0.1 to 0.3 mm and was never tested, yet "approximately 1 mm of true incisor intrusion" is a conclusion bullet.
- **What you will probably hear:** Most attendings will concede that a continuous reverse-curve wire mainly tips, with a little intrusion. Some will suggest the turbo unloads the lower incisors so they settle apically in every arm. A well-read one will remember that the same unit's 2022 RCT (Al-Zoubi and Al-Nimri, reference 4) found 0.28 mm at Point I with turbos alone against 1.01 mm with the reverse curve, P = .001, so the 2026 turbos-only figure is three times their own earlier value.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Table 4 gives no sign convention. The incisal tip is −2.46 mm and Point I is +0.99 mm on the same tooth, and the text calls both intrusion. In the 2022 paper the same group printed Point I intrusion as −1.01. Which way is up here?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Did the reverse-curve wire intrude the incisors?" The abstract says 1 mm; the sharp question is whether that differs from the arms with no intrusive mechanics.

### Q2. "Elastics beat the reverse curve by 11 days, 44 versus 56, with a confidence interval of 2 to 20 days, and patients were seen every 14 days. For a patient whose lower incisors are borderline, would you trade 11 days for 3.5° of proclination instead of 7.5°?"
- **Why it lands:** You are quoting Table 2 (mean difference 11.305 d, SE 3.759, P = .010) and Table 3 (LI-CA 3.46° vs 7.46°) in one breath, and you have noticed the gap is smaller than the review interval in the Methods. It turns the headline that elastics were the most efficient into the trade-off you actually face. Bonus point if needed: the Results call Group II's incisor change not significant, but 3.46 ± 3.89° with 27 patients gives t of about 4.6 by my arithmetic, so it is not zero, just less than the reverse curve.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The decision is incisor inclination and compliance, not 11 days." Elastics for the patient whose lower incisors are already forward and who will wear them; reverse curve for the upright, well-aligned lower arch with space to spare. Some will add that 11 days is less than one appointment and inside the operator's judgment about when to call it corrected.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "T1 was at bonding, so the 7.5° includes the alignment phase. In the 2022 trial the same wire placed after alignment (without turbos) gave 5.7°. Would you expect less flaring if the reverse curve goes in as a second wire?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Which group was fastest?" It is in the abstract.

### Q3. "Turbos alone extruded the lower posteriors at least as much as the elastics did, 1.30 versus 1.12 mm at the first molar mesial cusp in Table 4, but took twice as long. So where does the elastics' speed come from? The only thing Group II did that no other group did was 0.3 to 0.4 mm of upper posterior extrusion."
- **Why it lands:** It shows you read Table 4 across the groups, not down one column. The Discussion says posterior extrusion explains both the speed and the skeletal change, but Group III had equal or more lower extrusion (L5 1.22, L6 mesial 1.30, L6 distal 1.15 mm) and needed 94 days, and Group I had the least lower extrusion yet was faster than Group III. Group II is the only arm with upper premolar or molar extrusion above 0.15 mm. My read: the endpoint is posterior contact, so the turbo height fixes the total amount of extrusion; the mechanics change the rate.
- **What you will probably hear:** "The turbo sets the amount; the elastics set the rate." Some will point out that box elastics load both arches, so the gap closes from both sides. Some will add that Group III was the oldest arm (17.35 y) and passive eruption slows with age, which is a confounder, not a mechanism.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "If the upper arch is the differentiator, is an accentuated curve in the upper wire a middle option: faster than turbos alone, with less mandibular rotation than box elastics?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Why were the elastics faster?" The paper gives its answer; the question is why that answer does not separate Group II from Group III.

### Q4. "They used a 0.016 × 0.022 NiTi reverse curve as the first lower wire and restricted crowding to 3 mm so that would be possible. Do you ever start with a rectangular reverse curve, and what does that restriction do to who this protocol is for?"
- **Why it lands:** The Methods say Group I got the reverse curve from bonding, cinched distal to the second molars, and the Discussion says the sample was restricted to well-aligned or minimally crowded lower incisors specifically so the wire could go in on day one. Table 1 crowding is 1.25, 1.58 and 1.81 mm. You are pointing out that the protocol only applies to the flat, aligned lower arch, which is the case where a reverse curve is least needed.
- **What you will probably hear:** Most will align on round NiTi first and use a reverse curve as a second or third wire, if at all. They will note that cinching a stiff curved wire is what tipped the molars distally 6° and 9° (Table 3), and that the crowded deep-bite patient, where a reverse curve is tempting, was excluded.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Would a round 0.016 reverse-curve NiTi have opened the bite as fast with less torque expression at the incisors?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What wire did they use?" The Methods list it.

### Q5. "The endpoint was overbite under 30% with Shimstock contact on the posteriors while the turbos were still bonded, and about 1.6 mm of overbite was still there at T2. When do you take the turbos off, and how much of the correction do you expect to keep once you do?"
- **Why it lands:** You have read the endpoint definition in the Methods and understood that T2 is 6 to 14 weeks into treatment, not the end of it. Overbite fell 3.92, 3.65 and 3.57 mm from 5.51, 5.33 and 5.13 mm; my subtraction leaves 1.56 to 1.68 mm at T2 with turbos in place. The paper has no retention protocol and names the lack of follow-up as a limitation.
- **What you will probably hear:** Turbos come off once posterior contact is re-established on a working rectangular wire, often after deliberate overcorrection. The literature says expect rebound: Huang's 2012 systematic review (the paper's reference 1) found overbite going from 5.3 mm to 2.6 mm at debond and back to 3.4 mm long term, and Pollard 2012 found 1.2 to 1.4 mm of relapse in low- and normal-angle patients against 0.1 mm in high-angle ones.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "The Discussion cites Rozzi 2019 for stability of extrusion, but in that study the stable subgroup was high-angle; the low-angle patients relapsed through incisor inclination. This sample is average-to-reduced face height. Does that change your retention plan?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Is the correction stable?" The paper says it followed no one up.

## Tier 2 — Mechanics and biology questions

### Q6. "Group I flared 7.5° with only 1 mm of labial movement at the edge, and in the 2025 trial from the same unit a zero-torque steel reverse curve gave −0.3°. Is the flaring from the curve acting as labial crown torque in a rectangular wire, from the arch-length demand of levelling, or both, and would a zero-torque steel reverse curve be your answer?"
- **Why it lands:** Table 3 (7.46°) and Table 4 (horizontal 1.01 mm), plus reference 20 (Shakhtour and Al-Nimri, Angle Orthod 2025, misprinted in the reference list as 2024): NiTi 0.016 × 0.022 reverse curve +6.00°, steel reverse curve with torque +4.83°, steel reverse curve with the torque removed −0.31°, and Point I about 1 mm in all three arms. The literature says a reverse curve buys arch length (Clifford 1999, photoelastic model: 1 mm of reverse curve added 1.6 mm), that round and rectangular continuous wires flared incisors equally, 6 to 7°, in AlQabandi 1999, and that continuous-arch levelling proclines about 4° per millimetre of curve levelled (Pandis 2010). A continuous wire applies its intrusive force labial to the centre of resistance, so tipping is built in.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Both." The reverse curve in a rectangular wire expresses labial crown torque even with the play of a 16 × 22 in a 22 slot, levelling needs arch length, and the cinch turns the wire into a tied-back two-couple system that limits but does not stop the flare. Some will say the paper's own protocol is acceptable only when the incisors are upright and there is space.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "The paper never reports curve of Spee depth. Without it, can we say whether 7.5° is excessive for the curve they levelled?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Do reverse-curve wires procline incisors?" Everyone knows they do; the number and the alternatives are the point.

### Q7. "A 0.016 × 0.022 NiTi reverse curve delivers forces an order of magnitude above the intrusion range, and the paper reports neither force nor root status. With the second molar tipping back 9° and its distal cusp moving up 0.64 mm, what are you looking for on the next radiograph?"
- **Why it lands:** Table 3 L7-CA 8.94°, L6-CA 5.99°; Table 4 DC L7 −0.64 mm while MC L7 is +0.71 mm, which is the signature of tip-back about the cinched end. The literature says an in vitro test of this wire type (Sifakakis 2010) gave more than 9 N at 1.5 mm of deflection against about 1 N for a Burstone intrusion arch; Proffit's optimum-force table lists intrusion at 10 to 20 g per tooth. Root resorption with reverse-curve wires has been measured elsewhere: 0.44 to 0.88 mm of lower incisor root loss at 6 months with rigid reverse-curve wires in the same department (Nasrawi 2022), and the largest root-volume loss with a NiTi reverse curve in a small RCT (Mahmoud 2025, n = 21).
- **What you will probably hear:** "Eight weeks is short, and NiTi force falls as the deflection is used up." They will say the real cost of a cinched reverse curve is second-molar tip-back that has to be uprighted later, and that resorption over two months is unlikely to matter but was not measured.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Do you cinch a reverse-curve wire, or leave it free to slide and accept the arch-length gain as anterior flaring?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Are NiTi wires gentle?"

### Q8. "The same 3/16 medium box elastics extruded the lower posteriors 0.9 to 1.2 mm but the uppers only 0.3 to 0.4 mm. Why the asymmetry, and does the turbo height cap the total anyway, so the elastics only change how fast you get there?"
- **Why it lands:** Table 4, Group II: lower 0.90 to 1.19 mm (the text says 0.6 to 1.5 mm and the abstract says up to 1.1 mm; the table is the one to quote), upper 0.28 to 0.42 mm, from a reciprocal elastic that pulls both arches equally. You are showing you understand the force system and the endpoint at the same time: correction is declared when posterior contact returns, so the sum of upper and lower extrusion plus incisor change is fixed by the disocclusion the turbo created.
- **What you will probably hear:** Lower posteriors have more eruptive potential and carry the curve of Spee; upper molars are three-rooted and sat on a 0.014 wire in an arch bonded only as far as the first molars (upper 6 to 6, lower 7 to 7). They will agree that the amount is set by the turbo and the mechanics set the rate.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "The paper gives no elastic force in grams, only 3/16-inch medium. What do you use for box elastics in an adolescent, and do you ask for full-time wear?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Do elastics extrude teeth?"

### Q9. "The turbos-only group had the largest upper incisor intrusion of the three, −0.43 mm in Table 4, and it is not mentioned in the text. With the turbo on the palatal of the centrals carrying the whole occlusal load, do you worry about the upper incisor: pulp, mobility, root?"
- **Why it lands:** Table 4 upper incisor vertical −0.09, −0.14 and −0.43 mm; the Results attribute Group III's correction to passive eruption of the lower posterior teeth and say nothing about the upper incisors (the text also gives Group II's upper incisor change as 1.5°, which is Group III's Table 3 value; Group II is −0.34°). The literature from the same unit is the right thing to cite: Al-Tamimi 2026 found upper incisor pulpal blood flow at its lowest at 48 h and near baseline by 30 days with composite turbos, and Sotal 2026 found grade-2 lower incisor mobility in 74% with turbos against 25% without, with blood flow recovered by one month. A removable bite plane in children produced 0.21 to 0.25 mm of lower incisor root-length loss at 6 months (Sangwattanarat 2025, CBCT).
- **What you will probably hear:** Turbos are safe short term, mobility is transient, and one patient in 81 lost both turbos. Someone may say −0.43 mm is within the error of a palatal-vault superimposition, which is fair, but it is still the largest upper incisor change in the paper.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Does turbo height matter? The paper says standard Mini Mold turbos on both centrals but never gives the height, so we do not know how much disocclusion each arm had to close."
- **Avoid saying:** "Are bite turbos safe?"

### Q10. "The Discussion calls the sample nongrowing, but the mean ages are 15.8, 16.0 and 17.4 years, consent was taken from participants or their guardians, and the oldest group had the smallest skeletal change. How much of the posterior eruption here is treatment and how much is adolescence?"
- **Why it lands:** Table 1 ages; Table 5 Mn-Mx +0.69° in Group III against +1.84° in Group II; the Limitations paragraph itself admits there was no untreated control and that physiologic eruption could account for part of the vertical change. The literature says turbos in 12.9-year-olds opened SN-MP by 1.9° against −0.9° in untreated Bolton-Brush controls (Elbarnashawy 2023), and a 2025 RCT of a fixed bite plane against no treatment in 9- to 16-year-olds found no vertical skeletal change relative to controls (Dunbar 2025).
- **What you will probably hear:** Over 6 to 14 weeks the absolute contribution of growth is small and the change is dentoalveolar, but they will agree a 16-year-old is not nongrowing and that eruption rate is age-dependent. A Proffit-minded attending will turn it round: in a growing short-face patient, extrusion is exactly what the textbook permits because ramus growth compensates.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "If they are growing, is extrusion-based correction more defensible here, not less, and does the word nongrowing undercut the paper's own justification?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Were these adults?" Table 1 answers that.

## Tier 3 — Methodology questions (ask when the room is friendly)

### Q11. "Allocation was by operator judgment, including patient cooperation. Elastic wear is what makes Group II work, so isn't the primary outcome confounded by design? And this unit randomised the 2022 and 2025 trials, so what stopped them here?"
- **Why it lands:** The allocation sentence is in the Methods; the abstract does not mention it. Every baseline imbalance in Table 1 runs in the direction of the result: Group III is oldest (17.35 y), most crowded (1.81 mm), most brachyfacial (Mn-Mx 22.89°) and has the smallest SNA (81.29°); Group II has the highest Mn-Mx (26.16°); Group I has the deepest bite (5.51 mm) and a mean ANB of 5.14° despite an inclusion ceiling of 5°. This is confounding by indication, and the same senior author ran RCTs on this question in 2022 (Al-Zoubi) and 2025 (Shakhtour).
- **What you will probably hear:** "A reverse curve needs aligned incisors, so they could not randomise." That does not hold, because crowding of 3 mm or less was an inclusion criterion for everyone. An honest attending will call it a Level 3 cohort study and say the 50-day gap between elastics and turbos alone survives the confounding while the 11-day gap between elastics and reverse curve may not.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Are baseline P values of .067 for age and .095 for crowding with 27 per group evidence of balance? My recomputation from the printed means and SDs gives about .05 for age and about .01 for crowding, so I am not sure the printed values are right either."
- **Avoid saying:** "Why wasn't it randomised?" The paper lists that as a limitation; the sharp version says why it matters for this outcome.

### Q12. "The endpoint was called chairside every 14 days by the unblinded operator who also allocated the groups. Given that, how much weight would you put on an 11-day difference?"
- **Why it lands:** Methods: reviewed every 14 days; blinding applied only to measurement and analysis; one operator (R.K.) treated everyone. Days to correction is therefore interval-censored at 14-day resolution. My read: Group III's SD of 7.08 d is what you get when almost everyone is declared corrected at the 84- or 98-day visit. Kevin O'Brien raised the same unblinded-endpoint point in his blog review of the 2022 RCT.
- **What you will probably hear:** Agreement that the 39- to 50-day gaps to turbos alone are robust and that the elastics-versus-reverse-curve gap is one visit inside operator judgment. Someone may defend the Shimstock criterion as objective, which is true for contact but not for the 30% overbite estimate.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Would reporting the median visit number, or an interval-censored survival analysis, have shown this more honestly than a mean of 44.42 days?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Was the operator blinded?" The paper says no.

### Q13. "The text and Table 2 disagree on Group I's SD, 20.5 versus 10.5, and the Tukey standard errors are unequal even though the groups are supposed to be equal. Does that change how far you trust the between-group nulls in Table 5?"
- **Why it lands:** Results text: 55.7 ± 20.5 d; Table 2: 55.72 ± 10.52. Table 2 standard errors are 3.759, 4.229 and 4.173; with 27, 27 and 26 per group they would be within 1% of each other. My arithmetic: the printed SE for I vs II reproduces only with an SD near 20.5, so the text is right and the table is wrong; the Group III mean back-calculates from the Tukey differences to 94.40 d (the text value), not 94.15; and the SE pattern implies Group III was analysed with roughly 18 to 20 patients, a pattern that repeats in every block of Table 5. In the LFH block the SE of 0.887 implies a pooled SD near 3%, but the printed SDs (0.82, 0.84, 0.43) give about 0.7; and the Group 1 and Group 2 rows are transposed (from the means, 1 − 2 = −0.59, 1 − 3 = +0.56, 2 − 3 = +1.15, but the table prints +0.58, 1.14 and 0.56), so the 1.14% difference that actually belongs to elastics versus turbos alone carries P = .489 and the row labelled 2 vs 3 (0.56, P = .836) is really reverse curve versus turbos alone. Either way, a reported null on a 1.15% difference with SDs under 0.85 is not safe.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Angle tables get typos", then a pause. A statistically minded attending will agree that unequal SEs mean unequal n and ask how many patients were in each arm, which the paper never states. Nobody will have checked this before you.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "The G*Power inputs they give, f = 0.25, power 0.90, three groups, return about 200 patients, not 75. Which outcome and which test was the calculation for?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Are the statistics right?" Too vague, and it sounds like an accusation. Keep it to "typo or sample size?"

### Q14. "Group III's numbers in Tables 3 and 4 match the turbos-only arm of the 2022 RCT to two decimals: L6 0.88 ± 4.54, L7 0.71 ± 3.28, the distal-cusp values, the whole upper-arch block. That arm had 21 patients, metal turbos and a 0.016 × 0.022 lower wire. Is there sample overlap we should know about?"
- **Why it lands:** You compared the two papers' tables. I checked this against the 2022 tables myself: of 26 rows I could match, 14 are identical to two decimals in both mean and SD (L5-CA, L6-CA, L7-CA, lower incisor tip vertical −1.25 ± 0.80, L5, L6 distal, L7 mesial and L7 distal vertical, UI-Mx 1.47 ± 1.22, upper incisor vertical −0.43 ± 0.63 and horizontal 0.63 ± 0.38, and three of the four upper posterior rows), five more are near-identical, within 0.01 to 0.02 (L4, L6 mesial, upper molar distal cusp, Mn-Mx 0.69, LFH 0.41), and seven differ (lower incisor angle 1.55 vs 2.82°, L4-CA, incisor horizontal, Point I 0.86 vs −0.28 mm, FH-Mn mean, overbite change, duration 94.15 ± 7.08 d vs 3.15 ± 0.93 months). Two independent samples of different size, age, turbo material and wire cannot do this, and the paper discloses no reuse.
- **What you will probably hear:** Silence, then "that is worth a letter to the editor." Someone may suggest the earlier paper's table was used as a template and not fully overwritten, which is the charitable reading and still means the printed Group III cannot be trusted as a new dataset.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "The 2025 NiTi arm also reproduces the 2022 reverse-curve arm: horizontal 0.77 ± 0.64, tip −1.94 ± 0.90, Point I −1.01 ± 0.80. If the three papers share patients, has the three-month turbos-alone duration been independently replicated at all, or is it one cohort printed twice?"
- **Avoid saying:** Anything with the word fabricated. Ask about overlap and transcription and let the numbers do the work.

## Tier 4 — Questions that connect to Proffit and the exam

### Q15. "Proffit gives three ways to level a curve of Spee: absolute intrusion, relative intrusion and extrusion. In Group I, Point I moved 1 mm of a 3.9 mm overbite change and the premolars extruded 1.05 mm; the rest is tipping. Would you call any of these three arms levelling by intrusion in his sense?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit Chapter 15 (Leveling, Fig 15.23 in the 7th edition) and Table 4 in the same sentence. Relative intrusion in Proffit's account is mostly differential elongation of the premolars, and every arm here shows 1.05 to 1.22 mm of it. He puts the incisor-intrusion-to-molar-extrusion ratio of a segmented arch at about 4:1 in non-growing adults; here it is about 1:1.
- **What you will probably hear:** "None of them." All three are extrusion or relative intrusion; the reverse curve adds tipping with a small intrusive component. True intrusion needs a bypass or segmented arch with a known light force, and Proffit's optimum-force table puts intrusion at 10 to 20 g per tooth, which a fully engaged reverse-curve NiTi cannot deliver.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Weiland 1996 got 1.5 to 1.7 mm of incisor intrusion and no posterior extrusion with segmented arches in adults, against 1.3 to 1.6 mm of molar extrusion and 1.9° of mandibular plane opening with continuous arches. Is that the exam answer for an adult with a high smile line?"
- **Avoid saying:** "What is true intrusion?"

### Q16. "Proffit lists a rectangular reverse-curve wire in the lower arch among the commonest edgewise mistakes, because the curve becomes labial crown torque. Group I did exactly that on day one and got 7.5°. Does the play of a 16 × 22 in a 22 slot make it acceptable, or is this the textbook error with a number on it?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit's leveling-by-extrusion section says the lower arch should be level before a rectangular wire goes in, or step bends should be used instead of a curve; Table 3 says 7.46° despite cinching and pre-selected aligned incisors. Putting the textbook warning next to the measured consequence is exactly what an examiner wants to hear.
- **What you will probably hear:** "It is the textbook error, and the paper's own conclusion is Proffit's caveat restated." The torque play of a 16 × 22 in a 22 slot limits expression but the curve still delivers a moment at every bracket. Some will defend the wire for a Class II division 2 retroclined incisor where proclination is wanted, which is a different indication from this sample (baseline lower incisor angles of 86 to 90° in Table 1).
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Proffit accepts an accentuated-curve rectangular wire in the upper arch, where lingual root torque on the upper incisors is often wanted anyway. Would that have been a better fourth arm?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Does Proffit like reverse-curve wires?"

### Q17. "Proffit's finishing rule is: normal incisor display and short-to-average face, correct the overbite by moving the lower teeth; excess display or long face, intrude the uppers. This sample was selected for the first case only. What would you tell an examiner about a hyperdivergent deep bite with a gummy smile, because none of these three arms applies?"
- **Why it lands:** The inclusion criteria were average or reduced lower face height, average smile line and ANB 1 to 5°; Table 5 shows every arm opened the mandibular plane (0.69 to 1.84°) and Table 4 shows the upper incisors barely moved (−0.09 to −0.43 mm), so display was preserved by design. Proffit Chapter 17 and Chapter 20 (adults) both point to upper incisor intrusion with segmented mechanics or skeletal anchorage for the long face.
- **What you will probably hear:** "Segmented intrusion arch or miniscrews on the upper incisors, no posterior extrusion, and surgery if the skeletal component is large." The literature they may quote: about 2.2 mm of upper incisor intrusion with a Connecticut arch and 2.5 mm with miniscrews, over roughly 7 months by my arithmetic from the reported rates (Senisik 2012), and a 2025 meta-analysis favouring miniscrews over intrusion arches (Rasol 2025). They will agree that the paper's caution about hyperdivergent patients is opinion, because those patients were excluded.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Proffit says the vertical force of Class II elastics is contraindicated in nongrowers who cannot tolerate downward-backward rotation, and uses cross-elastics with great caution, if at all, in adults. Group II opened Mn-Mx by 1.8° in 44 days. In an adult, would you accept that?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Can I use this in every deep bite?"

### Q18. "Proffit says a maxillary retainer with a bite plane is often needed for years after deep-bite correction, and to hold the lower incisors into the late teens or early twenties. The Group I incisors flared 7.5° in a 16-year-old. How would you retain that case?"
- **Why it lands:** Proffit Chapter 19 (retention after deep-bite correction) against Table 3 (7.46°) and Table 1 (15.82 y), with the paper offering no retention protocol and citing Rozzi 2019 for stability of extrusion when Rozzi's low-angle subgroup, the phenotype here, relapsed through incisor inclination. The literature adds Dake and Sinclair 1989: utility-arch cases kept about 1 mm of true intrusion but the flared incisors uprighted and the overbite relapsed; and Kim and Little 1999: initial overbite is the strongest predictor of post-retention overbite.
- **What you will probably hear:** Bonded lower 3-3 plus an upper removable retainer with an anterior bite plane, overcorrect Class II deep bites toward edge-to-edge, and retain boys into their early twenties. They will say the incisor flare is the least stable component and the premolar extrusion the most stable in a growing patient.
- **Follow-up if they engage:** "Does that push you toward elastics or turbos alone for a Class II division 1 with proclined incisors, and a reverse curve only for the retroclined division 2 incisor?"
- **Avoid saying:** "Do deep bites relapse?"

## Do not ask these (they signal you did not read the paper)

1. "Was this randomised?" The Methods say allocation was by operator judgment, and the Limitations paragraph names the lack of randomisation.
2. "Which technique was fastest?" The abstract gives 44.4, 55.7 and 94.2 days.
3. "Did every group get bite turbos?" Yes; the design is turbos plus reverse curve, turbos plus elastics, and turbos alone.
4. "Was there an untreated control group?" The Limitations say no; ask what that does to the vertical findings instead.
5. "How many dropped out and why?" One of 81, because both turbos debonded; it is in the opening lines of the Results.
6. "What counted as corrected?" Overbite under 30% with posterior Shimstock contact, reviewed every 14 days; it is in the Methods.

## Questions they may fire back at you

**"What was the primary outcome and how was it measured?"**
Days from bonding to an overbite under 30% of the lower incisor crown with posterior Shimstock contact, judged at 14-day reviews (no separate assessor is named, and one operator treated everyone). Table 2 gives 55.72 ± 10.52 d for reverse curve plus turbos (the text says ± 20.5), 44.42 ± 10.88 d for elastics plus turbos and 94.15 ± 7.08 d for turbos alone (text 94.4), ANOVA P < .001, every Tukey pair significant; elastics versus reverse curve is 11.3 d, 95% CI 2.32 to 20.29, P = .010. No primary outcome is formally declared and the sample-size calculation does not name one.

**"Were the groups comparable at baseline?"**
Table 1 prints no P below .05, but allocation was by judgment, so direction matters more than P. Group III was oldest (17.35 vs 15.82 y), most crowded (1.81 vs 1.25 mm) and most brachyfacial (Mn-Mx 22.89° vs 26.16° in Group II), with the smallest SNA (81.29°); Group I had the deepest bite (5.51 mm) and a mean ANB of 5.14° despite an inclusion ceiling of 5°. Every imbalance runs in the direction of the result, so I read P = .067 as a warning rather than balance.

**"What did the reverse-curve wire actually do to the lower arch?"**
Incisors proclined 7.46° to the corpus axis (Table 3); the tip moved 2.46 mm vertically and 1.01 mm labially, and Point I moved 0.99 mm (Table 4). Molars tipped distally, 5.99° at the first and 8.94° at the second, and the first premolar tipped mesially 4.44°. Premolar extrusion (1.05 to 1.06 mm) matched the other arms; first molar extrusion was less (0.35 to 0.94 mm) and the second molar's distal cusp moved apically 0.64 mm.

**"So is the 1 mm of true intrusion real?"**
Point I moved 0.99, 0.71 and 0.86 mm in Groups I, II and III (Table 4), with no between-group test printed, and the arms with a round 0.014 wire and no intrusive mechanics registered nearly the same value. The reverse-curve-specific component is at most 0.1 to 0.3 mm and untested. The same unit's 2022 RCT reported 0.28 mm with turbos alone against 1.01 mm with the reverse curve, and Table 4 has no sign convention, so I would not quote 1 mm of true intrusion from this paper.

**"Why did the second molar's distal cusp go up in Group I while the mesial cusp came down?"**
Table 4 gives MC L7 +0.71 mm and DC L7 −0.64 mm, and Table 3 gives L7-CA 8.94°. The wire was cinched distal to the second molars, so the terminal molar became the anchor end of a tied-back two-couple system and rotated about a point near mid-crown: distal tip-back with the distal cusp rising, and the same pattern in milder form at the first molar (MC +0.94, DC +0.35 mm, 5.99°). It is the tip-back moment Proffit describes for a bypass or utility arch tied back at the molar, and the 2022 RCT showed it too (L7 −7.92°, distal cusp −0.83 mm in that paper's sign convention).

**"Which patient gets box elastics, and which does not?"**
A compliant adolescent with average or reduced lower face height whose lower incisors are already forward: the elastics arm was fastest (44 d) with 3.46° of proclination against 7.46° for the reverse curve (Table 3). Not a long-face patient: Group II had the largest Mn-Mx (+1.84°), FH-Mn (+1.31°) and LFH (+1.56%) changes in Table 5 and the only upper posterior extrusion above 0.15 mm (0.28 to 0.42 mm, Table 4). Hyperdivergent patients were excluded, so the paper cannot reassure me about them either way.

**"What level of evidence is this, and how does it sit with the 2022 RCT?"**
A prospective, non-randomised, single-operator cohort with allocation by judgment: Level 3 on the 2011 CEBM scale, and on GRADE the skeletal and elastics-versus-reverse-curve claims sit at very low certainty. Al-Zoubi and Al-Nimri 2022 was an RCT of 42 patients: reverse curve alone 4.85 months (about 148 d) versus turbos alone 3.15 months (about 96 d). Read together, turbos alone take about 95 days in both papers, reverse curve alone 148, reverse curve plus turbos 56 and elastics plus turbos 44, but the 2026 turbos-only arm reproduces the 2022 turbos arm to two decimals in most rows, so I do not count it as an independent replication.

**"Would it change your practice?"**
For a Class I or mild Class II adolescent with 3 mm or less of lower crowding and an average or short face, adding either a NiTi reverse curve or box elastics to bite turbos roughly halves the time to a 30% overbite, a saving of 39 to 50 days with effect sizes above 2.5 by my arithmetic. I would choose between the two on incisor inclination and compliance, not on the 11-day gap, which sits inside the 14-day visit interval. I would not use the paper to claim true intrusion from a reverse curve, to reassure a hyperdivergent patient, or to promise stability, because none of those was tested.

## Ten-second summary to open with if asked "so what did you think of it?"

"Adding either a NiTi reverse-curve wire or box elastics to anterior bite turbos roughly halves the time to a 30% overbite, 56 and 44 days against 94, in well-aligned, short-to-average-face adolescents. Elastics spare the incisors but open the mandibular plane the most; the reverse curve flares the incisors 7.5° and tips the molars back 6 to 9°. It is non-randomised, the operator chose the groups, and the millimetre of so-called true intrusion is the same in all three arms, so I take the ranking and leave the mechanism claims."
